IEA sees potential for looser oil, LNG markets after Hormuz crisis
The IEA says oil and gas markets could face abundant supply and lower prices if Middle East disruptions ease, but recovery timing and scale remain uncertain.
Global oil and natural gas markets could face a period of more abundant supply and lower prices once disruptions in the Middle East ease, although the timing and extent of any recovery remain uncertain, according to the International Energy Agency's (IEA) World Energy Outlook 2026.
The outlook points to sharply different demand trajectories depending on how governments respond to the 2026 energy crisis. Under IEA's Stated Policies Scenario (STEPS), global oil demand levels off at around 103 million b/d by 2030 before declining gradually, while natural gas demand continues growing until the mid-2030s. Under the Current Policies Scenario (CPS), consumption of both fuels continues to increase.
The projections assume that constraints on Gulf oil and gas exports will ease into 2027 and that conditions will allow unimpeded transit through the Strait of Hormuz by end-2027. IEA cautioned that the scenarios are not forecasts and that considerable uncertainty remains over the duration of the crisis and the future of Middle East exports.
The disruption to energy flows through the Strait of Hormuz has resulted in cumulative losses of 2.8 billion bbl of oil and 60 billion cu m (bcm) of natural gas supply from the Gulf. At its peak, global oil supply fell by 16.1 million b/d from pre-conflict levels.
Ample inventories, increased production and exports from non-Gulf suppliers, alternative export routes, and reduced Chinese crude imports helped cushion the initial impact. IEA member countries also coordinated the release of 400 million bbl of emergency oil stocks, announced Mar. 11, 2026.
As inventories declined and supply disruptions continued, however, higher prices increasingly weighed on consumption. Diesel markets have been particularly strained, with prices exceeding $200/bbl amid disruptions to Middle Eastern product exports and reduced Russian supplies.
IEA's November 2026 Oil Market Report expects global oil demand to decline by around 2.7 million b/d in 2026, its first annual decrease since 2020. Natural gas demand is expected to fall by around 1.5%, or 65 bcm.
IEA's longer-term projections differ considerably across scenarios.
In the CPS, which considers policies and regulations already in place, global oil demand rises to around 106 million b/d in 2035 and 114 million b/d by 2050. Reduced passenger car consumption from electrification, efficiency improvements, and biofuels is more than offset by rising demand from petrochemicals, aviation, and heavy freight trucks.
In the STEPS, which also accounts for formally proposed policies and government strategies, oil demand levels off at around 103 million b/d by 2030 before declining to 102 million b/d in 2035 and 96 million b/d by 2050.
The Additional Response Case (ARC) examines further measures under consideration by governments to reduce import dependence, diversify energy supplies, and accelerate electrification. Oil demand in this case is more than 4 million b/d below the STEPS level by 2035 and falls to 85 million b/d by 2050.
The differences in demand are reflected in IEA's oil price projections. Under the CPS, crude prices reach $85/bbl in 2035 and $106/bbl in 2050, compared with $75/bbl and $70/bbl, respectively, in the STEPS. The ARC puts prices at $72/bbl in 2035 and $62/bbl in 2050.
Natural gas markets face a similar divergence, with a major expansion in LNG export capacity expected over the next decade.
Around 350 bcm of new LNG supply is set to enter global markets between 2025 and 2035. The US leads the expansion, and IEA estimates that LNG supply capacity could increase by more than 50% if all planned projects proceed.
In the CPS, stronger gas consumption absorbs the new LNG supply, requiring prices in major importing markets to rise again in the 2030s to support additional investment.
Under the STEPS, slower demand growth leaves an LNG supply overhang that persists for longer. Gas demand levels off at around 4,800 bcm from the 2030s onward, and excess supply pushes import prices toward the short-run marginal cost of supply.
The imbalance could be more pronounced in the ARC. Persistent uncertainty over Middle East exports leads price-sensitive Asian importers to rely more heavily on domestic energy resources and invest less in LNG import terminals, pipelines, and other gas infrastructure. If Middle East supplies subsequently recover and exporters proceed with their expansion plans, IEA sees the potential for a large and lasting LNG surplus.
By 2050, global natural gas demand reaches approximately 5,800 bcm in the CPS, compared with around 4,800 bcm in the STEPS and 4,100 bcm in the ARC.
Despite the possibility of weaker prices, IEA expects continued upstream investment to be necessary to replace declining production from existing fields.
Oil and gas fields naturally decline at around 8-9% annually, with generally higher rates outside the Middle East. Nearly 90% of the $550 billion invested annually in upstream oil and gas over the past 5 years has gone toward offsetting production declines rather than meeting demand growth.
Global upstream investment is estimated at around $550 billion in 2026, about 1% above 2025, while the IEA Upstream Capital Cost Index is expected to increase by around 8%.
Additional production could come from countries including the United Arab Emirates (UAE) and Venezuela. The UAE, which left OPEC in May 2026, plans to expand crude production capacity to 5 million b/d by 2027. Its production reaches 5.8 million b/d in 2035 under the STEPS.
Venezuelan production is projected to rise to 1.5 million b/d by 2035 in the STEPS, supported by around $70 billion in cumulative investment to refurbish existing facilities and develop new fields.
IEA estimates that even in the ARC, where oil and gas demand declines, around 9 million b/d of new oil projects and 500 bcm of new natural gas projects will be required by 2035 to offset declining output from existing fields.

Hormuz Oil Exports Could Decline amid Inbound Tanker Shortage
The increased flows of oil from the Strait of Hormuz, mostly thanks to ship-to-ship (STS) transfers in the Gulf of Oman, are tying up supertankers for weeks waiting for the transfers outside the Strait
The market and most analysts assume that the steady flow of crude oil from the Strait of Hormuz and the entire Middle Eastern region will continue into the next months.
Analysts are touting the remarkable recovery in crude oil exports from the Persian Gulf, which have jumped close to pre-war levels in recent months.
The oil market reflects the bearish sentiment, with Brent Crude prices sliding in the past weeks to around $92 per barrel, down from a recent peak of over $118 per barrel in late October.
The market and most analysts assume that the steady flow of crude oil from the Strait of Hormuz and the entire Middle Eastern region will continue into the next months, rebalancing supply and demand and even tipping the balance into a large surplus next year.
However, in the volatile situation in and around the Strait of Hormuz, with no recent rapprochement for potential U.S.-Iran talks, nothing is given, and no forecast has more than 50/50 odds of panning out.
The Iran war has reshuffled trade routes and patterns and tied up more tankers in inefficient and lengthy voyages.
The increased flows of oil from the Strait of Hormuz, mostly thanks to ship-to-ship (STS) transfers in the Gulf of Oman, are tying up supertankers for weeks waiting for the transfers outside the Strait, limiting the availability of very large crude carriers (VLCCs) for the other global key oil trade routes, such as the U.S. Gulf Coast to Asia.
The inefficient shuttle shipping is tying up a growing number of smaller vessels, Suezmaxes and Aframaxes, too, on the Hormuz trade.
This leaves fewer available empty tankers globally willing to travel to the Middle East to pick up cargoes in the Gulf of Oman.
The total capacity of the empty tankers going to the Middle East has slumped by some 40% since the end of August, per data from Tankertrackers.com cited by Bloomberg .
There are “not enough vacant tankers to take oil out of the region,” Tankertrackers.com’s co-founder, Samir Madani, told the publication.
“It’s one thing to bring the oil out into the Gulf of Oman via the Strait of Hormuz, but it seems like there isn’t enough to take the oil out of the Gulf of Oman,” the analyst said.
As of November 16, the combined capacity of ballasting supertankers and Suezmaxes en route to the Gulf of Oman has slumped to 71 million barrels, down from 130 million barrels capacity at the end of August, according to data compiled by Tankertrackers.com.
To be sure, flows from the Middle East remain elevated, albeit somewhat lower so far in November compared to October. Crude oil exports from the Strait of Hormuz have held so far despite the worst month for attacks on tankers in the area since the war began.
The high crude oil export levels despite increased security risks give the market and many analysts confidence that the only path forward is continuous recovery and, ultimately, an oil glut within months.
Tanker rates have dropped from the record highs seen in early October, but they remain above historical norms and pre-war levels amid heightened war insurance premiums and security risks.
“Security risks continue to weigh on tanker operations in the Gulf,” Maria Bertzeletou, Senior Market Analyst at Signal Group, wrote in an analysis this week.
“Repeated attacks continue to restrict the pool of owners willing to accept Gulf employment, while elevated war-risk insurance costs and operational delays add to voyage expenses and limit effective vessel availability.”
So far this month, Iran has been reporting nearly daily incidents of tankers hitting naval mines in corridors that Iran has designated as “unauthorized” in the Strait of Hormuz.
Non-Iranian observers and organizations also report more frequent attacks on tankers in and near the Strait of Hormuz.
Five tankers have been struck near Hormuz in the week between November 11 and 18, according to remote-sensing intelligence by Windward .
“This highlights Iran’s continued strategy of maritime attrition, and it is unlikely to ease unless diplomatic conditions change,” the maritime intelligence firm said on Wednesday.
Despite the threat to tanker shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, investment banks and the International Energy Agency (IEA) expect the crude oil market to be in a surplus as early as this quarter.
“We continue to expect the oil balance to loosen as supply recovers, limiting the scope for a sustained rebound in prices,” ING’s commodities strategists Ewa Manthey and Warren Patterson said in a Wednesday note.
In its November monthly report , the IEA said that the global oil market will return to a surplus this quarter, with a more substantial build in 2027.
“As always, downside risks to the forecast persist, as attacks on shipping undermine maritime security, which is pivotal to a sustained recovery,” the agency noted.
Goldman Sachs Research’s latest forecast calls for oil prices easing gradually in 2027 as suppliers in the Middle East adapt to disruption from the war in Iran.
Goldman’s commodities analysts expect Brent Crude to average $88 per barrel in 2027 under their base case assumption of no definitive diplomatic breakthrough in Iran and no re-escalation with major long-term damage to oil infrastructure.
Under the same base case scenario, Goldman Sachs forecasts that Brent will fall from around $92 per barrel today to $78 by early December 2027 “as a sizable surplus decreases Brent’s fair value from $90 per barrel today to the high $70s by the end of 2027.”
