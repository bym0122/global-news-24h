Africa’s LNG Exports Set to Soar 175% as Buyers Flee Hormuz
Africa is emerging as a major alternative to Middle Eastern energy supplies, with upstream investment projected at $40 billion annually through 2030.
Sub-Saharan Africa's LNG exports could surge 175% by 2034, driven by major projects in Mozambique, Nigeria, Senegal and Mauritania.
Energy giants including TotalEnergies, ExxonMobil, Eni and Shell are expanding across Africa, as new discoveries and LNG infrastructure attract investment and strengthen global supply diversification.
Energy buyers burned by the Strait of Hormuz are turning to Africa, and the money is following. The African Energy Chamber projects that Africa’s upstream sector will attract ~$40 billion a year through 2030, lifting the continent’s hydrocarbon output to 12.5 MMboe/d. Mozambique, Nigeria, Angola, Libya and Ivory Coast will take the largest shares of that capital, while new projects in Uganda, Kenya and Namibia expand the production base. Major offshore discoveries and a wave of upstream deals are turning Africa into the world’s next natural gas and LNG frontier. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has emerged as a premier frontier for this global diversification strategy, offering large reserves of uncommitted gas entirely outside the Middle Eastern chokepoints. Backed by expanding infrastructure and floating LNG (FLNG) deployment, Fitch has forecast that SSA’s LNG exports will surge by nearly 175% to 98 billion cubic meters (bcm) by 2034, up from 35.7 bcm in 2024.
Meanwhile, the continent’s growing appetite for natural gas is expected to play a big role in driving infrastructure development, with Africa’s natural gas demand projected to grow by 60% by 2050. According to the Energy Institute’s Statistical Review of World Energy cited via The National , natural gas accounts for only 17% of primary energy use in Sub-Saharan Africa, considerably lower than the global average of 25%, thus leaving room for expansion. A paradigm shift is currently taking place in the continent wherein African nations are increasingly moving beyond simple raw resource extraction, with governments aiming to monetize gas in their domestic markets.
After enduring years of delays due to regional insecurity, Mozambique is currently witnessing a resurgence in its energy sector. Mozambique is now home to several world-scale projects in the Rovuma Basin off the coast of Cabo Delgado. First is TotalEnergies’ (NYSE:TTE) $20 billion Mozambique LNG project . Construction at the giant 13.1 MTPA facility resumed in January following the lifting of a four-and-a-half-year force majeure, with the project around 45% complete. Second is Eni’s (NYSE:E) Coral North FLNG project, estimated to cost $7.2 billion. Building on the operational success of Coral South, Eni and its partners launched an offshore drilling campaign for Coral North. Commercial operations are targeted for 2028, bumping Mozambique’s operating floating LNG capacity to 7 MTPA. Finally, ExxonMobil (NYSE:XOM) recently doubled its working interest in Mozambique’s $30 billion Rovuma LNG megaproject to 50%, following a major asset swap agreement with Italy’s Eni. ExxonMobil remains the operator of the onshore liquefaction development as the consortium advances toward a Final Investment Decision (FID) targeted for late 2026.

Major natural gas and LNG infrastructure expansion is also happening in Nigeria, with its Decade of Gas initiative serving as the strategic framework to transition the nation into a gas-powered economy. To reverse years of stagnant upstream investment, the federal government has implemented competitive fiscal frameworks, particularly targeting Non-Associated Gas (NAG) developments in onshore and shallow offshore assets, to scale domestic supply. Nigeria LNG’s Train 7 expansion is set to lift the company’s capacity to 30 MTPA from 2027. Last year, the Nigerian National Petroleum Company Limited (NNPC Ltd) and its upstream partners signed 20-year Gas Supply Agreements (GSAs), with options for extension, to supply 1.29 billion standard cubic feet per day (bscf/d) of feedgas to Nigeria Liquefied Natural Gas Limited (NLNG).
Finally, Africa’s frontier basins are undergoing a massive deepwater boom, shifting rapidly from exploratory zones into operational hubs that are reshaping global energy markets. The Greater Tortue Ahmeyim (GTA) project, shared by Senegal and Mauritania, has achieved commercial operations after shipping its first LNG cargo in April 2025. Phase one features a capacity of 2.5 million tons per annum (mtpa), with phase two aiming to scale up to 5 mtpa. The Senegalese government is looking to capitalize on this momentum, and is currently courting international investors with 109 exploration blocks.
In Namibia’s Orange Basin, TotalEnergies recently executed a major asset swap with Portugal’s Galp Energia (OTCPK:GLPEF), taking over operatorship of the massive Mopane discovery while consolidating its footprint alongside the Venus field. For the Venus project in PEL 56, TotalEnergies is targeting a development based on a floating production, storage, and offloading (FPSO) vessel, with a possible FID in 2026 that hinges on fiscal negotiations with the government. Appraisal drilling for Mopane is set to begin with a three-well campaign as early as the second half of 2026, targeting an FID in 2028.
Finally, Tanzania has largely completed negotiations on the commercial structure, taxation and revenue sharing for its massive $42 billion LNG project in Lindi, backed by a consortium including Equinor (NYSE:EQNR), Shell (NYSE:SHEL) and ExxonMobil, though a Host Government Agreement and parliamentary approval remain outstanding. A natural gas discovery in the Mnazi Bay area, Tanzania’s first in a decade, unveiled an estimated 223 billion cubic feet of gas. More than 80% of Mnazi Bay’s gas already goes to power generation at state utility TANESCO’s plants.

Qatar Becomes Largest Buyer of U.S. LNG Amid Hormuz Crisis
Qatar is set to become the largest buyer of U.S. LNG, signing long-term deals for 7 million tonnes annually with Cheniere and Woodside.
The Iran war has crippled Qatar's LNG industry, destroying 17% of export capacity, causing $20 billion in annual revenue losses and severely restricting shipments through Hormuz.
QatarEnergy is diversifying away from its reliance on the Strait of Hormuz, turning to U.S. LNG supplies to protect its global market position and improve supply reliability.
In an extraordinary turn of events, Qatar, the world’s second-largest LNG exporter before the Iran war, will become the biggest buyer of liquefied natural gas from the United States, the producer that overtook the Gulf nation as top LNG exporter earlier this decade.
This extraordinary turn of events is, of course, the result of the extraordinary disruption that the Iran war and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz inflicted on Qatar, the LNG producer whose expansion plans for the rest of this decade were rivaled only by the U.S.
The war and the Strait of Hormuz crisis, which is still blocking much of Qatar’s LNG exports, have upended these plans. State-energy firm QatarEnergy is now scrambling to repair the damage at its LNG facilities caused by Iranian missile attacks in March. These attacks took out 17% of Qatar’s export capacity and caused an estimated loss of $20 billion in annual revenue, QatarEnergy said in March.
Seven months later, Qatar is barely moving “very few” cargoes through the Strait of Hormuz as the protracted crisis chokes supply to markets of the LNG volumes that Qatar would have produced, even at reduced capacity.
QatarEnergy has been forced to declare force majeure for up to five years on some long-term LNG contracts. Suddenly, Qatar faces tens of billions of dollars in losses from missed sales. More importantly, the perception that Qatar is a reliable supplier of regular term deliveries has been undermined. Related: IEA Warns Global Gas Crunch Could Last Well into 2027
The Qatari state-owned energy giant last week extended the force majeure on LNG deliveries to Asia and Europe by another month through the end of December 2026, as LNG cargo traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains largely blocked.
Unlike oil, LNG exporters cannot transport volumes via pipelines, Saad Sherida Al-Kaabi, President and CEO of QatarEnergy and Qatar’s Minister of State for Energy Affairs, said in September.
“The problem is that LNG, our main export commodity, cannot be transported by pipelines. We will have to transport it by pipelines as gas and then liquify it at receiving terminals wherever that may be,” Al-Kaabi said at the Qatar Economic Forum Special Edition in New York in September.
“This means we are building redundant facilities to the ones we are already building in Qatar as part of the North Field expansion project. "It wasn&#x27;t just about one employee leaving. The company wanted to send a message to those who stayed that moving to a competitor wouldn&#x27;t be easy," the former employee said.

Once viewed as unlikely to catch up with established players, Chinese manufacturers are now seen as "meaningful competitors," she added.

By Qi Qin, China Analyst; Danny Hartono, Data Scientist; Lauri Myllyvirta, Lead Analyst; with contributions from Luke Wickenden, Energy Analyst
The disruption to oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz severely tested China’s energy security. Crude oil imports fell sharply, while oil prices and shipping risks increased. Yet, the import reduction did not produce the economic and social disruption in China one might normally expect from a shock of this scale, according to a CREA analysis. In fact, growth in non-fossil generation protected the country from economic costs more than any other in the world.
Temporary adjustments cushioned the oil-import shock. Reduced crude oil stockpiling accounted for 47% of the year-on-year drop in imports between March and August 2026.
Clean power and transport electrification provided a structural buffer. Electric vehicles (EVs) displaced 38 million tonnes of oil equivalent in March-August 2026, equivalent to around 13% of China’s crude oil imports in the same period of 2025.
Growth in non-fossil generation reduced coal and gas requirements, saving an estimated USD 9.1 billion in fossil fuel imports since 2020, more than any other country.
Lower oil and gas imports were not accompanied by a broad-based acceleration in coal use. Coal supply contracted by 3.4% and steam coal consumption fell sharply in Q3. Contrary to some expectations, growth from coal chemicals slowed after the crisis began.
Further clean-power growth and electrification can meet rising demand while reducing exposure to fuel-supply risks. By 2030, non-fossil power could reach 54% of the generation, or 58% if it also offsets an annualised coal-supply shortfall like that seen in March-August 2026, both exceeding China’s 50% target.
Additionally, deploying an additional 40 million EVs could save 45 million tonnes of oil annually, while cleaner petrochemical feedstocks could further reduce dependence on oil.
China’s immediate adjustment combined changes in inventories, refinery operations, and trade. Changes in implied crude stock balance accounted for around 47% of the year-on-year decline in net crude imports during the first six months of the crisis (between March and August 2026). At the same time, refinery throughput fell by about 9.6%. Lower exports offset roughly 29% of the decline in domestic production of petrol, diesel, and jet fuel. These measures redistributed the shock; they did not necessarily remove the underlying demand for oil.
Accelerated transport electrification provided a structural buffer. In the first six months after the Hormuz crisis, China’s EV fleet displaced an estimated 38 million tonnes of oil equivalent, 52% more than the same period of 2025. This was equivalent to around 13% of China’s crude oil imports in March-August 2025. Clean power provided a complementary buffer. Growth in non-fossil generation since 2020 avoided an estimated USD 9.1 billion in fossil fuel import costs over the same period — the largest savings of any country — primarily by reducing coal and gas requirements. As transport electrification expands, continued clean-power growth can meet more of the additional electricity demand without increasing reliance on fossil fuels.

China’s seaborne coal and gas imports for power generation mostly go to coastal demand centres such as the Yangtze River Delta, Guangdong, Fujian, Hebei, and Shandong. Around 34% of China’s clean power growth from 2020 to2025 happened in those fuel-importing provinces themselves. Including clean power delivered from the north-west via long-distance transmission, the share rises to 38%. The increase in clean power supply to fuel-importing provinces reached 730 TWh — 1.5 times China’s LNG imports if all that gas had been used for power generation.

Coal demand growth slows despite the oil and gas shock
China’s existing coal-based energy and industrial infrastructure offers some protection against disruptions to imported oil and natural gas. But this is different from turning to coal in response to the crisis.
China’s coal supply contracted in the six months after the crisis began. Between March and August 2026, domestic coal production plus coal imports fell by 3.4% year-on-year. On the consumption side, steam-coal consumption growth slowed from 4% year-on-year in the first quarter of 2026 to 2.1% in the second, before falling by 2% in July.
In the power sector, gas power generation fell by 4% year-on-year, around 7 TWh, in the first six months after the Hormuz crisis. Over the same period, hydro, nuclear, wind and solar generation rose by 139 TWh, far more than the gas shortfall, while coal power generation remained broadly flat. Additionally, according to the China Electricity Council , large-scale coal power plants used 0.4% less coal and generated 1.6% less electricity in the first nine months of 2026.

Coal consumption in the chemical sector increased 11.6% between March and July 2026, although growth slowed from 18.9% in the first quarter to 8.5% in the second. Coal use in the sector was still rising, but it did not clearly accelerate after the oil shock. Company disclosures suggest that higher chemical output partly reflected earlier developments rather than a new response to the crisis. Chinese coal mining giant China Coal Energy’s first-half polyolefin output rose 14.5%, but the comparison was affected by scheduled maintenance a year earlier. Baofeng, China's largest coal-to-chemicals producer, reported that feedstock disruptions and rising costs reduced petrochemical operating rates, while coal-based plants maintained high operating rates–a contrast that does not show increased coal-based output to offset the shortfall. Some producers nevertheless adjusted operations. Shenhua, China's largest state-owned coal mining enterprise and the world's largest coal producer, attributed higher chemical sales partly to changes in market demand and prices, as well as unusually low sales in 2025.
Some factories may also have used coal instead of natural gas to produce steam and heat for manufacturing. Coal consumption for the heating sector rose 4.6% in the second quarter. Meanwhile, the National Energy Administration reported that industrial gas consumption declined in the first half as some price-sensitive demand was substituted or priced out of the market.
China’s long-term goal is to meet growing energy needs with less exposure to imported fuels. Clean electricity, transport electrification, and cleaner alternatives to fossil-based chemical feedstocks offer complementary ways to achieve this.
China would need around 2,600 TWh of additional annual clean electricity supply by 2030, relative to 2025, to meet the IEA’s projected demand growth without increasing fossil-fuelled electricity supply. This would raise the non-fossil share of electricity generation to approximately 54%, up from 42% in 2025 and above the 50% target set out in China’s 15th Five-Year energy plan .
China’s net coal imports rose year-on-year in the six months following the Hormuz disruption. Higher imports helped compensate for domestic output losses, including those caused by mine closures and tighter safety inspections following the Shanxi mining disaster in May. Between March and August 2026, domestic coal output fell by around 98 million tonnes, while net imports increased by 9 million tonnes, leaving combined supply 89 million tonnes lower than a year earlier. If the same average daily supply shortfall persisted throughout an entire year and were absorbed entirely by the power sector, approximately 420 TWh of clean electricity would be needed annually to replace the associated coal power. This raises the requirement to around 3,030 TWh of additional annual clean electricity supply in 2030 relative to 2025, bringing the non-fossil share of electricity generation to 58%.

Further transport electrification could help keep oil consumption lower as economic activity recovers. Before the crisis, the IEA expected China’s total oil demand in 2030 to remain broadly at its 2025 level, as declining transport fuel demand was offset by growth in petrochemical feedstocks and other sectors. Faster electrification could bring consumption below this pre-crisis trajectory as the economy recovers. To match the March-September 2026 decline of about 0.9 million barrels of oil per day, around 40 million additional EVs are needed to deliver roughly 45 million tonnes of annual oil savings. This would mean an extra 8 million EVs per year, equivalent to nearly two-thirds of the 12.6 million EVs added in 2025. This would provide lasting fuel savings beyond those already included in the baseline.
China is also exploring cleaner alternatives to petrochemical feedstocks. According to the IEA , China’s petrochemical feedstocks accounted for almost all of China’s oil demand growth in 2025, while transport fuel consumption remained largely flat. Reducing reliance on petroleum-based feedstocks through plastics recycling and cleaner production routes can help sustain the decline in oil demand from transport electrification. China’s recent carbon-peaking action plan calls for replacing oil used in chemical production and exploring green hydrogen, ammonia, and methanol. Further cost reductions and stronger incentives will be needed to scale up these alternatives.

One question now is whether the Democrats will be able to use their overwhelming majorities in the House and Senate to push back on those policies.
Trump will probably resist the effort, claiming his powers, under Article 2 of the Constitution, as the armed forces’ commander in chief. He also threatened to veto bills that he considers “ insane ,” a term he applies to anything that challenges his prerogatives and priorities.
But a few caveats are worth noting. First, Democrats are projected to win at least 250 of the 435 seats in the House—just 40 seats short of the two-thirds needed to override a presidential veto. And given the widespread unpopularity of Trump’s war, tariffs, and other unilateral aggressions, it may not be so difficult to rally 40 Republicans to join an override campaign.
A huge consequence of the Democrats’ victory—especially its scale and margin, even in states that had previously been seen as Republican sure things—is the evaporation of Trump’s biggest reservoir of power: the fear that kept the entire GOP in his thrall. It is worth noting that the Vietnam War was ended in 1973 by a congressional amendment that cut off funding for any combat activities in the region. It’s also worth noting that the Senate at first narrowly defeated the measure but then passed it overwhelmingly a year later, after the 1972 elections—which widened the Democrats’ majority by two seats—and the deepening revulsion toward President Richard Nixon amid the Watergate scandal.
The language of the bill, sponsored in the Senate by Republican Clifford Case and Democrat Frank Church, was very specific:
None of the funds herein appropriated under this Act may be expended to support directly or indirectly combat activities in or over Cambodia, Laos, North Vietnam and South Vietnam or off the shores of Cambodia, Laos, North Vietnam and South Vietnam by United States forces, and after August 15, 1973, no other funds heretofore appropriate under any other act may be expended for such purpose.
Nearly identical language could be drafted this January, with a good chance of success, substituting the names of Iran and various other Middle Eastern countries.
It’s also quite possible that the boundless supply of U.S. weapons to Israel—a nearly automatic reflex for decades—will soon be sharply restricted, if not cut off entirely.
