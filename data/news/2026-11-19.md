IEA sees potential for looser oil, LNG markets after Hormuz crisis
The IEA says oil and gas markets could face abundant supply and lower prices if Middle East disruptions ease, but recovery timing and scale remain uncertain.
Global oil and natural gas markets could face a period of more abundant supply and lower prices once disruptions in the Middle East ease, although the timing and extent of any recovery remain uncertain, according to the International Energy Agency's (IEA) World Energy Outlook 2026.
The outlook points to sharply different demand trajectories depending on how governments respond to the 2026 energy crisis. Under IEA's Stated Policies Scenario (STEPS), global oil demand levels off at around 103 million b/d by 2030 before declining gradually, while natural gas demand continues growing until the mid-2030s. Under the Current Policies Scenario (CPS), consumption of both fuels continues to increase.
The projections assume that constraints on Gulf oil and gas exports will ease into 2027 and that conditions will allow unimpeded transit through the Strait of Hormuz by end-2027. IEA cautioned that the scenarios are not forecasts and that considerable uncertainty remains over the duration of the crisis and the future of Middle East exports.
The disruption to energy flows through the Strait of Hormuz has resulted in cumulative losses of 2.8 billion bbl of oil and 60 billion cu m (bcm) of natural gas supply from the Gulf. At its peak, global oil supply fell by 16.1 million b/d from pre-conflict levels.
Ample inventories, increased production and exports from non-Gulf suppliers, alternative export routes, and reduced Chinese crude imports helped cushion the initial impact. IEA member countries also coordinated the release of 400 million bbl of emergency oil stocks, announced Mar. 11, 2026.
As inventories declined and supply disruptions continued, however, higher prices increasingly weighed on consumption. Diesel markets have been particularly strained, with prices exceeding $200/bbl amid disruptions to Middle Eastern product exports and reduced Russian supplies.
IEA's November 2026 Oil Market Report expects global oil demand to decline by around 2.7 million b/d in 2026, its first annual decrease since 2020. Natural gas demand is expected to fall by around 1.5%, or 65 bcm.
IEA's longer-term projections differ considerably across scenarios.
In the CPS, which considers policies and regulations already in place, global oil demand rises to around 106 million b/d in 2035 and 114 million b/d by 2050. Reduced passenger car consumption from electrification, efficiency improvements, and biofuels is more than offset by rising demand from petrochemicals, aviation, and heavy freight trucks.
In the STEPS, which also accounts for formally proposed policies and government strategies, oil demand levels off at around 103 million b/d by 2030 before declining to 102 million b/d in 2035 and 96 million b/d by 2050.
The Additional Response Case (ARC) examines further measures under consideration by governments to reduce import dependence, diversify energy supplies, and accelerate electrification. Oil demand in this case is more than 4 million b/d below the STEPS level by 2035 and falls to 85 million b/d by 2050.
The differences in demand are reflected in IEA's oil price projections. Under the CPS, crude prices reach $85/bbl in 2035 and $106/bbl in 2050, compared with $75/bbl and $70/bbl, respectively, in the STEPS. The ARC puts prices at $72/bbl in 2035 and $62/bbl in 2050.
Natural gas markets face a similar divergence, with a major expansion in LNG export capacity expected over the next decade.
Around 350 bcm of new LNG supply is set to enter global markets between 2025 and 2035. The US leads the expansion, and IEA estimates that LNG supply capacity could increase by more than 50% if all planned projects proceed.
In the CPS, stronger gas consumption absorbs the new LNG supply, requiring prices in major importing markets to rise again in the 2030s to support additional investment.
Under the STEPS, slower demand growth leaves an LNG supply overhang that persists for longer. Gas demand levels off at around 4,800 bcm from the 2030s onward, and excess supply pushes import prices toward the short-run marginal cost of supply.
The imbalance could be more pronounced in the ARC. Persistent uncertainty over Middle East exports leads price-sensitive Asian importers to rely more heavily on domestic energy resources and invest less in LNG import terminals, pipelines, and other gas infrastructure. If Middle East supplies subsequently recover and exporters proceed with their expansion plans, IEA sees the potential for a large and lasting LNG surplus.
By 2050, global natural gas demand reaches approximately 5,800 bcm in the CPS, compared with around 4,800 bcm in the STEPS and 4,100 bcm in the ARC.
Despite the possibility of weaker prices, IEA expects continued upstream investment to be necessary to replace declining production from existing fields.
Oil and gas fields naturally decline at around 8-9% annually, with generally higher rates outside the Middle East. Nearly 90% of the $550 billion invested annually in upstream oil and gas over the past 5 years has gone toward offsetting production declines rather than meeting demand growth.
Global upstream investment is estimated at around $550 billion in 2026, about 1% above 2025, while the IEA Upstream Capital Cost Index is expected to increase by around 8%.
Additional production could come from countries including the United Arab Emirates (UAE) and Venezuela. The UAE, which left OPEC in May 2026, plans to expand crude production capacity to 5 million b/d by 2027. Its production reaches 5.8 million b/d in 2035 under the STEPS.
Venezuelan production is projected to rise to 1.5 million b/d by 2035 in the STEPS, supported by around $70 billion in cumulative investment to refurbish existing facilities and develop new fields.
IEA estimates that even in the ARC, where oil and gas demand declines, around 9 million b/d of new oil projects and 500 bcm of new natural gas projects will be required by 2035 to offset declining output from existing fields.

New page in history? Xi, Trump and Putin summit was more of a footnote
Far from the grand ambitions declared in Yalta in 1945, Shenzhen felt like a meeting in search of an agenda
It is arguably true, given it was the first such meeting of the leaders of China, Russia and the US, that Thursday’s gathering in Shenzhen deserved the description of “historic”.
But in terms of tangible outcomes, it is unlikely to warrant the “new page in the history of diplomacy” that Vladimir Putin claimed had been written, and will instead be consigned to a smallish footnote in any future volume of the 21st century.
It’s true that the meeting could yet be an icebreaker and the start of a permanent new dialogue. It’s also true that each leader can return home with something from the 90 minutes they spent together.
Despite falling asleep during the main Apec summit, Donald Trump had a chance to show his proximity to two authoritarian leaders he admires. With Ukraine having destroyed Russia’s bridge into Crimea , the US president greeted Putin as not just a friend but almost a wartime ally.

As such, the summit was a free gift to Putin, and underlined that Trump was no longer the leader of the free world, as the former British foreign secretary Jeremy Hunt commented.
From Putin’s perspective, a meeting in Berlin of the coalition of the willing was a sideshow. For Xi, it was a prestigious chance to play host and implicitly demonstrate that the era of American hegemony is over. No longer the sole arbiter in world affairs, the US, the consumer economy, must share the stage with China , the manufacturing giant, and Russia, the energy bank.

For grandeur at a time of various global crises, these ambitions hardly matched Yalta in 1945 or even the declaration after the Tehran summit in 1943, when the leaders of the UK, US and Russia sat on the veranda of the British embassy and pledged that their countries would work together after the war to build a “world family of democratic nations” free of tyranny and oppression. Winston Churchill declaimed in Tehran: “In our hands we have the future of mankind.” Joseph Stalin went further. “History has spoiled us,” he said. “She has given us very great power and very great opportunities.”
Shenzhen, by contrast, felt like a meeting in search of an agenda. The vacuum was filled with platitudinous commitments to “maintain strategic stability and international security”, a task that – looking around the world – none of those in attendance are currently pursuing with great effect.

It was the summit’s chief instigator, Putin, who was the most eager of the three to set the meeting in a historic context, describing China, Russia and the US as the victors of the second world war, in a deliberate slight to Britain. A pledge to “preserve and strengthen the system of international law” seemingly referred to ensuring certain world leaders were freed from the prospect of the ICC serving them with arrest warrants.
By coincidence, the other world court, the international court of justice (ICJ), found a state – Myanmar – guilty of genocide for the first time on the same day. The Shenzen statement did not name the ICJ as a target, beyond an oblique reference to the ICC and “other similar institutions”. Doubtless that is only because ICJ rulings can be dismissed with near impunity.
Xi, nervous that China’s status as protector of the global south might be damaged by being involved in a global superpower carve-up, stuck to a familiar script: “History tells us that when major powers are at peace, the world is secure. When they clash, there is chaos.”
It may be that behind the scenes, or at other events, serious progress is being made on how China might act as a guarantor of a peace deal with Iran, or on who should lead the UN through its coming phase of reform, or even on how Putin might bring himself to seek peace in Ukraine in a way that protects Ukrainian sovereignty.
For the moment, though, it looks as if what little history was made in Shenzhen was made for exclusively the wrong reasons.

House Speaker Mike Johnson, R-Benton, left, and House Majority Leader Rep. Steve Scalise, R-Jefferson, listen as President Donald Trump speaks with reporters, Sunday, Nov. 1, 2026, at Dubuque Regional Airport in Dubuque, Iowa, en route to a campaign rally.

U.S. House Speaker Mike Johnson, a Shreveport Republican, has signaled his support for tightening controls on artificial intelligence chip exports after the CEO of a leading chip producer resigned amid allegations of romantic relationships with employees and a Chinese spy .
The U.S. House has stalled for months on taking action on a set of bipartisan measures that aim to curb China’s access to chips. But Johnson’s public support, which comes after Democrats won control of Congress in the Nov. 3 midterm elections, signals a bigger shift in Washington in favor of AI regulations. Many lawmakers on both sides of the aisle say high-end AI chips could empower China to make gains on the U.S. in the race for superior AI technology.
“The export controls on chips to China has been a big issue around here. I’m in favor of that — of limiting those exports,” Johnson told Capitol Hill reporters in a recent recorded interview . “Especially in light of recent developments, I think it makes sense.”
His comments came the same day that Jensen Huang, the CEO of the tech company Nvidia, announced his resignation. As Huang’s company produced advanced AI chips, he also reportedly had an affair with a tech executive whom U.S. intelligence deemed a Chinese spy, according to The New York Times . He resigned after revelations of another romantic relationship with an internal staffer.
As speaker, Johnson plays a key role in setting legislative agendas, as does House Majority Leader Steve Scalise, another Louisiana Republican. Both have been in lockstep with President Donald Trump, a close ally of Huang’s.
“The ground has really shifted quite a bit towards (chip export restrictions), and I think Mike Johnson’s public position now is a reflection of that,” said Riki Parikh, policy director of the Alliance for Secure AI, a Washington-based nonpartisan organization that lobbies for AI regulations.
Trump, who is in regular contact with Huang and whose investment portfolio includes Nvidia stock , has supported Huang throughout the fallout from his reported affairs and even called for his reinstatement as CEO.
Meanwhile, Scalise appears skeptical of restricting AI chip exports. In a statement, a spokesperson for the majority leader cautioned that too many regulations could harm the U.S. economy.
The statement said Scalise was “working with the White House on legislative text to ensure export control legislation is done correctly without unduly tying the administration’s hands on foreign policy or unintentionally taking billions out of the American economy.”
The Nvidia scandal has heightened concerns that third-country companies could route AI chips to China, sparking outcry from lawmakers over guardrails on AI chip export restrictions. The plan, covering 2026–2030, marks a policy shift from rapid capacity expansion toward market-driven development, placing greater emphasis on project economics, utilization and grid services.
China had 136 GW of operational new-type storage at the end of 2025, rising to 167 GW/435 GWh by September 2026. The 2030 target implies approximately 900 GWh of energy capacity at the planned average duration, although the document does not specify a separate national GWh target.
The plan envisages more than 240 GW of storage serving provincial electricity systems by 2030, including approximately 110 GW of additions during the five-year period. Independent grid-side storage capable of supporting peak demand is targeted at around 140 GW, within the broader provincial total.
A further 50 GW to 60 GW is planned for major renewable energy bases, particularly large wind and solar developments in China's desert regions.
The more significant policy change concerns revenue mechanisms. The plan reinforces a capacity pricing mechanism for independent grid-side storage introduced in January 2026 and calls for reliable capacity compensation once electricity spot markets operate continuously.
It also promotes broader participation in electricity trading and ancillary services markets, including frequency regulation, reserves and ramping services, allowing storage operators to diversify revenues beyond peak-to-off-peak electricity price arbitrage.
Market-based dispatch is another priority. In regions with continuously operating spot markets, storage should generally be dispatched according to market-clearing results, while grid operators retain emergency dispatch powers.
The authorities also outlined differentiated development pathways for storage technologies. Lithium-ion batteries remain the dominant technology, while sodium-ion storage is targeted for wider deployment. Grid-forming storage receives particular attention, especially in weak-grid regions and areas with high renewable penetration, where it can provide system stability services beyond electricity shifting.
The plan also expands potential applications to virtual power plants, zero-carbon industrial parks, direct renewable electricity supply arrangements and data centers, including facilities serving China's eight national computing hubs.
