climate summit , COP31 , fossil fuels , global electrification , OPEC
(Oil & Gas 360) &#8211; The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries defended the continued role of fossil fuels at the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, arguing that expanding global electricity consumption will require petroleum products, conventional energy resources and substantial infrastructure investment. The position highlights growing divisions over how countries should balance emissions reductions with energy security and economic development.
OPEC Secretary General Haitham Al Ghais told delegates Wednesday that electrification, while an important component of future energy systems, cannot independently address the world's environmental and energy challenges. He emphasized that expanding electricity networks requires petroleum-derived materials, diesel-powered construction equipment and industrial processes that continue to depend on conventional fuels.
His comments come as Turkey promotes a voluntary initiative to increase electricity's share of global final energy consumption to 35% by 2035, compared with approximately 20% today. The proposal is intended to accelerate electrification across transportation, buildings and industrial activity.
However, the initiative does not require additional electricity generation to come exclusively from carbon-free sources, drawing criticism from environmental organizations and governments seeking stronger commitments to renewable energy and emissions reductions.
Turkish officials reported Wednesday that 75 of the nearly 200 participating countries had endorsed the voluntary pledge. Negotiations remain divided over whether the final agreement should establish stronger commitments to reduce fossil fuel consumption.
Al Ghais also identified potential risks associated with rapid electrification, including cybersecurity vulnerabilities in increasingly interconnected electricity systems and supply constraints involving critical minerals needed for batteries, transmission equipment and other technologies.
OPEC's position reflects its longstanding preference for policies focused on reducing emissions rather than restricting specific energy sources. During COP28 in Dubai, the producer group opposed proposals targeting fossil fuel consumption, while the final agreement called for transitioning away from fossil fuels without establishing a mandatory phaseout.
The debate has important implications for energy-producing and developing economies. Countries seeking to expand electricity access and industrial production face different financial, technological and infrastructure constraints, making uniform international targets difficult to implement.
For natural gas producers, increased electrification could create additional demand for dispatchable power generation, particularly in markets where renewable development is expanding but storage and transmission capacity remain limited. Oil demand could also persist in construction, petrochemicals, aviation and other applications that are more difficult to electrify.
At the same time, renewable energy, nuclear power and grid modernization remain central to efforts to reduce electricity-sector emissions. The climate impact of electrification will depend significantly on the generation sources used to meet additional demand.
For institutional investors and family offices, the disagreement reinforces the importance of evaluating energy investments against actual infrastructure requirements, national regulations and long-term demand rather than relying exclusively on international policy commitments.
With COP31 scheduled to conclude November 20, negotiations will test whether participating governments can agree on language that supports electrification while addressing differences over fossil fuel use and climate financing.
The outcome could influence long-term investment expectations across oil and gas production, electricity generation, transmission infrastructure and emerging energy technologies, although national policies and market economics will ultimately determine how capital is deployed.

Oil is forecast to ease gradually in 2027 as suppliers in the Middle East adapt to disruption from the war in Iran, according to Goldman Sachs Research.
Our commodities analysts expect benchmark Brent crude to average $88 per barrel in 2027 under their base case assumption of no definitive diplomatic breakthrough in Iran and no re-escalation with major long-term damage to oil infrastructure.
Under the same base case scenario, they project that Brent will fall from $93 per barrel today (as of November 17) to $78 by early December 2027 as a sizable surplus decreases Brent’s fair value from $90 per barrel today to the high $70s by the end of 2027. Goldman Sachs Research expects the risk premium in oil prices—which now appears relatively low—to gradually normalize.
Oil prices have risen less sharply this year than expected given the length of shipping disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz, writes Daan Struyven, co-head of global commodities research and head of oil research, in a report by his team.
In part, that was because of “remarkable oil market adaptation” to the war in Iran, Struyven adds. This included Gulf exporters redirecting supply through pipelines, dark exports, US Navy escorts, and ship-to-ship transfers.
The adaptation also included lower oil demand in response to higher prices—notably in China, which switched some of its energy demand to power and coal—and strong output of oil outside the Gulf, especially in Brazil.
“For 2027, we again expect a contest between high supply disruption risk and continued market adaptation,” Struyven writes.
