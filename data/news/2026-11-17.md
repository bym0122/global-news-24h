What the F Fund? Bond losses, rate hikes and the G Fund temptation
This episode of Fed Thread puts into plain-speak what’s happening with bonds, interest rates and the TSP’s G and F funds.
The G Fund has one very appealing quality right now in that it doesn’t lose principal. Meanwhile, the F Fund has been taking a beating, and anyone watching their TSP balance shrink might be wondering why they’re still holding it.
It’s an understandable temptation. But what if moving to the safer-looking option means giving up an opportunity that could be just around the corner?
That’s the dilemma at the center of this episode of Fed Thread , where certified financial planners Evan Radin and Neil Cain of Capital Financial Planners put into plain-speak what’s happening with bonds, interest rates and the TSP’s G and F funds.
Before you make the G Fund leap, they suggest there’s more to consider than which fund has performed better lately. Here are three questions worth asking yourself before doing anything:
The G and F Funds may both fall into the conservative investment category, but they work very differently.
The G Fund protects your principal and pays interest that resets monthly. The F Fund, on the other hand, tracks the broader U.S. investment-grade bond market, which means its value can fluctuate as interest rates change.
The catch is that when interest rates rise, existing bond prices generally fall. Then when rates move in the opposite direction, those same bonds can become more valuable.
That’s why Radin and Cain caution against judging the F Fund solely by its recent performance. If interest rates begin to decline, investors who abandoned bonds after a period of losses could miss some of the recovery.
Essentially, the G Fund’s stability is appealing, but that doesn’t automatically make it the better long-term choice.
This may be the most practical question of the three. Rather than trying to predict what the market or Federal Reserve will do next, Cain suggests looking at when you’ll need to withdraw your money.
If you’re expecting to draw from your TSP over the next one to three years, he says that money may belong in the G Fund, where principal won’t fluctuate with interest rates. Money you don’t anticipate needing for several years may have a different role in your portfolio.
That also matters for retirees considering the L Income Fund. Its conservative allocation may make sense for someone who needs regular withdrawals or simply can’t tolerate much market fluctuation, but it may be more conservative than necessary for others.
In essence, Radin and Cain recommend that your investment choices reflect your spending needs, not just your comfort level with today’s headlines.
Am I being too conservative, or not conservative enough?
It’s easy to focus on the risk of losing money, but the other risk that can sneak up on investors is being so conservative that their savings don’t keep pace with inflation.
Radin says he’s seeing clients on both ends of the spectrum. Some became overly conservative after getting spooked by market volatility, while others are taking on more risk chasing recent returns.
Neither reaction necessarily makes for a sound retirement strategy, and bonds aren’t a guaranteed hedge, either. Cain points to 2022, when inflation drove both stocks and bonds lower, showing that the economic conditions behind a market downturn matter just as much as the downturn itself.
The key lesson is to not let a bad stretch in the F Fund, a tempting G Fund rate or an unsettling market headline make the decision for you. Understand what each investment is designed to do, consider when you’ll need your money and make sure your overall allocation supports the retirement you’re planning for.
Sometimes the biggest investment mistake isn’t choosing the wrong fund, it’s making the right move at the wrong time.

The CAPE ratio suggests the stock market is overvalued
Developed by economist Robert Shiller, the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio compares the S&P 500's current price with its average inflation-adjusted earnings over the last 10 years. By smoothing out fluctuations in earnings per share (EPS), the CAPE ratio provides a longer-term perspective on stock market valuations.
Historically, the CAPE ratio has averaged approximately 18. But as of this writing (Nov. 13), it stands near 41. As the chart below illustrates, this places the stock market among its most expensive periods in history.
For perspective, the CAPE ratio reached 44 in December 1999, right before the dot-com bubble burst. Generally, prolonged readings between 30 and 40 have indicated elevated valuations and, consequently, increased vulnerability to economic shocks.
There is an important caveat to point out. The CAPE ratio does not predict when a stock market crash will occur. Stocks can remain expensive for a long time and valuations can normalize through earnings growth rather than falling share prices.
In addition, not every market crash starts with a rising CAPE ratio. The metric is more useful for evaluating long-term return expectations rather than accurately forecasting short-term market movements.
Nevertheless, today's elevated reading suggests investors should be mindful of the risks associated with paying historically high prices for stocks.
