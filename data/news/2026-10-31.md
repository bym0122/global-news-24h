### 53. ★★★☆☆ Google's AI chip in orbit answers its first prompt with a haiku - PPC Land

Google's Project Suncatcher prototype satellite has returned its first AI-generated output from orbit, a three-line haiku written by the Gemma 4 open model running on one of the company's data center class TPUs, according to a Google post dated October 30, 2026.
The result came about four weeks after the satellite, built in partnership with Planet, launched on October 1 aboard SpaceX's Transporter-18 rideshare mission. Both announcements were written by Travis Beals, Senior Director of Paradigms of Intelligence at Google. The October 30 post calls the result the project's "Hello World" milestone, says thermal and radiation testing comes next, and sets "twin interconnected satellites in 2027" as the following step. What it does not contain is any performance data.
Google put one of its own AI chips on a small satellite in October, and at the end of the month it got that chip to answer a request from space by writing a short poem. Google wants to find out whether computers that run AI could one day sit in orbit and run on constant sunlight, because building and powering data centers on the ground keeps getting harder and more expensive. Nothing changes in Google's products for now: the real tests of heat and radiation are still ahead, and the next step is two connected satellites in 2027.
The post describes a month spent getting the hardware ready. Since the launch, according to Beals, the team spent "the last few weeks completing our commissioning checklist, which included powering up the host and TPU trays and successfully running early machine learning tests." Then came the milestone: "This week, we reached our 'Hello World' milestone: the first-ever AI response from a data center class TPU in orbit."
The exchange itself was simple. During a ground-station pass, Google uplinked its first prompt to the Gemma 4 model running on board, asking it to write a haiku about being in space. "As the satellite passed by Svalbard, Norway, the downlinked poem confirmed success," the post says. The model's output read:
Starlight feeds my core. What the satellite performed was inference - running an already trained model on a new input to produce an answer - rather than training. After the first output, according to Google, the team "tested space-themed limericks and telemetry prompts reporting the technical status of the payload systems." Telemetry prompts are the more practical of the two: the model was asked to describe the condition of the hardware it was running on.
The next phase is the one that matters most for the project's premise. "Next, we will continue with a series of tests to evaluate how TPUs perform under thermal stress and radiation exposure in orbit," the post states. "Verifying our TPUs can reliably execute workloads in space paves the way for our next milestone of twin interconnected satellites in 2027."
Beals closed with a flourish. "Our chips have officially earned their space wings," he wrote, describing the result as "an important achievement on the long road ahead for this cosmic moonshot."
The claim is narrow, and deliberately so. Google describes the haiku as the first AI response "from a data center class TPU in orbit" - Google's own chip family - rather than the first AI model ever run in space. The post does not address whether other processors have run models in orbit before.
Several details are also missing. The October 30 post does not name the TPU generation on board, the number of chips, the size of the Gemma 4 variant, the satellite's altitude or power budget, or the results of the "early machine learning tests" it mentions. It gives no exact date for the first response beyond "this week," and it does not say why the team used Gemma 4, Google's open model family, rather than a Gemini model. Planet, named as the build partner on October 1, does not appear in the second post. No mission cost has been disclosed in either announcement.
The October 1 post was brief. "Our team has confirmed contact with the satellite and it is operating as expected," Beals wrote on launch day. He described the mission as "the first step in a long-term research moonshot exploring whether space could one day host scalable machine learning infrastructure."
That post set out the near-term plan: "Over the coming weeks, we'll gather in-orbit data on how our TPUs handle the physical stress of spaceflight and the radiation and thermal extremes of space." It also said the research behind the mission was now published as a peer-reviewed paper in Joule . "Some things can only be tested in space," Beals wrote. "As we begin our experiments, we'll use what we learn to refine our designs, and we're excited to share more as the mission unfolds."
Set the two posts side by side and the sequence becomes clear. On October 1, radiation and thermal data were to be gathered "over the coming weeks." On October 30, the same tests are described as what comes "next." Four weeks of commissioning have produced a working chip and a poem, but no published measurement of how the hardware is coping with orbit.
The schedule itself has moved. When Google revealed Project Suncatcher on November 4, 2025 , it described a learning mission with Planet that would launch two prototype satellites by early 2027, to test how models and TPU hardware operate in space and to validate optical links between satellites. What reached orbit on October 1, 2026 was a single prototype, months ahead of that date. The two-satellite element now appears as the 2027 milestone. A link between satellites cannot be tested with one spacecraft, so that part of the original plan still lies ahead. Neither October post explains the change in sequencing.
The economics Google laid out in November 2025 explain why the company is spending on this at all. The research, then a preprint, modelled an illustrative 81-satellite constellation operating at 650 km, inside a cluster with a radius of one kilometre , with neighbouring satellites roughly 100 to 200 metres apart. The satellites would sit in a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous low Earth orbit, where solar panels could be up to eight times more productive than installations on the ground and produce power nearly continuously.
Two technical results from that work bear directly on the tests now starting. A bench-scale demonstrator achieved 800 Gbps each way, 1.6 Tbps in total, through a single pair of optical transceivers, while the design calls for links of tens of terabits per second. And in ground radiation tests, Google exposed Trillium, its v6e Cloud TPU, to a 67 MeV proton beam. The most sensitive part was the high bandwidth memory, which showed irregularities only after a cumulative dose of 2 krad(Si), nearly three times the expected shielded five-year mission dose of 750 rad(Si). There were no hard failures up to 15 krad(Si) on a single chip.
Those were laboratory results. The prototype now in orbit is the first chance to see whether TPU hardware behaves the same way under real conditions, which is why the thermal and radiation phase carries more weight than the haiku.
The 2025 research was also frank about the obstacles. Satellites must shed heat through radiators, which limits power density. Commercial satellites typically last five to fifteen years, while data center equipment is often replaced within three to five. And existing satellite communication systems, according to the research, lack the capacity for data center-scale operations. The October 30 exchange illustrates that last point in miniature: the prompt went up and the poem came down only when the satellite passed within reach of a ground station.
Cost is the deciding variable. Google's analysis projected that, with sustained learning rates, launch prices may fall below $200 per kilogram by the mid-2030s, at which point a space data center could become roughly comparable to the reported energy costs of equivalent terrestrial facilities on a per-kilowatt-year basis. Launch prices had already fallen from about $54,500 per kilogram in 1981 to roughly $1,500 by 2021. The gap between $1,500 and $200 is the distance the whole idea still has to travel.
Spending on orbit while spending more on the ground
Google's leadership has been explicit that this is a long-term project. In an interview published on October 21 , Alphabet chief executive Sundar Pichai said the satellite was doing well and justified the bet on energy grounds, pointing to round-the-clock solar power in sun-synchronous orbits and to the growing size of data centers on Earth. He called Suncatcher a moonshot and the likely future way Google scales compute. The same interview made the near-term problem plain: Pichai said Google remained capacity constrained after Gemini 4 Argon, its newest frontier model , processed almost 25 trillion tokens in its first 72 hours.
A week later, on October 28, Pichai mentioned Suncatcher in Alphabet's third-quarter results , telling investors that Google had launched TPUs into space. The same results showed how much money is going into infrastructure that stays firmly on the ground.
Third-quarter purchases of property and equipment reached $51,355 million, up 114% from a year earlier .
Spending in the first nine months of 2026 totalled $131,953 million, against $63,596 million in the same period of 2025.
Free cash flow was negative $9,065 million, the second negative quarter in a row.
Long-term debt stood at $125.1 billion on September 30, up from $46.5 billion at the end of 2025.
Guidance has climbed all year. Alphabet set its 2026 capital expenditure range at $175 billion to $185 billion in February , moved it to $180 billion to $190 billion when it raised about $85 billion of equity in June , and raised it again to $195 billion to $205 billion in July . Meeting that range would require fourth-quarter spending of $63.0 billion to $73.0 billion, by PPC Land's calculation. At the time of the June raise, chief financial officer Anat Ashkenazi signalled a further significant increase in 2027. The October documents gave no 2027 figure.
The chips are also becoming a product in their own right. Google Cloud revenue rose 103% to $30,709 million in the third quarter, and Alphabet said TPU systems sales were a meaningful contributor to that growth, without disclosing a figure.
The case for orbit rests on the constraints that every hyperscaler now faces on land. In September 2025, Google withdrew a rezoning application for a $1 billion data center on 468 acres in Franklin Township, Indianapolis , minutes before a council vote, after residents organised against it. In the Netherlands, new data center connections can wait up to 10 years for grid capacity . Amazon has gone in another direction, backing a plan for up to 960 MW of small modular nuclear reactors on a former coal plant site in Indiana , with no construction date or cost disclosed.
Google is not alone in looking up. At Davos in January, Elon Musk said space would become the lowest-cost place to put AI within two or three years and that SpaceX plans solar-powered AI satellites . There is an irony in the arrangement: Google's own prototype reached orbit on a SpaceX rideshare.
Advertising is still the core of the business that pays for this. In the third quarter, Google's advertising revenue of $82,012 million accounted for 64.3% of Alphabet's revenue . Capital spending in the same quarter exceeded the $42,290 million of operating cash flow the whole group generated, with debt and share sales filling the gap. The money behind Alphabet's compute build-out, on the ground and in orbit, comes from a company whose profits rest on Search, YouTube and network ads.
Compute supply also shapes the products marketers work with. When capacity is tight, Google rations. Its most capable model, Argon, launched at $2 per million tokens for paid users only , and Pichai has described the constraint as one of compute rather than policy. How quickly AI features spread across Search, Gemini and advertising tools depends in part on how much capacity Google can bring online, and at what cost.
None of that changes because a TPU wrote a poem in orbit. By Google's own analysis, the economics of space-based compute depend on launch prices that may not arrive until the mid-2030s. The capacity Google is buying for 2026 and 2027 is terrestrial, measured in tens of billions of dollars a quarter. Suncatcher is a long-dated option on a different energy source - and its next test is not whether a chip can answer a prompt, but whether it can keep doing so through months of radiation and temperature swings. Will Google publish those measurements? The October posts make no commitment.

Although Wall Street now values NVIDIA ( NASDAQ:NVDA | NVDA Price Prediction ) at $4.94 trillion, the company started with three engineers and a diner menu. Per NVIDIA's own blog, in 1993 three friends, Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem, met at Denny's to talk about a chip that would bring realistic 3D graphics to personal computers. Speaking on Sequoia Capital's Crucible Moments podcast, Huang placed the booth "right at the corner of Capitol and Berryessa, this place in East San Jose." But within a few years, the startup that now anchors the technology-packed Nasdaq Composite was weeks from running out of cash.
On the same podcast, Huang said his first day of work was "February 17th, my birthday in 1993." He added, "You know, I was 29, I had just turned 30." The founders' first product, the NV1, reached retailers and then came straight back. Huang described the damage on Sequoia's podcast: "250,000 units went out. 250,000, well, 249,000 came back. Huang has since turned that close call into a management philosophy. Fortune reported on his Dec. 4, 2025 appearance on The Joe Rogan Experience , where he said: "You know the phrase '30 days from going out of business,' I've used for 33 years." NVIDIA went public in January 1999, and The Next Web reports that Sega later sold its stake for about $15m. From there, the company stacked one platform shift on another: CUDA in 2006, the 2012 AlexNet deep learning success on NVIDIA GPUs, several crypto cycles and today's AI infrastructure expansion.
