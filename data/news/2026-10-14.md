MI5 is devoting more resource to countering state threats than during the Cold War, the head of the intelligence service has said.
Sir Ken McCallum said threats from foreign adversaries had quadrupled in the last decade, with Russia "aggressor number one" as well as increased Iran-linked aggression against Jewish sites and military bases.
At an annual speech in London, MI5&#x27;s chief said Russia was waging hybrid warfare at a time when AI was "supercharging the cyber-security contest between attackers and defenders".
He also warned of rising threats from "extreme right-wing terrorism" and said MI5 had "caught terrorists seeking to exploit AI," including by integrating the technology with drones.
He said the biggest threat from the misuse of AI was extremist groups using the technology to harness new and existing bioweapons.

Sir Ken said Russia had stopped short of "conventional conflict" against Nato and is instead trying to unsettle European countries while its progress in its full-scale invasion of Ukraine is "minimal and horrifically costly".
Hybrid warfare or hybrid attacks are loose terms which tend to describe non-military methods, for example cyber-attacks, sabotage or misinformation, designed to exert pressure and destabilise an adversary.
These are quite often employed using proxies, but Russia always denies involvement in such attacks despite growing evidence to the contrary.
Sir Ken said MI5 successes in identifying and expelling Russian spies from the UK had left the Kremlin dependent on paying proxies for attempted attacks.
"With its traditional covert footprint eviscerated by almost 1,000 expulsions since the invasion, the Kremlin&#x27;s global toolkit is weak, he said.

BOSTON —While the U.S. economy is showing "resilience," especially with regard to a low unemployment rate, inflation is coming up on six years above the Fed's two percent target, necessitating higher interest rates to bring it down, Susan Collins, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston, said.
Speaking at the Boston Fed's annual conference for regional and community banks, Collins said she supported the Fed Open Market Committee's 25-basis-point rate hike last month and feels the central bank will have to raise "a bit further" by year's end to tame inflation .
Collins said that the most likely economic scenario, according to her reading of the data, "continues to feature healthy GDP growth, with the labor market remaining near full employment. In this scenario, inflation gradually returns to two percent, as continued efficiency gains support the balance between demand and supply, and contribute to disinflation."
The U.S. economy has shown resilience despite significant adverse shocks from tariffs and energy, she said.
"I see the strength in real economic activity as reflecting not just strong demand but also an increase in the economy's productive capacity due to efficiency gains," Collins said.
However, with pressure on pricing from the war with Iran, which has driven up costs for fuels like diesel and, crucially for New England as winter approaches, home heating oil, inflation is likely to continue growing, she said. Another issue is the drumbeat of spending on data centers for artificial intelligence, which has driven up construction costs.
"Against this backdrop, it will be important to monitor whether elevated inflation remains largely confined to energy and AI-buildout-related categories, while price growth continues to moderate elsewhere – or whether evidence of price pressures broadens," she said.
In a separate interview with American Banker, Collins said she looks to surveys and other data sources within and outside the Fed to inform her economic outlook, and relies particularly on meeting with individuals in the business and banking sectors in her district.
"I'm talking to folks from a wide range of contexts, age groups and sectors, from all across New England," she said. "I very frequently hear that widespread concern about the high cost of a variety of things."
The Fed's First District, which encompasses all six New England states except for the part of Connecticut bordering New York, has more mutual banks than is typical across the country. Although the banking sector has seen a pickup in mergers and acquisitions as Trump administration regulators have shown more willingness to approve combinations, the peak might have already happened. Bank earnings are stronger now than last year, and there have only been five bank mergers in the region, compared to 2025, which saw 15 mergers here, Collins said.
Collins stressed the importance of community banks continuing to provide credit and banking services to grow the local economy. She cited New Hampshire economic development projects involving the smallest businesses, as well as Connecticut banks providing credit and strategy help to childcare-facility owners.
Compared to other banks across the country, New England community banks have a higher proportion of their assets in loans, specifically residential loans for one- to four-family homes, she said. Banks in the region are seeing slower loan growth, but loans are performing well, she said, citing non-accruals to total loans staying below the long-term average. As loans with five-year rate terms have begun to reprice in today's higher rate environment, and as banks make new loans, local banks' net interest margins are coming back to their averages from after the global financial crisis, she said.
Banks now are posting strong earnings, the interest rates banks pay on interest-bearing accounts have stabilized after rising over the past two years, and core-deposit ratios are up, she said.
"New England remains a highly competitive lending environment, and overall returns for community banks in the region remain below those of national peers, continuing a long-term pattern," she said.
One major question for the local and national economy is the role of AI, not only in data-center construction as fuel for inflation, but also for increased productivity and in hiring decisions.
"Increasingly, AI is part of every conversation I have across the district," Collins said in the interview. She said regional companies are mostly getting productivity gains from process enhancements, not necessarily from replacing workers with more-efficient AI: "Consistent with a low-hire, low-fire environment, I rarely hear firms thinking about downsizing as a result of using AI, but they may be thinking more carefully about backfill and places they could improve."
Banks in particular have the chance to turn more repetitive or manual tasks over to AI, freeing employees to do more forward-thinking or creative tasks, she said.
Collins pointed out that her bank is applying the new supervision standards that Fed Gov. Michelle Bowman, the vice chair for supervision, set out in the last year.
As it deals with fighting inflation and boosting employment, the Fed is also facing attacks from President Donald Trump, who is currently battling to remove Fed Governor Lisa Cook from office . In the interview, Collins noted that she feels the Fed needs to maintain its independence from political pressure if it's going to meet Congressional mandates.
"I really value Gov. Cook's thoughtful perspectives and data-based analyses around the FOMC table," she said.

Zimbabwean writer Simbarashe Kundizeza has denied using artificial intelligence (AI) to write his short story, which won this year&#x27;s Caine Prize for African Writing.
His statement comes a week after he scooped the Â£10,000 ($13,000) award and a recent article in The Atlantic said AI-detection software Pangram had shown his work about a boy whose family falls on hard times was entirely AI generated.
"For the avoidance of doubt, I wrote The Blue Fighter," he posted on X , external .
The accusation has caused an uproar on social media and Caine Prize chair Ellah Wakatama told the BBC she was shocked that the US magazine had not waited for a full investigation before publishing.
"I do wish The Atlantic had spent more time investigating this so that alongside Pangram, they did have evidence of real human investigation of it before tainting the prize and tainting this winner," Wakatama told the BBC&#x27;s Front Row programme .
The chair of the Caine Prize&#x27;s board of trustees said she had been contacted on Friday about the allegation and Vauhini Vara&#x27;s article - Yet Another Prizewinner Seems to Be AI , external - had been published on Sunday.
It follows rows over other literary prizes this year, in particular the Commonwealth Short Story Prize, which was won by Trinidadian writer Jamir Nazir despite allegations that he had used AI, and the withdrawal of Canadian-Haitian author ThÃ©lyson OrÃ©lien from France&#x27;s top literary award, the Goncourt, over allegations he used AI for his first novel, which he denies .
Vara, who quoted Wakatama in her article, said her wariness of Silicon Valley was "well founded and reasonable" but she said Pangram&#x27;s internal research showed its assessments were rarely wrong.
The backlash to the article on social media has included comments from African writers with suggestions that such accusations are primarily being made against non-white writers.
Wale Lawal, the Nigerian founder of The Republic magazine, called out The Atlantic article for being "crazy, racist and wildly unethical" , external .
Zimbabwean writer Joe Ruzvidzo, whose novel Welcome to Anywhere came out last year, said he stood by Kundizeza and that it was time "to fight this literary stop-and-frisk" , external .
Nigerian publisher Modupe Daramola said it was necessary to discuss how "these AI allegations are targeting Black and African writers" and how they were often disproportionately scrutinised.
"There&#x27;s something incredibly sinister about this," the Noisy Streetss Limited publisher posted on X , external .
"Pangram is itself AI-powered. Why should its assessment be treated as definitive?" she added.
Wakatama said as part of an internal investigation for the Caine Prize Kundizeza would be sending in documentation about his various drafts and there were ongoing conversations with all the judges and The Shallow Tales Review, the publication that first published The Blue Fighter in 2025 , external .
In his statement, Kundizeza said had "a concrete, documented history" about the story&#x27;s inspiration and he set out the chronology of its development.
Wakatama, who is also editor-at-large at Canongate Books, said Caine Prize submissions, which are all previously published, were not checked for AI, but writers vouched that their work was original.
She added that she was not against using AI per se to check entries, but she was "against is it being used as a final arbiter in this case".

Wakatama said it was also important to remember Pangram was "not yet tested".
"I am not happy for the work of writers, and in this case especially black writers, to form the testing ground for something that is at best still rough and ready."
Research has found that AI detectors can wrongly flag human writing as AI generated. Vara conceded that any AI-detection product, "including one as good of Pangram" was "not going to be 100% accurate".
In her article, as well as citing Pangram research she also quoted a recent paper by researchers at Free University of Brussels , external , which said it had found zero cases of Pangram, and two other AI-detection tools, getting it wrong when it came to English-language writing by non-native English speakers.
The study notes that tools like Pangram should be considered "as an initial flag for further investigation".
Vara told BBC&#x27;s Front Row programme that she had also used her own judgment "to suss out these things" and that every other previous Caine Prize winning story she had put through Pangram "came out as 100% human".
"I also found an earlier 2021 story by the same author. I ran that through Pangram. It also came out as 100% human. And what struck me was that was a really good, interesting story. And tonally, it&#x27;s so different from this story, as if the same person hadn&#x27;t written both."
In response Kundizeza wrote: "To suggest that a writer cannot write in different styles or genres, or improve his writing substantially after five or more years fundamentally misunderstands the craft of writing."
For Vara, one of the stylistic issues that stood out about The Blue Fighter was that it had used sentence constructions using "X but Y", which she says is common in AI-generated texts.
"Not with anger, but with the quiet resignation of someone who had known all along this moment would come," is an example she gave from the story that described the character of the mother.
"The proliferation of those struck me," Vara told Front Row, adding that the story also had a lot of metaphors, also prevalent in writing generated by AI.
The day after winning the Caine Prize, Kundizeza told the BBC he was inspired after hearing a poem read by South African Vangile Gantsho at an event in Ghana last year about her father&#x27;s obsession with building fish tanks.
He said he had weaved that with fictionalised details from his own life around 2002 when Zimbabwe&#x27;s economic troubles began.
"Much like the father in the story, my own dad... was an accountant, but he quit his job to grow tomatoes. So it kind of touches on both of our fathers," he told BBC Newsday.
On X, Kundizeza said he was heartened by "the numerous messages of solidarity from fellow writers and readers", ending his message by saying: "Most importantly, I will continue to write."
