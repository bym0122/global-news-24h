Mike Rogers breaks with Trump in Michigan Senate race on tariffs, Iran
Michigan Republican Senate nominee Mike Rogers is distancing himself from President Donald Trump on tariffs and the Iran war as economic concerns dominate the battleground race.
Republican candidates are largely leaving Trump out of their general election ads after leaning heavily on him in the primaries, with just 4.3% mentioning him now, down from 47.1%, according to a CNBC analysis of AdImpact data.
The Michigan contest could help decide control of the Senate, making Rogers’ effort to hold Trump’s base while appealing to swing voters a key test for the GOP.

Michigan Republican Senate nominee Mike Rogers sat at a neighborhood bar, a bottle of Canadian Labatt Blue pilsner in hand, and delivered a message that would have been hard to imagine just weeks ago.
"Here in Michigan, we know that Canada is not our enemy," Rogers says into the camera in an advertisement released Oct. 7. "We need to end this tariff war now."
He never mentions the man who started it: President Donald Trump .
It&#x27;s a dramatic reversal for Rogers, who spent months defending Trump&#x27;s tariffs , saying as recently as September that " Trump is right to put America first " by imposing duties. But in Michigan, where farms, factories and tourism rely on Canadian trade, Trump&#x27;s trade fight and Ottawa&#x27;s retaliation have become a political quagmire three weeks before an election that could decide Senate control.
Rogers is locked in a dead heat with Democrat Abdul El-Sayed . The Cook Political Report with Amy Walter rates the contest a toss-up, with neither candidate emerging as a clear favorite. Polling aggregator FiftyPlusOne has El-Sayed with a four-percentage-point advantage over Rogers.
And Rogers&#x27; omission of Trump – mirrored in an earlier ad calling for an end to the war in Iran – reflects broader GOP anxiety heading into November&#x27;s election. Even as Trump urges voters to " pretend I&#x27;m on the ballot ," many battleground Republicans are barely talking about him in general election ads, with some using them to tacitly break with his signature policies.
Fifty-seven of 1,324 Republican candidate ads in the general election period mention Trump – 4.3%, down from 47.1% during the primaries, according to a CNBC analysis of AdImpact data. The analysis counts distinct ads, rather than how often they aired, and includes spots scheduled through the end of October.
Democratic candidates are invoking Trump far more often. He appears in 20.9% of their general election ads, nearly five times the Republican rate.
"Republicans needed to toe the Trump line to win the base in the primaries, but they need independence to survive November," said Ron Bonjean , a Republican strategist and partner at ROKK Solutions.
Michigan conducts roughly $80 billion in annual trade with Ontario, according to the Michigan Chamber of Commerce. Canada is also Michigan&#x27;s top export market, buying $23.2 billion in goods last year, roughly 39% of all state exports, according to the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative.

Western Europe’s highest mountain shrank by more than a metre last year. The peak of Mont Blanc in France now stands 4,807.47 metres above sea level, a team of surveyors said on Wednesday, putting “the roof of Europe” 1.29 metres lower than in 2025.
The mountain is about 4,792 metres tall but the summit constantly shifts as heat, wind and precipitation change the thickness of snow and ice on top. The height scientists recorded during an expedition last month was 1.88 metres greater than it was in 2023, when it reached the lowest levels in recent memory.
Chartered surveyors from the Union Nationale des Géomètres-Experts (UNGE) and the Ordre des Géomètres-Experts, who have regularly monitored the peak since 2001, used precise satellite technology to measure the mountain, and drones to create a detailed model of the snow and ice cap.
The summit has shrunk back by 3.43 metres since a high of 4,810.90 metres in 2007, the data shows.
The experts cautioned against linking fluctuations in height from year to year to the rapid loss of Alpine ice being caused by fossil fuel pollution.
“Regarding ice mass, it is essential to distinguish between changes in surface altitude and changes in the total volume or mass of ice,” a UNGE spokesperson said. “An increase in summit altitude does not necessarily mean that the mountain has gained ice mass, nor does it contradict the documented retreat of Alpine glaciers.”
Climate breakdown was a factor in western Europe’s hottest summer on record this year, scarring Alpine regions and melting ice at dizzying speed. Earlier this month, Swiss glaciologists warned of an “ explosion of melt rates ” after confirming that one-fifth of the country’s ice had been lost in the past five years, and 5.5% of volume was lost between 2025 and 2026.
Jean des Garets, the president of the Haute-Savoie departmental chamber of surveyors and the leader of the Mont Blanc expedition, said the warming trend could be seen with the naked eye in the rest of the Alpine massif but that “for the moment, climate change doesn’t seem to have the same impact above 4,000 metres”.
