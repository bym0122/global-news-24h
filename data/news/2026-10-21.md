Fresh data points from September showed the housing market continues to deteriorate just as the Federal Reserve is considering raising interest rates again.

The Federal Reserve&#x27;s inflation fight is running into a growing complication: The housing market is slumping even as broader price pressures persist.
Housing data deteriorated further in September , according to separate reports Tuesday, adding to an already complicated policy landscape for the central bank as it contemplates its next move on interest rates.
Things appear poised to get worse before they get better. If the Fed raises short-term rates as expected over the next several months, mortgage rates that are already at 7.43% could push even higher, putting a further damper on affordability. Higher home borrowing costs have coincided with a general rise in inflation and a housing shortage.
"The housing market is about to go into a deep sleep unless Washington can do a 180 on its policies and make the cost of a new home more affordable," wrote Chris Rupkey, chief economist at Fwdbonds. "It is not winter yet but housing is going into a deep freeze."
The National Association of Realtors reported that pending sales, a forward-looking index that measures home sales under agreement but not completed, hit an all-time low in records that stretch back to 2001. The index slid 7.3% for the month and was off 11.3% from a year ago. The Wall Street estimate was for a decline of just 0.4%.
Housing starts, another important metric when evaluating the state of the market, also posted a decline well outside already-muted expectations Tuesday.

Privately owned housing starts fell at a seasonally adjusted annualized rate of 5.2% on the month, against expectations for a decline of just 0.4%, while building permits were off 0.2%, against the Dow Jones consensus outlook for a 2% gain, according to Commerce Department data.
Moreover, single-family permits totaled just 845,000, down 4% from August and the lowest number since 2023.
"Market conditions weakened in September due to fast-rising mortgage rates," said Lawrence Yun, the NAR&#x27;s chief economist. "Although job growth and income gains can support home purchases, housing affordability is more affected by changes in mortgage rates."

Outside of the pending sales number, home builder sentiment is at lows not seen in 14 years.
Under some conditions, Fed officials might respond to the housing weakness with easier monetary policy. Lower rates generally help spur demand by lowering borrowing costs.

Readings last week on consumer and producer prices provided few signs of optimism, with wholesale final demand goods prices rising 2.6%, the fastest pace in a Bureau of Labor Statistics data series going back to December 2009.

"In 2027, we expect the headwind from affordability—reignited by the recent rebound in mortgage rates—to increasingly dominate, weighing further on activity," Walker wrote. "Sustained higher mortgage rates will continue to have their most pronounced impact on housing turnover, and the recent run-up in rates is likely to push existing home sales lower in the coming months."
Several data points tell the story: More than 95% of mortgage borrowers have rates below the prevailing level, and 65% are 2 percentage points below. Goldman forecasts residential investment to decline 2.5%, 4% and 1%, respectively, over the coming three quarters, with a possibility for a steeper decline if mortgage rates don&#x27;t fall.

Walker attributed the middling levels as a product of the "lock-in" effect in which it&#x27;s too expensive for current homeowners to move and take on higher borrowing costs while deteriorating affordability weighs on new construction.
"Housing activity has remained rangebound in recent years, capped on the upside by affordability headwinds and buffered on the downside by the longstanding housing shortage," Walker wrote. "In 2027, we expect the headwind from affordability to increasingly dominate, weighing further on activity."

"Given that mortgage rates have already risen much more than the Federal Reserve&#x27;s short-term interest rate hikes, mortgage rates could be topping out," he said. "Buyers and sellers may simply be holding back until mortgage rates move favorably."

Gellert Global Group is a third-generation family business that is paying 50% tariffs on cheese exported from Canada. The New Jersey-based firm is also facing retaliatory tariffs from Canada on American-made cheese it sells north of the border.
Co-chief executive Thomas Gellert said the firm had spent generations building relationships with suppliers and customers in the food industry.
"These tariffs disrupt those relationships overnight and create costs and uncertainty that ripple through the entire supply chain," he said on Tuesday.
The Liberty Justice Center is challenging the administration&#x27;s use of Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act to hit Canadian goods from wine to dairy products and hockey sticks.
That section of the nearly 100-year-old Act is aimed at protecting US business against foreign discrimination, but the legal group is arguing that it does not allow the executive branch "to impose sweeping tariffs or import restrictions whenever a trade dispute arises".
The lawsuit seeks to challenge the White House&#x27;s reliance on retaliatory tariffs imposed by Canada to justify its own duties under Section 338.
Canada has imposed a series of countermeasures against numerous US tariffs. Last month, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney matched the Section 338 US tariffs "dollar for dollar" after Trump imposed them for what he called "unequal treatment" of US cars, dairy and alcohol.
It also points out Section 338 requires a 30-day waiting period before levies can be imposed, but the tariff on products including cheese was added to the list just a week before tariff collection began.
Sara Albrecht, chief executive of the Liberty Justice Center, said the case was not about Trump himself but about the presidency.
"No president should have the power to pull a dormant statute off the shelf and use it to impose sweeping tariffs or import bans without the limits Congress required," she said.
The lawsuit argues that Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 has been superseded by Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.
It also argues that if Section 338 still applies, the White House has not established the legal basis for the sweeping tariffs against Canada.
"The case is not an objection to tariffs as such. It asks who has the power to impose them and whether the executive branch followed the law Congress wrote," the Liberty Justice Center said.
The law group has also filed a motion for a preliminary injunction to block their collection.
It is the fourth case brought by the group against Trump&#x27;s tariffs, including its successful case against the sweeping tariffs brought in under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) that were struck down in February this year.
Albrecht said the group had brought all the cases for the same reason: "the Constitution gives tariff power to Congress. The executive branch cannot keep moving the same policy from one statute to another and pretend the limits disappear".

Global LNG market faces demand cuts as Hormuz disruptions strain supply
Ongoing disruptions in LNG shipments through the Strait of Hormuz, driven by regional conflicts and attacks, are tightening global gas markets with limited alternative supplies, potentially extending into 2027 as European inventories remain low amid higher prices.

Prolonged disruptions to LNG shipments through the Strait of Hormuz are tightening global gas markets, as limited spare liquefaction capacity and constrained alternative supplies leave demand reductions critical to restoring balance and raise the risk of market tightness extending into 2027.
Prolonged disruptions to LNG shipments through the Strait of Hormuz are putting mounting pressure on global natural gas markets, with limited spare liquefaction capacity leaving demand reductions as a key mechanism for restoring market balance.
Despite elevated prices in Europe and the Asia Pacific, alternative suppliers have limited ability to quickly replace lost Gulf exports, raising the prospect of tighter markets extending into 2027.
European gas prices remain elevated as the Northern Hemisphere enters winter with unusually low inventories. Dutch TTF natural gas futures traded around &euro;88/MWh (about $29/MMbtu) on Oct. 19, while EU underground storage stood at 74.6% of capacity as of Oct. 18, compared with a 5-year seasonal average of about 89%. German storage was particularly low, at about 61%.
The Strait of Hormuz remains a major supply constraint. Since the Middle East conflict began in late February, around 80% of previously available LNG exports from Qatar and the UAE have been sidelined. Combined exports averaged 1.73 billion cu m (bcm)/month during March&ndash;September, compared with a 5-year pre-crisis average of 9.37 bcm/month. Renewed attacks on commercial shipping have complicated prospects for a sustained recovery in shipments.
An October analysis by the Oxford Institute for Energy Studies (OIES) suggests that prolonged supply disruptions would be absorbed primarily through reduced LNG imports and demand rather than increased production elsewhere.
Using an April 2027 restart of Gulf LNG exports as its modeling baseline, OIES estimates that delaying the recovery until October 2027 would create a 49-bcm supply gap in 2027 relative to that baseline. Other exporters would provide only 7.3 bcm of additional supply, leaving 41.7 bcm, or 85% of the shortfall, to be absorbed through lower imports and demand.
US LNG exports offer limited additional flexibility
US LNG export installations continue operating at high utilization rates, supported by the substantial price differential between domestic and international markets.
Henry Hub futures traded near $3/MMbtu on Oct. 20, far below European prices. US Energy Information Administration (EIA) data showed working gas inventories at 3.57 tcf for the week ended Oct. 9, following a 67-bcf injection. Stocks were 1.3% above the 5-year average, although the surplus narrowed from 2% the previous week.
Meanwhile, LNG feedgas demand has remained strong. Deliveries averaged about 18 bcfd in September, up 4.4% from August, according to American Gas Association data citing Rystad Energy.
However, strong domestic gas production and favorable export economics do not necessarily translate into substantially greater LNG shipments in the near term. Existing liquefaction capacity and the timing of new project start-ups remain key constraints.
OIES modeling suggests that US LNG exports would change little relative to its baseline across the different 2027 Hormuz scenarios, despite the potential for growth from new capacity additions. Based on OIES modeling, Australia would provide most of the incremental supply response.
With limited scope for additional LNG supply, importing regions would bear most of the adjustment burden.
Under the OIES October 2027 restart scenario, European LNG imports would decline by 20.8 billion cu m relative to the April restart baseline, accounting for about half of the global demand adjustment. China's imports would fall by 6.6 billion cu m, followed by a 5.4-billion cu m reduction in South Asia.
The impact would vary significantly across markets.
In Europe, much of the adjustment would come through reduced summer storage injections rather than immediate cuts in consumption. Already-low inventory levels raise the risk that prolonged supply disruptions could leave storage insufficiently replenished ahead of winter 2027&ndash;28, extending pressure into another heating season.
South Asian importers would face more direct demand reductions. Sustained high LNG prices could force some buyers to reduce consumption, switch to coal or oil, or face power shortages. Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan would be comparatively insulated because of their greater ability to secure higher-priced cargoes.
The timing of a sustained reopening of Hormuz remains central to the gas price outlook.
Under the OIES baseline assuming an April 2027 recovery, European TTF prices would fall to $16.05/MMbtu in first-quarter 2027 and $12.39/MMbtu in the second quarter, as the market anticipates returning Gulf supply.
A delayed recovery until October 2027 would instead push TTF to a peak of $31.35/MMbtu in second-quarter 2027. Continued restrictions through 2028 would produce a similar peak of $32.05/MMbtu. A partial recovery to 50% of pre-crisis output beginning January 2027 would produce a smaller price response, with TTF peaking at $18.42/MMbtu in first-quarter 2027.
Current forward prices remain substantially above the OIES April restart scenario, indicating that traders continue to price in significant uncertainty over the timing of supply recovery.

EU Stands Firm Against Arctic Oil and Gas Drilling Despite Energy Crisis
The European Union reaffirmed its opposition to Arctic oil and gas development on Tuesday, even as the bloc prepares for another expensive winter with depleted gas storage and prices approaching levels last seen during the 2022 energy crisis.
The European Commission's new Arctic strategy calls for keeping oil, coal, and gas reserves in the ground, including in Arctic regions, and preventing hydrocarbon developments that threaten the Arctic ecosystem.
Brussels did make one concession. The new strategy drops a 2021 pledge to pursue an international legal ban on new Arctic drilling and refuse purchases from those developments. An EU official acknowledged that convincing other countries to cooperate has become increasingly difficult.
Europe's current energy situation makes the timing particularly interesting.
EU gas storage stood at just 74% of capacity on October 14, compared with a five-year average of roughly 86%. Benchmark Dutch TTF gas prices reached &euro;86.03 per megawatt-hour last week, near their highest since December 2022.
Middle Eastern LNG flows remain approximately 80% below year-earlier levels, with disruptions through the Strait of Hormuz continuing to constrain supplies. Europe has already paid an estimated additional &euro;100 billion for oil and gas this year.
Norway, Europe's largest gas supplier, has urged Brussels to reconsider its Arctic drilling opposition. Norwegian production is expected to decline during the 2030s unless companies including Equinor make new discoveries beyond existing mature fields.
New Arctic developments would not solve Europe's immediate winter supply problems. Exploration, development, and infrastructure construction take years. But Norway's concern extends well beyond this heating season.
Europe's gas supply outlook has already deteriorated enough for EU energy commissioner Dan Jorgensen to propose delaying methane emissions requirements on imported energy to ease pressure on the market.
Brussels is apparently willing to reconsider the timing of its emissions rules, but not its opposition to new Arctic oil and gas development.
Norway's existing fields, meanwhile, will continue aging whether Brussels approves of new Arctic drilling or not.
