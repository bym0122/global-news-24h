Despite US-Israeli airstrikes and Tehran’s attacks on shipping, the waterway still has to provide a lifeline for those that live and work there
By Stefanie Glinski ; photographs by Mohammad Mohsenifar in Bandar Abbas/on Hormuz Island
Capt Farouq turns the wheel as he watches the ferry terminal of Bandar Abbas grow smaller, its bright lights flickering in the distance.
In front of him, the navigation radar beeps periodically, its green and pink lights glowing in the darkness. The ferry, today at about half capacity with roughly 100 passengers, turns and heads into the darkness of the strait of Hormuz.
He’s been a captain for the past 18 years, and the strait of Hormuz is his second home
Farouq, 39, sits comfortably in his cabin, overlooking the pitch black horizon as he sails towards Hormuz Island, a short commute of about half an hour from the Iranian mainland’s Haqqani Port, for 340,000 toman, about £1. He is relaxed and smiles as he pulls into the deeper waters. He’s been a captain for the past 18 years, and the strait of Hormuz is his second home.
Farouq says the crossings have not stopped since the start of the US-Israeli war on Iran on 28 February, although passenger numbers are down and the journey has become more dangerous. For months, Farouq has heard explosions coming from the strait, unclear as to whether that is air defence or attacks, or both. Tonight is no different.
Inside the ferry, passengers say they have gotten used to the sounds too, and, to an extent, the war itself. They drink tea and smoke cigarettes. Some nap, while others entertain their children. It’s hot and humid, the air thick, the windows fogged with condensation.
Even with a fragile ceasefire in place, here on the coast, it has barely felt as though the war has stopped.
According to Iranian government sources, attacks on the southern port of Bandar Abbas have damaged 760 fishing and commercial vessels. Farouq says his own ferry was also damaged by shrapnel while docked at the port, though it was quickly repaired.
Since the start of the war, Iranian forces have repeatedly attacked vessels attempting to cross the strait. In the first week of October alone, at least 12 attacks were reported against oil, liquefied natural gas and liquefied petroleum gas tankers, sending oil prices back above $100 a barrel .
With pressure mounting on the US president, Donald Trump, before the midterm elections, he is rumoured to be once again considering strikes.
But beyond the headlines and global markets, the strait is first and foremost a lifeline for many Iranians living in the southern province of Hormozgan.
Fishers in Bandar Abbas selling their catch on the street
Fishers, captains and harbour workers all depend on the strait for their livelihoods and the war has made their work increasingly difficult. Fishers can no longer venture as far out, ports have come under attack, and many people fled Bandar Abbas in the early months of the war for smaller, more remote villages in search of safety.
Local residents fishing for their own daily consumption
Anvar Sadat, a 38-year-old fisher who works at Bandar Abbas’s fish market, says business has slowed and that making ends meet has become increasingly difficult.
“Fishing boats can’t go out as freely as before the war, and our movement is restricted,” he says, adding that several of his colleagues in the city have been killed in attacks.
Anvar Sadat, who works at the fish market in Bandar Abbas
“This place was hell,” says Abdul Salam, a 45-year-old garbage collector. The beaches, particularly those near the port, were among the most dangerous places during the height of the war, he says, describing heavy attacks that forced him to stop working almost entirely to avoid the risk.
Life on the island, however, has been different, Farouq explains as he steers into the small, dimly lit port of Hormuz Island, his last crossing of the day before heading back to his family for the night.
Hassan, 28, cafe owner – has run a cafe on the island for eight years
Hormuz Island has long been known as a kind of “hippy paradise”, a place Iranians have come to regard as “free”. Artists and creatives moved here from bigger cities in search of peace; women swim in bathing suits, writers and painters find space to work, and life moves slower. There is “love and magic” here, as Yashar, a 35-year-old surfer, puts it, “a different rhythm”.
A group of local women swimming off the coast of Hormuz Island
Life here has changed little, Yashar says, although the war has brought some new restrictions, and a handful of attacks, mostly on infrastructure. The roughly 8,000 residents are trying to support each other, and to preserve the sense of freedom that has set the island apart, he adds.
The government has closed off parts of Hormuz Island’s north, but beyond the now restricted areas, life has continued, he explains. “As long as you don’t go north, they leave you alone,” he says of the army.
Omran, a 45-year-old tour guide – the tourism sector has been hit by the conflict
The Majara hotel, also known as Rainbow accommodation, is one of Hormuz’s attractions. It has been closed since the beginning of the war
“The tourism sector has taken the biggest hit,” says Omran, a 45-year-old tour guide, adding that he prefers to go by his first name only. “Guesthouses and homestays remain closed, as do restaurants and many cafes.” Visitors have largely stopped coming since the war, dealing a heavy blow to an island whose economy depends on tourism and fishing, both of which have been disrupted.
The Salt valley on Hormuz Island, one of the island’s tourist attractions
From the higher parts of the island, Omran says, it is possible to see deep into the strait on clear days, when the haze lifts and the humid air clears, all the way to the mountains of Khasab in Oman.
Residents have climbed up these barren mountains of red and white stone, at times watching the war unfold in the distance. Tankers remain stranded in the strait, explosions can be heard regularly. Yet residents also say the war has not driven them from the island.
Khaleh Kaniz, a 67-year-old painter who was born and raised on Hormuz, laughs when asked whether she had considered leaving in February, at the start of the war.
“Why should we leave? Where would we go? Our homes and our lives are here.”

Despite this, Tehran has held firm to its position that the war will not end, and the Strait of Hormuz will not reopen, until the US accepts its conditions.
Iran maintains an unyielding stance on its contentious nuclear programme, which was a factor in the Trump administration’s decision to launch the war on February 28.
“Not a single gram of enriched uranium will be removed from Iran,” Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, said late Tuesday after meeting Munir.
But he did not rule out downblending the highly enriched uranium inside the country under supervision from monitors, something Iran has offered before.
Iran’s authorities present the decades-old nuclear programme as a sovereign right and an achievement gained despite strikes, sabotage and assassinations. Rezaei described it as “national wealth” on Tuesday.
Iran has also used low-enriched uranium for civilian purposes like manufacturing radiopharmaceuticals and for power generation at the Bushehr plant in cooperation with Russia.
The authorities argue that surrendering the nuclear programme under military pressure would amount to capitulation, if it did not guarantee that Iran would not be left exposed to more attacks.
US President Donald Trump has repeatedly emphasised since the start of the war that he wanted to dismantle the Iranian nuclear programme. US Vice President JD Vance, however, suggested earlier this month that a “meaningful” reduction in the level of enrichment capacity may satisfy US demands.
Before Israeli and US attacks on Iran’s nuclear facilities in June 2025, the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) estimated that Tehran possessed 440 kilogrammes (970 pounds) of uranium enriched up to 60 percent. A nuclear bomb would need enrichment of more than 90 percent.
The attacks, followed by further US-Israeli strikes on nuclear sites in 2026, severely damaged Iran’s nuclear infrastructure. With the IAEA unable to inspect key facilities, the current size, condition and whereabouts of much of Iran’s enriched uranium stockpile remain unverified.
Tehran has refused access to the IAEA to inspect its nuclear facilities since the war started, accusing the agency of advancing Western interests through adopting resolutions and referring the nuclear file to the United Nations Security Council .
Iranian authorities maintain they are not pursuing a bomb. But senior Iranian officials, like Rezaei, have publicly questioned the validity of being a party to the international nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty if Iran’s nuclear facilities and other infrastructure continue to be attacked.
Still, Iranian officials have hosted numerous foreign diplomats this week. Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov was in Tehran on Monday after attending a meeting with Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries a day earlier. There were also indications of visits to Tehran by other Arab officials.
US Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth is also reported to have met Israeli security officials earlier this week. These meetings did not appear to have decreased the prospects of renewed war.

“The reality is that the country is truly in war conditions and the war continues. We have not reached peace conditions and are away from it, so all decisions must be made within war conditions,” he said.
Parliament speaker and chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf told Munir that Iran had “nothing new” to tell Washington and expected realisation of conditions offered in New York last month to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days.
Mediators did not publicly divulge details of their proposals to Tehran this week, but were told the US needed to build trust by accepting Iran’s conditions. These include returning to the June memorandum of understanding and recognising an Iran-Oman deal over temporary transit in the Strait of Hormuz .

One day earlier, Witkoff met Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy in Oslo, where Ukrainian and European leaders sought to ease a widening dispute with the United States over military aid, intelligence sharing, and access to Starshield—the military version of SpaceX’s Starlink satellite network.
Trump’s deepening rift with Zelenskyy and recent accommodation of Putin have unleashed a torrent of criticism not only in Ukraine, but also in Europe and the United States. He has been accused of betraying a fledgling democracy and victim of Russian aggression in favor of a ruthless autocrat, who is, moreover, seeking to undermine the United States across the globe.
While the outrage may be justifiable, it has done little to clarify the hard choices the West and its Ukrainian partner confront. As before, rhetoric substitutes for a hard-nosed assessment not so much of what is at stake as of what can be achieved in the short term and what long-term goals should guide current policy. Three matters need to be soberly addressed: What near-term settlement of the Russia-Ukraine war is both desirable and achievable? What type of near-term relations with Russia are beneficial to Western interests? And how should relations with Russia be structured in the long term?
Ukraine and its Western backers continue to insist on a just peace, without defining it in practical terms. But everyone knows that the settlement will not be just in the strictest terms. The victim will not be made whole again and the aggressor will not be sternly punished. Russia will retain control of most of the Ukrainian land it has seized. Even Ukrainian leaders concede that they will not be able to liberate all the land within their internationally recognized 1991 borders through military means. At best, Ukraine can withhold legal recognition of Russian control.
Similarly, Putin is not going to be hauled off to The Hague to be held accountable for his war crimes. To the contrary, peace will have to be made with him. At best, he can be made to pay a stiff price for his aggression by ensuring that Russia ends the war in a worse strategic position.
In part, that would entail preserving Ukraine’s sovereignty and independence on as broad a swath of its 1991 territory as possible with its ambitions to join Europe undimmed. The slow but steady integration of Ukraine into European security structures, even if well short of NATO membership, would be a step in the right direction. That should be the goal of Ukraine and its
backers today. Not a just peace, to be sure, but an achievable peace that holds open the promise of a better future for Ukraine.
Reaching such a peace requires continued strong Western backing for Ukraine, especially through the provision of the weapons and intelligence it needs to thwart, if not reverse, Russian advances on the battlefield and to protect itself from Russia’s fierce aerial bombardment. Sanctions do have a role to play, but the experience of the past four and a half years demonstrates that they are much less consequential than promised and probably have long since reached the point of diminishing returns.
With pressure only able to go so far, dialogue and diplomacy are critical to reaching a settlement. But engagement is also a step toward the normalization of relations with Russia. Ukrainian and European leaders have categorically rejected this, railing against the United States’ talk of normalization because, in their view, it would reward an unrepentant Russia. That argument misses the point, however, in large part because it conceives of normalization as a return to a broadly cooperative relationship, like the one that existed in the first post-Cold War decades and definitively ended with Russia’s illegal annexation of Crimea in 2014.
This is far too positive and generous an interpretation of normalization. The West enjoyed fairly normal relations with the Soviet Union during most of the Cold War, despite the implacable rivalry. Reliable channels of communication and eventually habits of consultation helped avert crises or at least prevented them from spiraling into unwanted, and likely devastating, military confrontations. Such normalization is needed today for the same reasons. It may include some major commercial deals or other cooperative endeavors, as Cold War relations did, but they would not conceal the fundamental rivalry.
In the initial post-conflict years, Europe and Ukraine will almost certainly organize their security against Russia. They will need a credible deterrent force to stabilize the long frontier stretching from the Barents Sea to the Black Sea across Ukrainian territory. In time, however, the desire to reduce the costs of stabilization will likely lead to arms control agreements with Russia. Societal interaction across the frontier will also assuredly grow. Russia and Europe, including Ukraine, remain natural commercial partners, as they have been historically. Europe does need access to Russian energy resources, if not to the excess of earlier years, to diversify its suppliers and lower prices. And Russia, despite the talk of a turn to the East, remains strategically focused on Europe, as the war against Ukraine perversely demonstrates. Like it or not, Europe, along with Ukraine, and Russia share a continent.
Those who seek justice and moral clarity will be disappointed by the dénouement of the Russia-Ukraine war and the likely evolution of relations with Russia. But wars in which neither side achieves a decisive victory end in messy compromises, and countries that share a continent must eventually find a way to coexist.
Whatever comes of Witkoff’s talks in St. Petersburg and Oslo, they should be measured against what is achievable. Victory as commonly understood may be out of reach, but an imperfect peace that holds out the promise of a better future can be achieved. That is a goal for Ukraine and the West worthy of the sacrifices already made and those still to come.
