Higher Financing Costs, Limited Demand Due to Worsening Affordability  for Homebuyers & Renters Among Factors Keeping Investor Sentiment Weak
SOUTH WINDSOR, Conn. , Oct. 22, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Real estate investor sentiment improved modestly after falling for two consecutive quarters, recovering from an all-time low index score of 84, and improving to 89 in the Fall 2026 RCN Capital/CJ Patrick Company Investor Sentiment Index (ISI)™. The index score marked the third consecutive quarter with a score below 90 and was 12 points lower year-over-year.
Two noteworthy factors appear to be influencing investor sentiment. First, the war in Iran, which nearly 60% of investors feel has had a negative impact on the real estate market. Some 40% cite increased energy costs and rising inflation due to the conflict, which they claim has impacted profitability and reduced demand from prospective homebuyers and renters.
Investors are also nonplussed by the recent action by the Federal Reserve to raise the Fed Funds rate. Over 50% of the respondents agreed that this will make borrowing more expensive, increasing their financing costs and reducing their profitability. There's also a concern that higher interest rates, which have already manifested themselves in mortgage rates climbing to 7.5%, will further hamper affordability for consumers looking to purchase a home, and put more strain on consumer finances for potential renters.
"Investor sentiment has tracked closely with investor activity – industry reports note that investor home purchases in the first two quarters of 2026 were down by 22% from a year ago," said RCN Capital CEO, Jeffrey Tesch. "Hopefully, the improvement in their outlook in our Fall survey is an indication that we'll see investors resume their important role in stabilizing a housing market that's suffering from a dearth of traditional homebuyers."
According to the Fall 2026 RCN Capital Investor Sentiment Survey™, the percentage of investors who viewed today's market as better or much better than it was a year ago, improved from just under 26% in the Summer to 37%, the highest number in three quarters. Those who viewed the market today as being worse fell from 44% to 38%. Similarly, 39% of the investors believe that conditions will improve over the next six months, up from 36% in the previous survey; and the number expecting conditions to worsen held steady at 30%.
"Real estate investors were already dealing with difficult market conditions – relatively high financing costs, limited inventory, rising home prices, and weak demand," said Rick Sharga, CJ Patrick Company CEO. "But the impact of the war in Iran – higher inflation, higher fuel prices, even higher lending costs – has soured investor sentiment, keeping it in line with consumer and builder sentiment, both of which are at or near all-time lows."
Many Investors Finance Purchases, So Lending Costs are Critical Investors responding to this survey mostly finance their property purchases – just under 20% said they made cash purchases – so the high cost of financing continues to be a major headwind. Investors use a variety of loan types to buy properties, including bridge loans, DSCR loans for rental properties, and conventional loans (typically 30-year, fixed-rate loans). Just over half of rental property investors use conventional financing once a tenant is in place.
Unsurprisingly, the high cost of financing continues to be cited as the biggest challenge faced by investors in today's market: over 70% of respondents claimed this was one of the biggest problems today. And almost half of the investors surveyed (45%) don't expect to see relief anytime soon, anticipating that interest rates will stay above 7% between now and the end of the year.
Investor Buying Plans Mostly Status Quo The Fall survey respondents differed a bit from those surveyed in the Summer when it came to their buying plans. About 36% plan to buy between 1-5 properties, down significantly from 55% in the Summer; on the other hand, 26% plan to buy between 6-10, a huge increase from last quarter's 10%; and the percent who plan to buy 11 or more properties doubled, from 3% to 6%. The number of investors who plan to make no purchases in 2026 fell two points, from 32% of respondents to 30%.
For the majority of investors (50%), this is about the same number of properties they purchased in the last 12 months. Only 7% plan to buy more properties than last year, down from 9% last quarter; while about 43% plan to buy fewer, up from 36% in the prior quarter.
In terms of market outlook, the contrast was stark between rental property and fix-and-flip investors. Only 13% of rental investors expected market conditions to get better in this most recent survey, while over 50% expected them to worsen. Flippers' sentiments were almost diametrically opposed, as over 56% of them believed conditions would improve, and just 17% feared that situations would be less favorable in the months ahead.
Insurance Challenges Continue to be a Concern for Real Estate Investors Insurance costs and limited availability of insurance continue to concern real estate investors. Nearly 80% of the respondents said that insurance issues were a factor in their investment decision-making, and 63% said insurance-related factors had caused them to miss out on a deal. Both of these results were more than 10 points higher than in the previous survey.
There were also significant differences between flippers and rental investors in their views on insurance: almost 90% of fix-and-flip investors claim that insurance issues are part of their decision-making process, compared to 69% of rental property investors. And flippers have also lost out on more deals than rental investors, 73% vs. 50%.
Most Investors Expect Home Prices to Rise Despite higher mortgage rates, almost two-thirds of the investors surveyed expect home prices to rise by the end of the year. Over 66% of the respondents said they anticipate higher home prices over the next six months. Almost 43% of the respondents expect those price increases to be higher than 5%, significantly higher than year-to-date price appreciation. On the other end of the spectrum, the number of investors expecting price declines fell by two points, from 13% last quarter to 11%.
Immigration and Tariff Policies Having an Impact Over 48% of the respondents said that immigration policies had made it harder for them to find and keep skilled workers – a 16-point jump from last quarter's results. The number of investors who said immigration policies had increased their construction costs rose from 29% to over 36%. And the number who said that these policies had little or no impact on their investing declined from 50% to just under 38%.
Tariffs also continue to be unpopular among investors: over 62% of investors felt that tariffs had resulted in higher prices on products and materials, up from 48% last quarter; 28% said they'd seen a reduction in their net margins, up two points from the last survey; and 26% said they'd noticed a disruption in supply chains, also rose by six points, from 26% to over 32%. Only 25% of the investors felt that tariffs did not have an impact on their business, down from 30% in the last quarter.
Rising Home Prices, Rising Material Costs, and Limited Inventory Major Challenges
The major challenges cited most often by investors in the Fall survey were similar to those noted in prior reports. But higher interest rates resulted in the number of investors concerned about the high cost of financing to spike, rising from 55% to over 70%. Rising home prices were noted by 38% of respondents, a 10-point dip from the prior quarter. Other frequently mentioned challenges included lack of inventory (31%), and rising material and product costs (23%).

More Fed rate hikes are coming. Politics aside, they are going to honor their mandate. With inflation remaining stubbornly high and the economy showing few signs of weakness, the Fed has little choice but to keep raising interest rates.
The central bank has a dual mandate that involves inflation and employment. When one becomes a problem, they need to act. If unemployment becomes a problem, they lower the fed funds rate to stimulate the economy. If inflation is the problem, they raise the rate to slow down the economy and push back against rising prices. How are things going now?
Emplo y ment isn’t a problem right now in any way, shape or form. In fact, the current unemployment rate is near the lowest level on record. At the same time, the economy is rock solid. The economy doesn’t need any help from the Fed. Inflation is a quite different story.
Inflation went away as a problem from the early 1980s to the 2020s. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) began lower than 2% in 2020 after having averaged around 2% for decades. But the inflation rate exploded in the following years. The CPI went from under 2% in 2020 to over 9% by the middle of 2022. The rate did move down to around 3% before the recent spike. But the genie is out of the bottle.

There are a couple of important things to understand about inflation . One is that the price increases are permanent; they never go back down. The other thing to understand about inflation is that it’s a pain in the you-know-what. You can’t get rid of it without a deep recession or worse. This was obvious during the inflation of the 1970s. Not without a painful economic downturn.
Inflation is sticky. And it’s here to stay. The Fed will raise the fed funds rate again at least one more time this year, and probably more next year. They’ll have to raise the rate until a crummy economy lowers inflation. That’s the inflation conundrum.
The current high interest rates are not an aberration. It’s the new normal.

Ukraine has a fraction of Russia's resources, but its aggressive development of drone warfare on defensive and offensive fronts has helped it endure an onslaught from its much bigger adversary, which invaded Ukraine in early 2022. That was true until last week, when Ukraine reported a throttling of the Starshield network that was causing its drones to stop midflight and fall out of the sky.
The two SpaceX networks operate on a constellation of thousands of low-Earth satellites &mdash; Starlink is the commercial version, while Starshield is a military product licensed to the U.S. government and its partners, among them Ukraine.
Kyiv's reliance on drones has made Starlink and Starshield nearly indispensable to its arsenal. When Ukraine experienced a disruption to those networks, it lost the shield of interceptors and drones that offered some protection against Russian drone and missile strikes.
Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy believes Musk throttled Starshield at the request of U.S. officials , though the world's richest man has denied any role and said it's the U.S. government that controls the military-grade network. President Trump has also denied he's responsible for slowing the network.

Here's what's known about Starshield and Starlink &mdash; and their role in the Ukraine war.
On Oct. 13, Ukraine's military began experiencing sudden difficulties with Starshield equipment, making it more difficult to carry out missions with fast-moving drones, officials and soldiers told CBS News .
Ukrainian soldiers said they noticed "speed limits" had been imposed on their use of SpaceX's Starshield internet dishes, the military-grade Starlink dishes that Ukrainians strap onto their drones so remote pilots can maintain their connection with the unmanned aircraft as they fly toward Russian targets.
Access was restricted by turning off dishes on Ukrainian drones flying above a certain speed, causing them to fall out of the sky. Ukraine's Ministry of Defense sources said SpaceX had unilaterally imposed the Starshield restrictions without informing them beforehand.
Zelenskyy said Saturday the speed limits had prevented Ukraine from using Starshield to shoot down Russian Shahed attack drones.
Starshield, the military-grade satellite network, runs on the same constellation of satellites as its civilian sister, Starlink, but has fewer technical limitations and more advanced security features. Both are operated by Musk's company SpaceX.
The U.S. government pays SpaceX to license the use of Starshield and controls who can access the technology. Its use has been limited to Ukraine and occupied territories. It is not available in Russia.

Starshield has no artificial speed cap and is known to work at speeds of at least 160 km/hr (99.4 mph). Starshield-equipped strike drones cruise at 160 km/h or more, and they're the backbone of Ukrainian mid-range strikes.
During the throttling over the last week, Ukrainian units reported link drops at speeds as low as 90 km/hr (55.9 mph), making those drones inoperable. As before, rhetoric substitutes for a hard-nosed assessment not so much of what is at stake as of what can be achieved in the short term and what long-term goals should guide current policy. The West Is Thinking Too Small About Ukraine's Victory
Since fall 2023, Ukraine's supporters have fallen into a pattern: Ukrainians warn that Russia is targeting its energy infrastructure to prevent them from heating their homes, schools, and businesses in the coming winter. Allies provide insufficient support to Ukraine's efforts to harden its energy grid and other critical infrastructure. Ukrainians devise new ways to stay warm, stretch the aid they do get as far as it can go, and survive the winter without catastrophic collapse. Finally, its supporters applaud their resilience, breathe a sigh of relief at the first signs of spring, and busy themselves with other things until the next fall. This recursive conversation belies the fact that the West, even Ukraine's staunchest allies, have a fundamentally short-term view of Russia's war on Ukraine. We are failing to understand Ukrainian victory as a generational project, one that requires deep, consistent engagement, not flailing, haphazard responses.
The West's approach to Ukraine often falls into two categories. The first is the crisis response, characterized by the cry, "Something must be done!" This exclamation &mdash; while an understandable reaction to Russian forces, say, dropping a bomb on a city bus or kidnapping a Ukrainian journalist and returning her body without organs &mdash; is rarely followed by action worthy of the crimes committed. Diplomats and national leaders who exclaim it usually then reach for the nearest salve they can find: often a hastily mixed concoction of strongly worded condemnations and sanctions. And if they are feeling exceptionally enraged, a token contribution of aid. In their haste, they rarely ask questions like, "Why, after 12 years of reacting to the same crimes with the same responses, does Russia continue to commit those crimes, and dream up even more sadistic ways to torture Ukrainians?"
The second could be described as "one weird trick" thinking. It goes something like this: If we just get Ukraine some exquisite weapons system, the war will end. If we just convince Ukraine to give up some chunk of land, the war will end. If we just sanction this Russian official, the war will end. If we just remove sanctions on this Russian official, the war will end. If we just get Ukrainians and Russians into a room together to talk, the war will end. And so on. And so on.
Of course, it is not surprising that these are the options that policymakers reach for. Statements, sanctions, aid packages, organizing meetings &mdash; these are all policy levers that one can pull with relative ease. In most cases, they are legal, quick, and relatively uncomplicated. They can be presented to democratic electorates who will give leaders points for caring without risking accusations of overinvestment or entanglement. These levers are historically precedented and, taken on their face, there are even examples of them working to stop conflicts in the past. They are also necessary. No Ukrainian official would ever refuse aid or sanctions packages, nor should they. For better or worse, Ukraine's armed forces would not be able to put up their heroic stand on the battlefield without capabilities and materiel provided by the West, equipment which Ukrainians have adapted and stretched past its capacity in ways its originators could not have imagined.
However, Russia is prosecuting its war on Ukraine in a way that is antithetical to both "something must be done" and "one weird trick" thinking. Some of the most insidious parts of Russia's effort to destroy the Ukrainian nation do not happen at one discrete moment, on one particular day. Over the past four winters, while avoiding the complete collapse of their energy grid, Ukrainians have endured the grinding reality of going to school, working, and keeping their lives in order, all while cold. It is true that Ukrainians' ingenuity extends to finding ways to teach their children and provide them with a semblance of normalcy amid full-scale war. And yet, even the luckiest Ukrainian kids still spend winters learning underground for hours on end or attempting to pay attention in class after cold, sleepless nights.
The West may not recognize Ukraine's winter experience as a slow-motion travesty, but for Russia, winter attacks have become a key part of its approach to degrading Ukrainian society. Look at some of the targets Russia has hit repeatedly this fall. Warehouses containing more than 8 million children's books , including textbooks for the coming school year. Data centers and mobile service providers . Trains between Poland and Ukraine. Bridges between Moldova and Ukraine.
In aggregate, what does this all mean? The nearly five million children living in Ukraine have faced chronic disruptions to their school days since the novel coronavirus pandemic, which was winding down as Russia invaded in 2022. When schools close because the power is out or traveling there is too dangerous, parents are left responsible for childcare, pulling them away from their jobs that keep the Ukrainian economy running. Getting an internet connection , especially in Kyiv, to work or go to school remotely is becoming challenging. Many Ukrainian families feel they have no choice but to leave , though they are increasingly trapped.
Liubov Zaliubovska and her colleagues at the Ukrainian educational organization Go Global often talk about Ukraine's young people as the " generation of victory ." This generation has earned this plaudit and much more. As Zaliubovska reminded us at an event I moderated recently, they are the ones who come up with innovations that give the Ukrainian armed forces their edge. They are the ones inventing new ways for cities to have heat and light through the winter. They are the ones who are rebuilding their nation as Russia attempts to destroy it.
This inspiring description alludes to a different, less comfortable truth. Victory for Ukraine will take a generation. This is not to say, God forbid, that Ukrainians will be fighting in a full-scale war for decades more. Rather, it is to say that victory will be determined by the success or failure of today's Ukrainian young people over the next decades. They have years ahead of defending Ukraine's borders, making it livable again, revitalizing its democracy and economy &mdash; all of which have just as much to do with victory as the terms of some eventual settlement.
This is an unfair burden to place on them. Ukrainians could be forgiven for reacting to Russia's depraved targeting of civilians and torturing of prisoners with flashes of white-hot rage followed by hasty responses built around vengeance. They could also be forgiven for falling for snake-oil salesmen who offer them too-good-to-be-true, easy ways out of the misery of this war. And yet, these Ukrainians who have grown up through pandemic and war succeed in recognizing the need for long-term thinking where their allies sitting in capitals across the West have failed.
The good news is that we have a model for what policymaking with an eye towards generational victory could look like for Ukraine, and it comes from the drone and defense technology industries that the generation of victory is best known for building. Ukraine's ministry of defense has created pathways for European allies, most famously Denmark , to invest in co-production models where they partner with Ukraine's most innovative and effective firms to produce weapons and other materiel needed on the front lines.
You can read plenty of good analysis about how these deals work and how they are mutually beneficial to investing countries and Ukrainians. However, the operative point here is that countries are taking direct interest in specific Ukrainian industries, firms, and people. They are thinking deeply about both how to leverage their investment into immediate battlefield success and the sustainability of this sector of Ukraine's economy decades into the future. Identifying firms, working with CEOs, structuring agreements, troubleshooting production delays &mdash; none of this is quick and easy. And yet, because these countries recognize the defense technology sector as wrapped up in Ukraine's long-term security and their own, they are willing to get into it. This attention to detail and committed partnership could be replicated across the sectors essential to Ukraine's survival.
For every vicious tactic Russia has used to wear down Ukrainian society, there are Ukrainians responding by developing solutions that are innovative, widely applicable, and underfunded &mdash; just as the drone and defense tech sectors were in the early years of the full-scale invasion. Take, for example, Mariupol State University , which has been operating in exile in Kyiv since its rector escaped the city during Russia's siege in late February 2022. The university has developed both high- and low-tech methods of reaching students from Mariupol. They are among Ukrainian educational institutions that are still reaching students in Russian-occupied territories in eastern Ukraine and Crimea where the censorship and surveillance apparatus borders on all-consuming . The university has succeeded at both imparting the skills needed to find jobs in Ukraine's patchwork, largely remote wartime economy and maintaining a sense of the culture of Mariupol and the university.
Governments or universities that enter long-term partnerships with schools like Mariupol State University could surely benefit from the expertise staff and students have to offer in areas ranging from veteran education to curriculum design for up-and-coming fields like drone design and operation. But more to the point, for those that recognize Ukraine's success as necessary for transatlantic security or an inherent good, closer attention would reveal that Russia could debilitate the Ukrainian state by placing unbearable strain on its core institutions, even if it cannot wipe it out through gains on the battlefield. Ukrainians are attempting to address massive gaps in elementary and middle school education while nearly 4 million internally displaced people are grasping for ways to remain connected to their hometown cultures. These are problems that require both a deep appreciation of what is happening the ground and a willingness to uncover and implement targeted solutions designed to address particular threats to Ukraine's survival.
Ukraine's political leadership recognized the outsized contributions of Mariupol State University when they made the university's rector, Mykola Trofymenko, deputy minister of education and science in 2025. As much as Ukraine benefits from having Trofymenko's experience integrated into its education policy, Ukrainian officials simply lack the time and funding to systematically cultivate more nation-sustaining institutions like Mariupol State University or visionaries like Trofymenko.
