More Fed rate hikes are coming. Politics aside, they are going to honor their mandate. With inflation remaining stubbornly high and the economy showing few signs of weakness, the Fed has little choice but to keep raising interest rates.
The central bank has a dual mandate that involves inflation and employment. When one becomes a problem, they need to act. If unemployment becomes a problem, they lower the fed funds rate to stimulate the economy. If inflation is the problem, they raise the rate to slow down the economy and push back against rising prices. How are things going now?
Emplo y ment isn’t a problem right now in any way, shape or form. In fact, the current unemployment rate is near the lowest level on record. At the same time, the economy is rock solid. The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta currently estimates 3.5% GDP growth in the third quarter . If true, that will be the highest level of GDP growth since the pandemic recovery in 2022. It’s also well above the average level of economic growth over the past several decades.
The economy doesn’t need any help from the Fed. Inflation is a quite different story.
Inflation went away as a problem from the early 1980s to the 2020s. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) began lower than 2% in 2020 after having averaged around 2% for decades. But the inflation rate exploded in the following years. The CPI went from under 2% in 2020 to over 9% by the middle of 2022. The rate did move down to around 3% before the recent spike. But the genie is out of the bottle.
These aren’t just a bunch of numbers for economics nerds to crunch. I’m sure you see it yourself. The prices of just about everything have gotten outrageous. By 2025, money was worth more than 20% less than it was just four years earlier. I bought two sandwiches at the deli the other day, nothing else, no chips or drinks. It cost $29. Are you kidding me?
I’ve morphed into my father. Apparently, I have some old-fashioned idea about what things should cost, and I’m still negatively surprised every time a bill comes due. I keep running around making outraged declarations like “twenty bucks?!”, just like Dad used to. It seems I’ve been transported to a world I don’t understand and don’t like. I know I’m weird, but I can’t be alone in this. I’ll bet you’re the same way.
There are a couple of important things to understand about inflation . One is that the price increases are permanent; they never go back down. Sure, commodity items like food and gas prices do go up and down. But the general level of prices never decreases. That would be deflation. That hasn’t occurred since the Great Depression. Reduced inflation only means that prices increase from current levels at a slower pace.
The other thing to understand about inflation is that it’s a pain in the you-know-what. You can’t get rid of it without a deep recession or worse. This was obvious during the inflation of the 1970s. The Fed would jack up rates and wreck the economy to get inflation under control. It worked. But just for a while. As soon as the Fed took its foot off the gas, inflation came right back, worse than before. Inflation is like one of those annoying birthday candles that you can’t blow out.
Inflation, as measured by CPI, has come way down from the nosebleed levels earlier this decade. In fact, CPI averaged less than 3% from the middle of 2024 until early this year. But inflation has moved higher again since the Iran war, and ensuing higher oil prices, began in March. Since then, the monthly headline inflation rate has averaged 3.6%, well above the Fed’s mandated 2% level.
If you’re old like me, you probably remember the double-digit inflation of the late ‘70s and early ‘80s. Back then, we would have killed for 3.6% inflation. It seems like today we have a bunch of wussies whining about a little 3.6%. But it’s not the same. Inflation is measured differently than it was back then . By the same 1970s standards, the rate is much higher. And it is a problem.
Sure, the recent increase in inflation is largely due to the higher energy prices that have resulted from the conflict with Iran. It’s also likely that the conflict will end in the not-too-distant future and oil prices and inflation will come back down. But it will not come down to the Fed’s mandated level of 2%. No way. Not without a painful economic downturn.
Inflation is sticky. And it’s here to stay. The Fed will raise the fed funds rate again at least one more time this year, and probably more next year. They’ll have to raise the rate until a crummy economy lowers inflation. That’s the inflation conundrum.
The current high interest rates are not an aberration. It’s the new normal.

​Europe stands by Ukraine – but how long can that last?
As US support ebbs away, looming European elections and growing financial strains across the continent could fracture the precious coalition that has kept Ukraine afloat
E urope woke up last Friday to another belligerent outburst from Donald Trump – this time berating Ukraine’s wartime leader Volodymyr Zelenskyy again. Just a few days after his diesel deal with the Kremlin, the US president said Zelenskyy was more difficult to deal with than Vladimir Putin and blamed Ukraine’s leader for rocketing oil prices – without any reference to the impact of his own war on Iran.
The contrast in Europe was obvious. When Zelenskyy walked into the multi-coloured summit room of the European Council headquarters in Brussels that Friday morning, the EU’s 27 leaders burst into applause, getting to their feet to offer big hugs and brisk handshakes. European Council president António Costa told Zelenskyy and the media: “This is no time to withdraw the support to Ukraine ,” calling instead for increased pressure on Russia. Zelenskyy was thankful, pointedly saying he was happy to have “huge support” from “real leaders”.
The symbolism was powerful. As US support crumbles, Europe seems to be stepping up. According to the Kiel Institut’s Ukraine Support Tracker , Europe – including non-EU countries such as the UK and Norway – has spent €236bn supporting Ukraine from 2022 to April 2026, compared with a US outlay of €115bn.
While Trump is weakening sanctions, Europe is promising more. The EU has passed 21 rounds of sanctions against Russia and last year agreed to raise a €90bn loan for Ukraine – an unprecedented sum for a non-member state. Meanwhile the EU has opened accession negotiations with Kyiv and given shelter to 4.4million Ukrainian refugees.
Behind the declarations of Europe’s enduring support, the outlook is more complex and less encouraging for Ukraine, as it approaches its fifth winter since the launch of Russia’s all-out invasion.
First, Europe is not able to replace US military dominance in areas such as intelligence. This was brutally exposed by the disruption to the US SpaceX technologies that Ukraine uses in frontline drone operations and air-defences protecting civilians from Russian air attacks. Zelenskyy said the US had intentionally throttled its Starlink satellite internet access, a charge that senior figures in the Trump administration have denied.
Ukraine also depends on US-made Patriot interceptors capable of shooting down Russian ballistic missiles. Deepening the spat, Zelenskyy this week accused the Trump administration of preventing European countries from passing on their Patriot stocks to Ukraine.
Secondly, Europe’s monetary support for Ukraine is not evenly spread. Germany is the second-largest donor, having spent €54bn since the start of the war, a particularly crucial contribution since Trump ceased virtually all new US funding. But a report from the Kiel Institut describes this funding as “sizeable but not exceptional” given the scale of the German economy. Four Nordic countries together have given more than Germany, even though they total only a third of Germany’s population, the researchers note. The EU’s other big economies, France and Italy, give less than Germany.
The Nordic, Baltic and other north European states are Ukraine’s most generous donors when contributions are measured against national income, according to the Kiel Institut. Denmark, for instance, spends an equivalent to 4.17% of its gross domestic product supporting Ukraine, and Estonia 3.77%, compared with 1.49% spent by Germany, 1.1% by France, 1% by Italy and 0.86% by the UK. With Ukraine’s huge bill to defend itself growing, and Russia’s relentless attacks continuing to wreak a heavy toll, these more generous donors might arguably feel Europe’s biggest economies – and other western allies – could do more to shoulder these ever-rising costs.

There is a risk 2027 will be the year “when political reality, electoral reality, policy reality, economic reality catches up with Europe and prevents it being able to go further in its support for Ukraine”, he said.

The far-right leader and frontrunner Marine Le Pen is seeking to present herself as the candidate to restore order and security, after days of rioting and looting across French cities. As before, rhetoric substitutes for a hard-nosed assessment not so much of what is at stake as of what can be achieved in the short term and what long-term goals should guide current policy.
