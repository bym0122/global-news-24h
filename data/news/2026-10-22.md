Despite US-Israeli airstrikes and Tehran’s attacks on shipping, the waterway still has to provide a lifeline for those that live and work there
By Stefanie Glinski ; photographs by Mohammad Mohsenifar in Bandar Abbas/on Hormuz Island
Capt Farouq turns the wheel as he watches the ferry terminal of Bandar Abbas grow smaller, its bright lights flickering in the distance.
In front of him, the navigation radar beeps periodically, its green and pink lights glowing in the darkness. The ferry, today at about half capacity with roughly 100 passengers, turns and heads into the darkness of the strait of Hormuz.
He’s been a captain for the past 18 years, and the strait of Hormuz is his second home
Farouq, 39, sits comfortably in his cabin, overlooking the pitch black horizon as he sails towards Hormuz Island, a short commute of about half an hour from the Iranian mainland’s Haqqani Port, for 340,000 toman, about £1. He is relaxed and smiles as he pulls into the deeper waters. He’s been a captain for the past 18 years, and the strait of Hormuz is his second home.
Farouq says the crossings have not stopped since the start of the US-Israeli war on Iran on 28 February, although passenger numbers are down and the journey has become more dangerous. For months, Farouq has heard explosions coming from the strait, unclear as to whether that is air defence or attacks, or both. Tonight is no different.
Inside the ferry, passengers say they have gotten used to the sounds too, and, to an extent, the war itself. They drink tea and smoke cigarettes. Some nap, while others entertain their children. It’s hot and humid, the air thick, the windows fogged with condensation.
Even with a fragile ceasefire in place, here on the coast, it has barely felt as though the war has stopped.
According to Iranian government sources, attacks on the southern port of Bandar Abbas have damaged 760 fishing and commercial vessels. Farouq says his own ferry was also damaged by shrapnel while docked at the port, though it was quickly repaired.
Since the start of the war, Iranian forces have repeatedly attacked vessels attempting to cross the strait. In the first week of October alone, at least 12 attacks were reported against oil, liquefied natural gas and liquefied petroleum gas tankers, sending oil prices back above $100 a barrel .
With pressure mounting on the US president, Donald Trump, before the midterm elections, he is rumoured to be once again considering strikes.
But beyond the headlines and global markets, the strait is first and foremost a lifeline for many Iranians living in the southern province of Hormozgan.
Fishers in Bandar Abbas selling their catch on the street
Fishers, captains and harbour workers all depend on the strait for their livelihoods and the war has made their work increasingly difficult. Fishers can no longer venture as far out, ports have come under attack, and many people fled Bandar Abbas in the early months of the war for smaller, more remote villages in search of safety.
Local residents fishing for their own daily consumption
Anvar Sadat, a 38-year-old fisher who works at Bandar Abbas’s fish market, says business has slowed and that making ends meet has become increasingly difficult.
“Fishing boats can’t go out as freely as before the war, and our movement is restricted,” he says, adding that several of his colleagues in the city have been killed in attacks.
Anvar Sadat, who works at the fish market in Bandar Abbas
“This place was hell,” says Abdul Salam, a 45-year-old garbage collector. The beaches, particularly those near the port, were among the most dangerous places during the height of the war, he says, describing heavy attacks that forced him to stop working almost entirely to avoid the risk.
Life on the island, however, has been different, Farouq explains as he steers into the small, dimly lit port of Hormuz Island, his last crossing of the day before heading back to his family for the night.
Hassan, 28, cafe owner – has run a cafe on the island for eight years
Hormuz Island has long been known as a kind of “hippy paradise”, a place Iranians have come to regard as “free”. Artists and creatives moved here from bigger cities in search of peace; women swim in bathing suits, writers and painters find space to work, and life moves slower. There is “love and magic” here, as Yashar, a 35-year-old surfer, puts it, “a different rhythm”.
A group of local women swimming off the coast of Hormuz Island
Life here has changed little, Yashar says, although the war has brought some new restrictions, and a handful of attacks, mostly on infrastructure. The roughly 8,000 residents are trying to support each other, and to preserve the sense of freedom that has set the island apart, he adds.
The government has closed off parts of Hormuz Island’s north, but beyond the now restricted areas, life has continued, he explains. “As long as you don’t go north, they leave you alone,” he says of the army.
Omran, a 45-year-old tour guide – the tourism sector has been hit by the conflict
The Majara hotel, also known as Rainbow accommodation, is one of Hormuz’s attractions. It has been closed since the beginning of the war
“The tourism sector has taken the biggest hit,” says Omran, a 45-year-old tour guide, adding that he prefers to go by his first name only. “Guesthouses and homestays remain closed, as do restaurants and many cafes.” Visitors have largely stopped coming since the war, dealing a heavy blow to an island whose economy depends on tourism and fishing, both of which have been disrupted.
The Salt valley on Hormuz Island, one of the island’s tourist attractions
From the higher parts of the island, Omran says, it is possible to see deep into the strait on clear days, when the haze lifts and the humid air clears, all the way to the mountains of Khasab in Oman.
Residents have climbed up these barren mountains of red and white stone, at times watching the war unfold in the distance. Tankers remain stranded in the strait, explosions can be heard regularly. Yet residents also say the war has not driven them from the island.
Khaleh Kaniz, a 67-year-old painter who was born and raised on Hormuz, laughs when asked whether she had considered leaving in February, at the start of the war.
“Why should we leave? Where would we go? Our homes and our lives are here.”

​Europe stands by Ukraine – but how long can that last?
As US support ebbs away, looming European elections and growing financial strains across the continent could fracture the precious coalition that has kept Ukraine afloat
E urope woke up last Friday to another belligerent outburst from Donald Trump – this time berating Ukraine’s wartime leader Volodymyr Zelenskyy again. Just a few days after his diesel deal with the Kremlin, the US president said Zelenskyy was more difficult to deal with than Vladimir Putin and blamed Ukraine’s leader for rocketing oil prices – without any reference to the impact of his own war on Iran.
The contrast in Europe was obvious. When Zelenskyy walked into the multi-coloured summit room of the European Council headquarters in Brussels that Friday morning, the EU’s 27 leaders burst into applause, getting to their feet to offer big hugs and brisk handshakes. European Council president António Costa told Zelenskyy and the media: “This is no time to withdraw the support to Ukraine ,” calling instead for increased pressure on Russia. Zelenskyy was thankful, pointedly saying he was happy to have “huge support” from “real leaders”.
The symbolism was powerful. As US support crumbles, Europe seems to be stepping up. According to the Kiel Institut’s Ukraine Support Tracker , Europe – including non-EU countries such as the UK and Norway – has spent €236bn supporting Ukraine from 2022 to April 2026, compared with a US outlay of €115bn.
While Trump is weakening sanctions, Europe is promising more. The EU has passed 21 rounds of sanctions against Russia and last year agreed to raise a €90bn loan for Ukraine – an unprecedented sum for a non-member state. Meanwhile the EU has opened accession negotiations with Kyiv and given shelter to 4.4million Ukrainian refugees.
Behind the declarations of Europe’s enduring support, the outlook is more complex and less encouraging for Ukraine, as it approaches its fifth winter since the launch of Russia’s all-out invasion.
First, Europe is not able to replace US military dominance in areas such as intelligence. This was brutally exposed by the disruption to the US SpaceX technologies that Ukraine uses in frontline drone operations and air-defences protecting civilians from Russian air attacks. Zelenskyy said the US had intentionally throttled its Starlink satellite internet access, a charge that senior figures in the Trump administration have denied.
Ukraine also depends on US-made Patriot interceptors capable of shooting down Russian ballistic missiles. Deepening the spat, Zelenskyy this week accused the Trump administration of preventing European countries from passing on their Patriot stocks to Ukraine.
Secondly, Europe’s monetary support for Ukraine is not evenly spread. Germany is the second-largest donor, having spent €54bn since the start of the war, a particularly crucial contribution since Trump ceased virtually all new US funding. But a report from the Kiel Institut describes this funding as “sizeable but not exceptional” given the scale of the German economy. Four Nordic countries together have given more than Germany, even though they total only a third of Germany’s population, the researchers note. The EU’s other big economies, France and Italy, give less than Germany.
The Nordic, Baltic and other north European states are Ukraine’s most generous donors when contributions are measured against national income, according to the Kiel Institut. Denmark, for instance, spends an equivalent to 4.17% of its gross domestic product supporting Ukraine, and Estonia 3.77%, compared with 1.49% spent by Germany, 1.1% by France, 1% by Italy and 0.86% by the UK. With Ukraine’s huge bill to defend itself growing, and Russia’s relentless attacks continuing to wreak a heavy toll, these more generous donors might arguably feel Europe’s biggest economies – and other western allies – could do more to shoulder these ever-rising costs.
Financial aid from 24 January 2022 to 31 August 2026 as a % of a country’s 2021 GDP. Illustration: Guardian graphic/Source: Kiel Institute for the World Economy Thirdly, and most critically, Europe’s support for Ukraine could be reaching its limits, as Europe enters a supercycle of elections in which the far-right are expected to make gains. National votes are due in Spain, France, Poland and Italy, four of the five biggest EU member states by population. Mujtaba Rahman , managing director for Europe at the Eurasia Group, argued that the biggest risk for Ukraine is not Trump’s opinions, but the potential for European support to fragment. There is a risk 2027 will be the year “when political reality, electoral reality, policy reality, economic reality catches up with Europe and prevents it being able to go further in its support for Ukraine”, he said.
France’s elections could be the most consequential. The far-right leader and frontrunner Marine Le Pen is seeking to present herself as the candidate to restore order and security, after days of rioting and looting across French cities. She has also vowed to cease aid to Ukraine within six months of taking office “because we simply cannot do it any more”. Speaking to the Financial Times (£) , Le Pen vowed to slash France’s contributions to the EU budget, which raises questions about a proposed €100bn fund for Ukraine in the upcoming 2028-34 budget cycle. Beyond Ukraine, her election could plunge the EU into a deep internal crisis that will consume political energies and attention.
US patience is running out with Ukraine as US special envoy Steve Witkoff meets with Vladimir Putin in Russia. Photograph: Kristina Solovyova/Reuters “European support for Ukraine is already fragmenting,” argued Rahman, who points out the €90bn loan for Ukraine was agreed by only 24 EU countries (Hungary, Czechia and Slovakia opted out). But a Le Pen victory could tilt the balance, especially if anti-Ukraine parties make gains in Spain, Poland and Italy. “Le Pen is the biggest gamechanger because she will provide cover for many other countries to say: ‘enough is enough, we can’t keep funding,’” Rahman said.
That scenario paints “a really dark picture for Ukraine, a really sad picture for Ukraine” he said. “Because it’s not about Ukraine’s performance on the battlefield – Ukraine has done a remarkable job of holding the line. This is the risk Europe has been talking about for a very long time, and has promised Ukraine many times it would not allow to happen: that through strategic patience, Russia gains in the negotiating room what it has not been able to gain on the battlefield. And that is precisely the risk Ukraine is now confronting.”
To head off this risk, EU leaders will seek to shore up support for Ukraine by agreeing another funding plan. The head of the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen, confirmed that Kyiv has a €70bn funding gap in 2027, although she expects Ukraine’s other international partners to help raise the sum.
Jana Kobzová, co-director of the European Security Programme at the European Council on Foreign Relations, said an EU leaders summit in December will be the moment when “we should have a clear idea and mechanism” on providing the €70bn for Ukraine ahead of key elections. “I think the urgency and the consciousness on the side of the European leaders is already there.”
To some extent, EU Russia policy is protected, after a recent decision to keep sanctions in place for three years, avoiding the need for divisive rollover decisions every six months.
But a Le Pen victory in six months’ time could lead to a bigger problem than financing Ukraine, suggests Kobzová. “If there is a huge shift in Europe as a result of these elections that are going to affect the way the EU itself works, then Ukraine might become collateral damage.”
