This disparity is central to “Aftershocks,” a study published on September 17 by the McKinsey Global Institute. It assesses 65 economies by their exposure to supply disruptions and their capacity to respond. The surge in freight rates pointed to the binding constraint: deliverability, not availability. A cargo of crude oil, refined products or LNG needs a suitable vessel, insurance and safe passage, and refining, storage or regasification capacity at its destination.
Greece already controls key links in this supply chain, possessing the world’s largest merchant fleet by carrying capacity, refineries and storage facilities, ports, and shipbuilding and repair yards. What Greece still lacks is a permanent mechanism for coordination between the state and the shipping industry when supply chains break down.
Norway has relied on such a framework for decades. In 1940, its government assumed control of most of the merchant fleet outside German-controlled areas. The model was revived during the Cold War and took its present form in 2002 as Nortraship, a standing body linking government and shipowners, with an operational role in crises.
