In its report , the Hague-based group, Global Rights Compliance (GRC), said North Korea reaps funds from the operation, taking much of the wages earned by its workers who are sent to Russia and made to stay under threat of retribution.
The group cites open-source information on the operation run by a Russian businessman, facial recognition technology to identify North Korean officials, and evidence gathered by its members posing undercover as prospective employers.
“This investigation exposes a dangerous convergence between two states’ military ambitions,” said the group’s legal adviser, Elise Lauriot Prevost.

Russia’s foreign ministry and the North Korean Embassy in Moscow did not respond to emails seeking comment.
The rights group said it contacted two Russians identified as key figures in the network, but only one replied, denying the accusation. It did not say whether the other responded.
Since 2024, North Korea has sent more than 10,000 troops to fight alongside Russians in the Ukraine war, as well as weapons to replenish Moscow’s dwindling armory while gaining valuable battlefield experience, South Korea’s intelligence agency has said.
To evade U.N. sanctions that ban member nations from hiring North Korean workers, the latter enter Russia on student visas in the guise of enrolling at colleges, GRC said.
Then they are contracted out to Russian employers, with brokers receiving a commission of up to 150,000 rubles ($1,860) for each, it added.
GRC investigators posed undercover as prospective employers and found the workers were hired at production sites, including a military rations factory.

One advertisement assured prospective employers that workers would not “run away,” the report said. Workers labor 12-hour days with two days off each month, it added, citing such ads.
After North Korea’s sixth and last nuclear test in 2017, a U.N. Security Council resolution put a blanket ban on the hiring of its workers overseas and mandated their return home.
Russia and China , two permanent members of the Security Council that voted for the ban, have since been accused by South Korea and the West of noncompliance with resolutions.
Such measures, dating back to 2006, banned the flow of goods, energy, weapons and workers with North Korea.
In previous years, Russia employed North Korean laborers in its far east while many North Koreans staffing restaurants in China sent home wages that were channeled to the state treasury, rights groups have said.
North Korea is directly involved in running the network supplying workers in Russia, with officials from its Moscow embassy identified as inspecting work sites for prospective employment, the GRC report said.
“These workers do not come cheap, yet little, if any, of the money paid for their labor reaches them,” said Dylan Carter, a GRC lead investigator.
“Instead, it lines the pockets of the DPRK state,” he added, referring to North Korea by a short version of its official name, the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea.

In Lisbon, tens of thousands of mostly young people marched earlier this month. Tear gas and water cannon were used on protesters in Brussels, after young men dressed in black, their faces covered to hide their identities, threw rocks and firecrackers at police. In Albania, demonstrators alleging government corruption are demanding that their Prime Minister Edi Rama - already 13 years in power - stand down.
This continent feels volatile, socially and politically.
I&#x27;ve spent years in Europe reporting from a multitude of demonstrations, both peaceful and violent. I remember well the ardent Catalan pro-independence marches of 2017, the desperation and Molotov cocktails flying through Athens&#x27; streets during the 2015 Greek debt crisis, and various outbreaks of violence in France during President Emmanuel Macron&#x27;s first term in office, sparked by fuel tax and pension reform proposals.
Image caption, Hundreds of thousands have joined protests in France, some of which turned violent
But this feels different. Protests are exploding simultaneously across Europe and the majority of demonstrators - both peaceful and violent - are young. Some really young. Among those arrested, there&#x27;s an eyebrow-raising number of minors. Over half of those detained in Barcelona scuffles last weekend were under 18. Minors were among those apprehended by police in Milan after clashes, according to Italy&#x27;s LaPresse news agency.
Europe&#x27;s governments, for the most part, have been careful to distinguish between looters and rioters on the one hand, and loud but peaceful, legally organised demonstrations on the other. The latter tend to focus around a particular cause. In the current protest wave, placards and chants mainly take aim at education grievances, social inequalities and the rising cost of living - though each country also comes with its own unique set of complaints.
What has triggered this public fury in Europe&#x27;s youth, seemingly so quickly? Are all these demonstrations in some way interwoven? And with major European elections on the horizon, how will governments choose to respond - listening to protesters&#x27; concerns, or playing the law and order card to win support from older voters?
Students have taken to the streets in France, Belgium, Ireland, Spain, Portugal, Italy, Greece, Serbia and Albania - a more than 1,000-mile span of the continent.
Demonstrations are often organised in an ad hoc way via TikTok or Instagram. Belgium&#x27;s interior minister has complained about what he calls the "TikTokisation" of protests.
Student unions play a role in organising marches, but they are also inspired by the actions of peers in other countries, even other continents. Think of the so-called Cockroach student movement in India - a Gen Z social and political protest movement that emerged in May this year, starting with criticism of the Indian education system.
France, where the current wave of student protests kicked off in late September, fuelled by student complaints over staff shortages and dilapidated buildings, is a stand-out catalyst in Europe. There&#x27;s a saying in neighbouring Belgium: whenever it pours in Paris, it at least drizzles in Brussels. The Belgian capital and the town of LiÃ¨ge have also now seen weeks of protesting youngsters.
Image caption, In Athens, school pupils and teachers have demonstrated over education and the cost of living

In Athens, 17-year-old Greek school pupil Yiannis Papanikolaou told AFP: "School buildings are terrible and poorly maintainedâ¦ I think something might change, because of what France is doing, France will spur Greece."
There&#x27;s a continent-wide sense of economic malaise felt by large numbers of young people â and it goes far beyond education. They worry about the environment, and fret that AI will rob them of job opportunities. They fear the spiralling cost of living could prevent them from finishing their education and stop them buying or renting a home big enough to start a family.
In Madrid and in Lisbon, frustration has focused on the prohibitive price tag attached to housing. Portuguese Instagram-linked campaigns stated "enough is enough" and called on people to take to the streets as peers had done in Spain.
In Lisbon, Carolina â Cruz, 26, a PhD student and biology researcher, said she faces losing her home as her rental contract nears expiry. "I&#x27;m the first person in my family to enter a doctoral programme and even with all the studying I&#x27;ve done, I still can&#x27;t make it," she told Reuters.
It&#x27;s an anger shared by 22-year-old Riccardo, a protester in Italy&#x27;s financial capital, Milan. "Even if we don&#x27;t manage to throw the country into chaos like in France or block every road like in Spain, the aim is at least people will start talking about these issues again," he told AFP.
And it&#x27;s certainly not just young people on the left of politics who are angry. At the presidential campaign launch of French nationalist Marine Le Pen last weekend, young supporters described different concerns to me. Calling themselves the "silent majority" - not (yet) out demonstrating - they say they also worry deeply about "uncontrolled immigration" and safety on France&#x27;s streets, as well as the cost of living.
The outpouring of discontent has spread beyond young people to include trade unions, France&#x27;s disparate Yellow Jacket movement that protested against the cost of living crisis during Macron&#x27;s first term, and other citizens&#x27; initiatives. This weekend, Kent in coastal southern England saw two protests, one for and one against immigration.
This miscellaneous mix of protests can seem confusing.
Mark Leonard, the director of the European Council of Foreign Relations, speaks of a build-up of cascading, competing crises in Europe, none of which have ever been properly resolved, he says. That sense of pent-up anger is now bubbling over onto the streets.
Image caption, Anti-government marches have swept through Tirana, the Albanian capital
"One crisis layers on top of the other," he told me. "The financial crisis of 2007-8, the migrant crisis of 2015, the environmental crisis, Covid, inflation after Russia&#x27;s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 and now again with the war in the Gulf."
Leonard says different "tribes" have emerged out of these crises. Social media consumption and algorithms harden their differing perspectives, leading to a real sense of polarisation in Europe.
"We&#x27;ve already seen this in the US," says Leonard. "A sense of &#x27;my crisis is more important than your crisis&#x27;."
Nathalie Tocci, a former Italian government adviser who is now a politics professor at Johns Hopkins University, tells me we shouldn&#x27;t be so taken aback by the protests, or the youth engagement in them.
"We tend to think of youngsters as politically switched off and disinterested. We think they don&#x27;t bother to go out to vote etc, but they are truly engaged," she told me, "just in a different, more grassroots way. Think of the "Fridays for the Future [environmental movement] across Europe a few years ago, or the demonstrations against Israel&#x27;s actions in Gaza after 7 October.
She points to the student-led protest movement in Serbia, which recently defeated long-standing leader Aleksandar VuÄiÄ in parliamentary elections. "It&#x27;s quite something. Intelligence services in France and Belgium have described looting on the edges of legally approved protests as mainly opportunistic.
The rioting, they suggest, is a mixture of "mindless violence" - also identified by the Spanish authorities in Barcelona to describe clashes there at the weekend - plus mayhem intentionally orchestrated by youth from ultra-left radical groups.
Tocci reminded me that, in Italy, you can hear the word "maranza" being used by some Italians upset by the perpetrators of destruction seemingly for the sake of it: smashing or burning cars or public bins, for example.
In France, rioters are often described as "casseurs" - people who break things. But in private, some use a more politically charged term - "racaille". Like "maranza" in Italy, it&#x27;s pejorative slang, generally used to describe disaffected, rowdy urban youths frequently from working-class and/or immigrant backgrounds. It was used in October 2005 by then-French Interior Minister Nicolas Sarkozy, to describe young delinquents and rioters during a tense visit to the housing projects of Paris suburb Argenteuil.
French media has also asked whether there&#x27;s been an alliance of convenience, however informal or incidental, between two groups of youths: ultra-left activists and young people from France&#x27;s disadvantaged neighbourhoods. Like the anti-capitalist left, youth from public housing projects on the outskirts of French cities (known as the banlieues) often feel discriminated against, and complain of police heavy handedness towards them.
Image caption, Puerta del Sol square in Madrid has been occupied by tents since late September
The political right and centre in France, including Macron, blame left-wing radical groups and "delinquents" for planning the riots. In contrast to Le Pen&#x27;s National Rally Party, they have not mentioned a link with past or current immigration to France.
Critics accuse anti-immigration parties in Europe of trying to capitalise from the riots to score political points. In Belgium, the anti-immigration opposition Flemish nationalist party, Vlaams Belang, appeared to blame migrants for violence in LiÃ¨ge and Brussels, describing the riots as "imported" and blaming a failure of integration and of open borders. Belgium&#x27;s Interior Minister Bernard Quentin has pointed the finger at ultra-left groups.
Fresh mass demonstrations have been planned over the next days in France and in Italy.
Governments are trying to crack down on the violence. There have been thousands of arrests, and Italy and France have promised to impose a "you break it, you pay for it" policy for rioters.
Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni is also making parents of minors liable for damage caused by their kids, including deducting welfare support received by the family. The Civil Code in France already holds parents who live with their children liable for damage they cause.
But some of these European governments may not be in power for long. All the big EU countries have been holding, or will hold, key elections in the coming months. France&#x27;s presidential vote is considered the most pivotal for Europe. Le Pen is consistently polling far ahead of her rivals. It&#x27;s assumed the unrest on French streets is helping her, thanks to her party&#x27;s law and order message.
In Spain, Italy, Germany and Poland, nationalist, tough-on-immigration parties are also polling well. These parties promise to restore order and national pride. Leonard believes populist or non-traditional parties on the left and the right have become "crisis entrepreneurs", using the turbulent situation in Europe to attract disaffected voters.
Meanwhile, Europe&#x27;s political mainstream faces a crisis of leadership, says GrÃ©goire Roos, director of Europe at the Chatham House think tank.
I would argue they also face a crisis of legitimacy. The fact that so many people - the young and not so young - are out protesting indicates they have failed to get on top of things.
Europe&#x27;s car makers are in crisis. Will the threat of war rescue them?

But it&#x27;s not at all clear that new elections will resolve public frustration. France is so polarised, for example, that if Le Pen were to become president, many predict her opponents would fill French streets in anger.
Modern Europe is built on an assumption that citizens pay taxes and obey laws. In exchange, they get good public services, like education and healthcare, personal safety and a stable society that follows certain rules and regulations.
But this "social contract" has clearly eroded, says Roos.
Looking at the ferment on Europe&#x27;s streets, this moment of protest looks likely to continue.

KYIV, Ukraine — Like many tech entrepreneurs in Ukraine, Yaroslav Azhnyuk turned to developing drone warfare after Russia's 2022 invasion.
Drones have transformed the way wars are fought. Azhnyuk says artificial intelligence is making drones faster and more capable.
"When the other side has orders of magnitudes higher capabilities, it gives you an asymmetric advantage," he says. "It can make you 10,000 times more effective."
Azhnyuk runs the Ukraine-based defense firms The Fourth Law and Odd Systems. They are among the top companies producing AI-powered drones in Ukraine. Dozens of Ukrainian military units already use their autonomous drone, called the Lupynis-10-TFL-1.
Russia is also deploying AI-enabled drones against Ukraine. AI warfare is part of a high-tech arms race that has many worried, including Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He has warned that AI could lead decisions on the battlefield as soon as next year.
Azhnyuk told NPR that he acknowledges the concern over destructive weapons but says it's not specific to AI.
"It has been with humanity since the invention of fire or steel," he says. "Any kind of tool can be used for good or for bad. And in the end, it's the human that decides how to use that tool."
Azhnyuk is 37, a gregarious computer scientist who hails from a family of scholars and scientists. NPR meets him in a conference room in Kyiv; for security reasons he asked us not to reveal much else about the location.
He worked for years in Silicon Valley and started several companies, including Petcube , which makes smartphone-powered cameras that use artificial intelligence to watch and even entertain pets while they're alone.
"These cameras allow pet parents to watch pets when they're not at home," he says, "and fling treats to them and shine a laser toy for them. We sold a million of these devices that are quite popular in the U.S."
After Russia launched its full-scale war, Azhnyuk, by then living in Ukraine, pivoted to the defense space.
"I was like, OK, I've got to move on from Petcube and do something to protect my country," he says. "And so now we're making not cameras that fling treats but cameras that fling explosives at people who came to occupy our land."
The cameras he's talking about are part of an image recognition system incorporated into first-person-view or FPV drones.
"We are leaders in autonomous drones and thermal cameras," he says.
A Lupynis-10-TFL-1 drone is seen in the air at a test site in central Ukraine.
The autonomous Lupynis TFL-1 drone combines a small, affordable FPV drone with an autonomous terminal guidance unit. That means the drone has an onboard computer and AI technology that takes control during the last 500 meters (or 0.3 miles) of the flight.
"But it's very different from, say, the kind of AI that sits on those data centers that power Claude," he says. "Or even the kind of AI that does self-driving for your Tesla."
Azhnyuk says this AI technology uses simple tools to recognize images and make smart software "which then allows you either to navigate autonomously without a human or to choose a target autonomously or to fly toward the target autonomously."
The Fourth Law's autonomous drone is named after the 20th century Ukrainian poet and political activist Anatoliy Lupynis, who endured decades of Soviet imprisonment and psychiatric torture. The company named all its weapons after Ukrainian intellectuals and artists killed or persecuted by Russians in the last century.
"It is important for us to help to resurrect those people," Azhnyuk says. "Their ideas are still with us. And now they're fighting back."
The Fourth Law teams test the Lupynis TFL-1 drones every day. We meet one team in an overgrown field outside Kyiv. They've set up a fold-out table near a copse of trees and a hornets' nest.
A slight, sandy-haired man stands up from the fold-out table and says he's the head of the test and integration team. He uses the military call sign Stab. The Fourth Law asked NPR not to reveal this location or the identities of those who work here for security reasons.
"So you see here is our camera, plus the microcomputer inside this 3-D model," Stab says. "All of the mind is here," he adds with a laugh.
The microcomputer inside the drone runs on a deep-learning neural network that uses hours of combat footage to recognize targets.
"Like vehicles, light-armored vehicles, tanks, and flying objects," he says.
The drone begins its flight guided by a pilot, who also asked us to use his call sign, Professor. He navigates the drone with a control stick. He selects a target and checks a video monitor. Then he sees a red flash.
The drone has locked onto its target: a dilapidated car an employee is driving along a dirt road. Professor lets go of the control stick. The drone flies itself to the car. The microcomputer inside constantly runs algorithms to keep it on course.
"See, Professor is doing nothing, and the drone is doing everything itself," Stab says.

Nearly halfway through his second term in office, U.S. President Donald Trump has undercut both the United States’ capacity to protect its interests related to China and its role as a provider of regional security in Asia. His undisciplined approach to dealing with Beijing, in which he has focused on building warm personal ties with Chinese leader Xi Jinping and discounted or ignored longstanding sources of tension between the two countries, has reduced Washington’s leverage to shape the relationship. At the same time, Trump’s sustained attacks on allies and partners have driven them to hedge against the United States and strengthen their ties with China. Beijing, in turn, has successfully cast itself as a steady counterweight to an erratic Washington: in 25 of 36 countries that the Pew Research Center surveyed in the spring of 2026—among them Australia, Canada, France, Germany, and Mexico—more respondents had a favorable view of China than of the United States.
Given the failures of Trump’s second-term strategy, many Democrats and even moderate Republicans may try to strike more hawkish postures on China after the midterm elections and during the 2028 presidential campaign. This temptation is understandable: there is a strong desire to provide a contrast to a flailing approach and correct what Trump has gotten wrong. But succumbing to it would be dangerous. If Congress and future presidential candidates reflexively adopt a more aggressive stance toward China, they could both undercut a future administration’s capacity to preserve vital diplomatic space with Beijing and further disquiet allies and partners that do not want to be trapped in a new twilight struggle. Overcorrecting Trump’s China policy also would be unlikely to help candidates get elected or boost their public profile in the runup to 2028. Recent polling shows that only a small share of Americans in seven battleground states view China as an enemy or consider it to be one of the top threats to U.S. security.
The goal for the next Congress and, eventually, Trump’s successor, should be to prioritize steady competence over performative toughness in dealing with China. Political point scoring and wistful nostalgia for strategies that discount Beijing’s mounting power, and growing domestic support in the United States for great-power calm, would compound Trump’s mistakes rather than mitigate them. A smarter, more coherent China policy—one rooted in disciplined competition with Beijing, patient efforts to build goodwill with anxious allies and partners, and vigorous efforts to refresh the domestic sources of U.S. strength—can make life safer, healthier, and more affordable for Americans.
Trump’s views on China are iconoclastic in Washington, and even at odds with other members of his own administration and party. Whereas most policymakers agree that China is the United States’ foremost strategic competitor, Trump has embraced a narrow conception of competition focused primarily on the size of China’s trade surplus and its export of chemicals used to make fentanyl. He also sees Xi less as a determined geopolitical foe than as a transactional business rival with whom he can negotiate to stabilize bilateral ties.
This judgment—that dealing with China can and should be nonideological and transactional—was also present in Trump’s first term in office. It even survived the COVID-19 pandemic, during which Trump, believing that Xi had exported the virus, empowered senior advisers to implement a hardline approach to China that included challenging the legitimacy of Chinese Communist Party rule and openly calling for political change. But Trump’s core views did not dictate U.S. policy as greatly then as they do now because he had less command over his own administration during his first term and was less dominant over the Republican party writ large.
Trump also has been chastened into a more realistic appreciation of Beijing’s power. China is the only country in the world that stared down Trump’s April 2025 tariff barrage and forced him to retreat. In 2026, Beijing used Washington’s fear of a fresh economic rupture to deter Trump from targeting major Chinese banks as part of his global efforts to squeeze Iran’s economy. Even as the president claims to be ushering in a new era of U.S. dominance, he has had to acknowledge China as a near-peer—a designation that his administration codified in its 2025 National Security Strategy. Similarly, he has touted the prospect of a “G-2” of the United States and China, making allies and partners worry that he is privileging Beijing’s sensitivities over their concerns.
Trump’s mishandling of China policy has opened space for a new strategy. In some discrete areas, the Trump administration continues to view China through a competitive lens. For example, it is reducing U.S. dependence on Chinese rare-earth exports through initiatives such as Project Vault, an effort to stockpile critical minerals, and pressuring countries to forswear participation in Chinese-led artificial intelligence initiatives. And even as Trump lambastes allies and partners, alleging that they exploit U.S. beneficence, Washington is deepening its security cooperation with treaty allies in Asia, including Australia and the Philippines, and stepping up its training of troops in Taiwan.
Yet on balance, the president has been more conciliatory than competitive toward China. And with his other foreign policy goals—dominating the Western Hemisphere, pushing for regime change in Iran, and ending the war in Ukraine—vying for priority, Trump has devoted far less attention to the world’s most consequential bilateral relationship than many observers had expected when he retook office. His various retreats on China have left the United States worse off: he has undermined Washington’s leverage over Beijing while causing U.S. allies and partners to question whether they should align with a country that prefers a veneer of stability with China to coordinated efforts to address Chinese assertiveness abroad and repression at home.
Because Trump’s China policy places him at odds with much of the foreign policy bureaucracy and the vast majority of lawmakers, including those within his own party, there will likely be pent-up pressure to resume intense strategic competition with China. Candidates seeking the 2028 presidential nomination, in particular, will be tempted to pledge to take a tougher approach to Beijing, potentially including calls to curtail high-level diplomatic exchanges between U.S. and Chinese officials or adopt more expansive measures to limit China’s technological progress. But attempting to restore or make minor updates to the status quo that existed before Trump’s second term—or to go even further in an effort to project ostensible strength—would be misguided.
Most obvious, the nearly decadelong U.S. effort to slow Chinese innovation through sanctions, tariffs, and export controls is foundering. In AI, for example, Chinese companies are finding workarounds to such efforts and advancing their capabilities alongside those of their U.S. peers. Any sustainable attempt to limit technology access also requires multilateral cooperation; as Washington’s ties with many of its closest allies and partners fray, questions are mounting about whether any coalition can thwart Beijing’s technological strides and would be willing to bear the costs of trying to do so.
At the same time, China is widening its lead in sectors such as electric vehicles, advanced robots, and life sciences. Attempting to construct a technological moat around the United States is unlikely to help it regain its competitiveness. Instead, Washington will need to strike a better balance between mitigating the security risks of Chinese investment and collaboration and securing commercial benefits from Chinese knowledge and industry. The United States could gain from using the same technology transfer practices that China employed against U.S. companies starting in the late 1970s, when Washington led in many cutting-edge technologies. In battery manufacturing, for instance, U.S. firms would benefit from acquiring know-how from their Chinese competitors, and both countries would gain if their laboratories pooled capabilities to accelerate the discovery of cures to diseases such as cancer.
The United States also may not be able to pick up where it left off before Trump retook office because even its well-wishers no longer can treat his foreign policy as an aberration. He won a second term after losing in 2020, and he did so more decisively than in 2016. Although U.S. allies and partners would welcome a more multilateralist administration after Trump, they will find it difficult to overlook the possibility that aggressive unilateralism will periodically shape Washington’s foreign policy. Merely eschewing Trump’s crude bullying will not be enough to rebuild trust; his successor must avoid treating allies and partners as instruments of strategic competition.
Paradoxically, though, Trump’s mishandling of China policy has opened the political space for a new, more durable strategy to emerge. Previously, proponents of sustained high-level diplomacy between the United States and China were often dismissed as shortsighted, Chinese investment in the United States was seen as a Trojan horse, and critiques of zero-sum conceptions of bilateral relations were considered naive. But Trump’s actions have shifted the Overton window of what is possible, creating room for a wider variety of ways to work productively with China.
Both Republican and Democratic members of Congress have gradually resumed bipartisan visits to China. Even Secretary of State Marco Rubio, one of the foremost hawks during his time in the Senate, said in September that it would be “outrageous” for top U.S. and Chinese officials to avoid each other. Respected voices in Washington, including former senior Biden administration officials, are calling for some joint ventures with Chinese companies to help U.S. companies develop. And there is a growing recognition in both Congress and the White House that China’s steady accrual of power necessitates a more focused approach to competition.
There is also public support for a new China policy. Although many policymakers frame relations with Beijing as a competition to be won, most Americans regard it as a reality to be managed. In a July 2025 survey by the Chicago Council on Global Affairs, 53 percent of Americans wanted the United States to pursue “friendly cooperation and engagement with China”; it was the first time since 2019 that a majority of respondents had expressed that view. Young people are driving this shift. A January 2026 Pew poll found that only 32 percent of Republicans and ten percent of Democrats between the ages of 18 and 49 called China an enemy, compared with 55 percent and 18 percent, respectively, of those age 50 and older. A March 2026 survey by the Searchlight Institute, a center-left think tank, found that 62 percent of respondents between 18 and 34 wanted the United States to “cooperate with China and improve U.S.-China relations.”
A better China policy will begin by abandoning hollow admonitions to “win” the strategic competition with Beijing. Although China faces numerous internal challenges, including slowing productivity, an aging population, and mounting government debt, it also has powerful advantages, two of which have become undeniable in Trump’s second term: China’s dominance of supply chains that provide vital commodities to Americans and its stranglehold on rare earths that the U.S. automotive industry and defense industrial base need to function.
The next president should neither understate nor exaggerate Beijing’s trajectory; rather, as we have previously argued, Washington should consider it “an enduring yet constrained competitor.” The inescapable reality is that neither country can deliver any knockout blows against the other without doing grievous damage to itself. Both countries will remain interdependent and have to operate with awareness of their vulnerabilities to each other.
If the world’s two foremost powers are fated to compete over the terms of coexistence, they must redouble efforts to avoid a catastrophic confrontation. Washington will have to build trust with rightfully wary allies and partners if it is to shore up deterrence in Asia. It will also have to continue signaling to Beijing a willingness to take reciprocal steps to reduce the risk of incidents that could spiral out of control, such as accidental air or naval collisions. Such signaling will become ever more essential as AI rapidly advances. Protecting the U.S. homeland against AI misuse and misalignment will require Washington to coordinate with Beijing on guardrails for frontier models and reject the argument that such efforts are inimical to securing a decisive strategic advantage over China. The need to work together on AI safety is already shaping U.S.-Chinese relations: at September’s summit between Trump and Xi, the two countries agreed to establish an incident alert system, and many members of Congress have publicly supported broader bilateral efforts with China to reduce the risks of AI-enabled security crises.
Reindustrialization will also be a major project for Trump’s successor. Progress on that front will be essential if the United States is to reduce its vulnerabilities to China and put itself on firmer footing to pursue a favorable coexistence. The next Congress and potential 2028 candidates could outline realistic plans for reducing dependence on China for active pharmaceutical ingredients, rare earths, and potential future chokepoints. They could also prioritize key industries upon which U.S. security depends, such as AI-powered robotics and shipbuilding, and explain how they would deploy funding and talent to those sectors. Elsewhere, with public support for the president’s tariffs sagging, his successor could narrow, place sunset provisions on—or, through executive order, rescind—protectionist policies toward China that lower productivity and increase the costs of inputs for American producers. Such efforts would be broadly politically popular at home and would better position the United States to work with allies and partners to push back on China’s unfair economic practices, including extensive subsidies, undervalued currency, and suppressed domestic demand.
Ultimately, the United States must remember that its external competitiveness will flow from its internal vigor. Leaders in Washington should accordingly embed policy positions on China in a hopeful vision: helping the United States realize its potential as a pluralistic, self-correcting democracy. Framing U.S.-Chinese relations around the United States’ capacity to steer its ties with China in productive directions is more likely to produce a resilient approach than casting Washington as a victim or reasserting a policy framework that no longer meets the moment.
Observers can and should debate specific policy positions vigorously. But another harsh swing in the United States’ approach to China would create more policy instability while also discounting the extent to which Beijing’s rise is transforming the geopolitical balance. Taking advantage of the political space that Trump has generated, however ineptly, the new Congress taking office after the 2026 midterms and presidential aspirants looking to run in 2028 will have an opportunity to present a steadier approach, articulating a clear focus on national renewal and a balanced posture toward Beijing that reflects the mood of the American public. Approaching China’s challenge with this sensibility would not only be more prescriptively sound; it would also be more politically sustainable.
