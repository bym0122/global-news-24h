SEOUL, South Korea — A Russian network of brokers sponsored by Pyongyang has taken North Koreans to work in Russia in exploitative conditions and in violation of U.N. sanctions, some at factories that turn out items Moscow uses in the Ukraine war , a rights group said.
The neighbors have greatly strengthened military and political ties since pledging renewed cooperation at a 2023 summit of their leaders, while a mutual defense pact is a cornerstone of a partnership treaty signed the following year.
In its report , the Hague-based group, Global Rights Compliance (GRC), said North Korea reaps funds from the operation, taking much of the wages earned by its workers who are sent to Russia and made to stay under threat of retribution.
The group cites open-source information on the operation run by a Russian businessman, facial recognition technology to identify North Korean officials, and evidence gathered by its members posing undercover as prospective employers.
“This investigation exposes a dangerous convergence between two states’ military ambitions,” said the group’s legal adviser, Elise Lauriot Prevost.
These were “North Korea’s use of overseas forced labor to finance its weapons program, and Russia’s growing reliance on labor to sustain its war economy,” she added.
Russia’s foreign ministry and the North Korean Embassy in Moscow did not respond to emails seeking comment.
The rights group said it contacted two Russians identified as key figures in the network, but only one replied, denying the accusation. It did not say whether the other responded.
Since 2024, North Korea has sent more than 10,000 troops to fight alongside Russians in the Ukraine war, as well as weapons to replenish Moscow’s dwindling armory while gaining valuable battlefield experience, South Korea’s intelligence agency has said.
To evade U.N. sanctions that ban member nations from hiring North Korean workers, the latter enter Russia on student visas in the guise of enrolling at colleges, GRC said.
Then they are contracted out to Russian employers, with brokers receiving a commission of up to 150,000 rubles ($1,860) for each, it added.
GRC investigators posed undercover as prospective employers and found the workers were hired at production sites, including a military rations factory.
The workers are forced to stay on the job through coercion and threats of retribution against them and their families.
One advertisement assured prospective employers that workers would not “run away,” the report said. Workers labor 12-hour days with two days off each month, it added, citing such ads.
After North Korea’s sixth and last nuclear test in 2017, a U.N. Security Council resolution put a blanket ban on the hiring of its workers overseas and mandated their return home.
Russia and China , two permanent members of the Security Council that voted for the ban, have since been accused by South Korea and the West of noncompliance with resolutions.
Such measures, dating back to 2006, banned the flow of goods, energy, weapons and workers with North Korea.
In previous years, Russia employed North Korean laborers in its far east while many North Koreans staffing restaurants in China sent home wages that were channeled to the state treasury, rights groups have said.
North Korea is directly involved in running the network supplying workers in Russia, with officials from its Moscow embassy identified as inspecting work sites for prospective employment, the GRC report said.
“These workers do not come cheap, yet little, if any, of the money paid for their labor reaches them,” said Dylan Carter, a GRC lead investigator.
“Instead, it lines the pockets of the DPRK state,” he added, referring to North Korea by a short version of its official name, the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea.

WASHINGTON (TNND) — The Federal Reserve is planning to cut down the number of its meetings devoted to moving its benchmark interest rate over the next two years in one of the most significant shifts yet under its new chairman.
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh announced the change after Wednesday’s meeting, where the central bank opted to keep rates steady despite mounting pressures on inflation. Beginning next fall, the Fed will dedicate one of its eight policy meetings to weigh broad-based questions about the economy instead of voting on interest rates. In 2028, there will be two meetings without a rate decision.
Warsh argued the longer gap between meetings would give officials more time to gather data to see how the economy is responding to previous decisions and allow them to make more informed judgements on how to move forward.
“Getting policy right involves assessing near-term developments at timely, regular and information-rich intervals,” Warsh said. “But it’s equally important we reserve time and space as an intellectually diverse group for strategic deliberations, for thinking anew about big questions, looking out over longer time horizons.”
Cutting down the number of rate-setting meetings is a significant shift for the Fed, which has run on its eight-meeting schedule since 1981. While Fed officials will still meet at the same frequency, they will have fewer planned opportunities to shift monetary policy.
“It serves a useful purpose to meet eight times a year. It&#x27;s arbitrary whether it&#x27;s six or eight, but it gives you an opportunity to actually have to make decisions, and that is do more than talk about something where you really have to figure out where things are and whether you want to stay the same,” said Thomas Hoenig, former president and CEO of the Kansas City Fed and distinguished senior fellow at the Mercatus Center. “By eliminating the meeting, you may decrease the likelihood that you have a discussion about where the economy is and whether you should actually take an action. I think that&#x27;s a loss, frankly.”
Depending on which meetings are changed on the calendar, the Fed could go nearly three months without a regularly scheduled opportunity to adjust interest rates, compared to the current roughly six-week interval.
It’s not yet clear what, if anything, will come out of the meetings that do not include a rate decision. Warsh also did not indicate which meeting will be the first to go.
“I do not anticipate making any policy decision, putting out a policy statement. But what might we come out with? It&#x27;s not obvious yet,” Warsh said.
The Fed’s rate-setting meetings are always closely watched by investors and economists but have been under particular scrutiny in recent years after inflation spiked to a high of 9.1% in 2022. While the pace of price increases has slowed significantly since then, inflation still remains well above the Fed’s 2% target.
It is not the first time the Fed has considered scaling back the number of rate-setting meetings , but it has never made the switch over concerns it could become less responsive to sudden changes in the economy. The Fed is required to meet at least four times a year, which Warsh said during his confirmation hearings was not enough.
Some economists have argued fewer rate decisions would give Fed officials more time to see meaningful changes to the economy and allow them to make more informed decisions. The Fed can also hold emergency meetings during times of sudden turmoil, which it has done in the past, most recently in 2020 during the onset of the coronavirus pandemic.

While the chair does not make interest rate decisions alone, the decisions on whether to meet or adjust rates could take on more importance.

Running a model on another architecture can require specialized work on kernels, memory use and distributed execution.
“Getting a frontier model to run on a TPU is one thing,” Kimball said. “Getting it to run with tuned kernels for attention, mixture-of-experts routing and long-context inference, is months of specialized work.”
The cost of that engineering, along with performance differences, would factor into whether TPUs offer a financial advantage over Nvidia GPUs.
OpenAI’s familiarity with Google’s technology could ease the work. Kimball noted that OpenAI’s chip team is led by an executive who helped build TPUs at Google. That familiarity could help, though it would not eliminate the need to optimize workloads for the new architecture.
Kimball expects the earliest economic benefits to emerge in inference, the computing required to generate AI responses. A purpose-built accelerator can improve the cost of serving workloads, but the result depends on throughput, latency, utilization, memory requirements and operating costs.
A TPU deployment could also provide another route to capacity as demand for AI computing grows. But it would not free OpenAI from the semiconductor industry’s supply constraints. GPUs and TPUs both depend on advanced manufacturing, packaging, high-bandwidth memory and data center power.
Adding a supplier gives OpenAI another potential source of hardware, rather than an escape from those bottlenecks. Kimball said the ability to negotiate with a credible alternative supplier could be valuable even if Nvidia remains OpenAI’s primary accelerator platform.
Oracle announced Tuesday that it plans to offer Google TPUs through Oracle Cloud Infrastructure, with deployments beginning in 2027. The agreement will make OCI the first hyperscale cloud outside Google to offer the accelerators.
“The next generation of AI will require a diverse range of compute,” said Amin Vahdat, Google’s senior vice president and chief technologist for AI and Infrastructure, in the announcement. “TPUs give customers a powerful, purpose-built option for demanding training and inference workloads.”
The deal reflects Google’s effort to expand TPU access beyond its own cloud. For customers, offering the chips through OCI could provide another accelerator option without requiring them to operate their own hardware.
A potential OpenAI purchase would extend that strategy to a company competing with Google in AI models and services, although the reported transaction remains unconfirmed.
Kimball does not see the development as a direct threat to Nvidia. He pointed to Nvidia’s software ecosystem, broad availability through cloud providers and hardware vendors, and roadmap serving customers from enterprises to sovereign AI programs.
“Demand continues to soar,” he said. “And it’s growing faster than any single supplier can support.”
Running several accelerator architectures can complicate infrastructure operations. Different programming environments and performance characteristics make workloads harder to move and tune across hardware.
Kimball said broader adoption will require orchestration and control-plane tools that automate deployment and optimization across architectures. Without them, the engineering overhead could erode the benefits of adding another chip supplier.
For OpenAI, the test will be whether TPUs deliver useful capacity and competitive workload economics after software optimization and operating costs are included. For infrastructure teams considering a similar strategy, hardware choice is only one part of the calculation.

The Pentagon told Congress that the cost of the US-Israel war on Iran as of October 16 had risen to at least $47.1bn.
Congressional officials shared the Pentagon’s cost estimate breakdown with The New York Times.
According to the Times and other US media, the Pentagon’s estimate covers the operational costs of the war and includes a projected cost of $29bn needed to replace expended munitions such as bombs, bullets and other military hardware that has been deployed in the war so far.
It also reveals that since the war started on February 28, the US Air Force has spent $7.6bn on fighter and bomber units, the US Navy has spent $1.5bn on two dozen warships and aircraft carriers and the US Army has spent $2.1bn to sustain its troops in the Middle East.
According to the US military’s Central Command, there are more than 50,000 American soldiers stationed in the Middle East, comprising personnel deployed to large, permanent bases and to smaller forward sites across the region.
Officials told the Times that the Pentagon has spent $6.1bn to replace damaged aircraft and munitions, $645m on transport and support for commandos in the region and $24.5m on “in-house medical support” for US service members.
A congressional official added that the US Transportation Command also spent $92.2m to move equipment and the US Cyber Command spent $14m on “cyber support operations”.
The Pentagon’s estimate, however, does not include how much Washington will need to spend to repair its military bases across the Middle East that were damaged in Iranian attacks and also does not take into account the cost of fuel to power ships and aircraft used in the war.
While the Pentagon has publicly acknowledged 18 US service member fatalities in the war, The Washington Post has cited six unnamed US officials stating the true death toll is at least 22. According to the official Pentagon Defense Casualty Analysis System (DCAS) database, 865 military personnel have been wounded in the war with Iran.
John Phillips, a British safety, security and risk adviser and a former military chief instructor, told Al Jazeera that the Pentagon’s $47.1bn estimate should be treated as a running total and not the total cost of the war.
“If the war continues without a durable outcome, the US risks paying repeatedly to sustain military pressure while simultaneously trying to rebuild the capability it has consumed,” he said.
He said that replacing advanced weapons also requires industrial capacity and time and that money and production resources committed to this conflict may also compete with other defence priorities.
“What concerns me most is the possibility of an open-ended commitment: continuing to spend heavily without a clear political end state or a settlement that delivers lasting security,” he said.
Currently, the US must weigh three costs, Phillips said: the money spent, the military capability consumed and the economic disruption generated.
“A tactical victory is not necessarily a strategic victory. If the conflict leaves US stockpiles depleted, energy markets unstable and regional security unresolved, the question is not simply how much the war will cost but whether its lasting benefits justify the price,” he said.

What does the rising cost of the war mean for the midterm elections?
The US-Israel war on Iran has affected the US economy through direct government spending as well as rising energy and fuel costs.
On Wednesday, the average price of a gallon of diesel in the US hit $6.35, compared with $3.75 last year, according to the American Automobile Association.
Higher fuel costs are also driving up the cost of food – with shortages of oil and gas affecting nearly every stage of the food supply chain.
Meanwhile, the country’s national debt has surpassed a record $40 trillion, two years before expected.
As a result, the war has become deeply unpopular in the US and Trump’s approval ratings have slumped to just 33 percent – the lowest they have been at any time during his two terms as president.

It tends to reinforce rather than degrade regime control, strengthen the hand of hard-liners over any remaining moderates, and provide a ready-made excuse for collective suffering that might otherwise be blamed on the country’s leaders.
Such a conflict will, by its nature, defy U.S. and Israeli hopes for a decisive blow. After the initial kinetic period, the hostilities will likely continue whether or not there is active shooting, forcing every affected actor to prepare as if violence could return at any moment. Talk of victory or defeat—whether for U.S. domestic political consumption or, worse, for the Pentagon's actual war planning—has little meaning in a conflict that Iranians rightly expect to continue for many years to come. America’s regional allies that have to live in this environment can see this clearly and want none of it. That constant risk of a sudden eruption would deny the Gulf states the kind of stability and security they desperately need to get their economic projects back on track. And it would leave the door open to flare-ups elsewhere, such as the Houthi sweep down the Red Sea coast and seizure of the Bab el-Mandeb chokepoint in September.
The suggestion to launch a renewed, intense military campaign represents a tacit recognition of U.S. failure. No amount of happy talk about the increased volume of oil flowing through the Strait of Hormuz and the mounting economic costs of the blockade on Iran can mask the new strategic reality. Iran has proved that it retains the capacity to close the strait when it wishes, and the Houthi advances in the Red Sea and effective targeting of Saudi Arabia have expanded Iran’s range of options. A diplomatic solution has been rendered largely impossible by the Trump administration’s constantly changing positions and incoherent messaging, which give Iran no reason to even consider a deal. From Tehran’s vantage point, any agreement would likely be broken before the ink was dry. Trump’s signature, as Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney has quipped , is written in pencil.
But if Iran can live with the state of potential war and episodic eruptions of violence, the Arab Gulf states cannot. Bahrain, Kuwait, and Qatar have no alternative shipping routes other than the Strait of Hormuz. Saudi Arabia, which for a time thought it could adapt by shifting its oil exports to the Red Sea, learned the hard way that it could not count on that option in the rapidly mutating conflict. The Houthi seizure of Yemen’s Red Sea coast and control over the Bab el-Mandeb and an Iraqi militia drone strike temporarily disabling the East-West pipeline put an end to that fantasy.
The escalating Houthi attacks on Saudi airports, oil facilities, and other targets, along with the Saudi inability to mount an effective military challenge to the new Houthi positions, have caught Riyadh in an untenable bind. The United States rather shockingly declined to come Riyadh’s assistance against the Houthis. That may be for the best. The large-scale international intervention against the Houthis proposed by the Saudi ambassador to the United States this week would simply pull the world deeper into an unwinnable war scape. Nor is the Mecca Pact between Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan going to ride to a military rescue in Yemen. The three countries may have ratified the defense pact and floated intervention possibilities, but neither country is likely to provide the ground troops that would be needed.

But there could be a silver lining in this parade of failure. With no plausible military path to victory in Yemen or over Iran, Saudi Arabia has little stomach for renewed war and strong incentives to find a diplomatic way out of its predicament. The Saudis desperately need the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab el-Mandeb reopened in a predictable and regulated way. They need an extended period of calm to reassure investors and tourists essential to economic transformation plans. That’s probably why a high-level Mecca Pact delegation visited Tehran this week and why other Gulf states have been urgently looking for a diplomatic path away from war. As Oman’s foreign minister recently noted , there is no security order in the Gulf without Iran and no alternative to a working relationship. That view seems increasingly prevalent in the Gulf—outside of Abu Dhabi—even as Washington prefers to dive deeper into its unwinnable war.
The current Iranian leadership does not think about this in terms of the resumption of war. It considers the war to be ongoing. While it would have little faith in any U.S. promises, it is conceivable that it might view Saudi-led outreach more positively, as a way to lock in its gains from the war and create a buffer against America’s more extreme options. The damage inflicted on Iran’s economy and infrastructure is real and will require massive rebuilding. There’s precedent. Following the conclusion of the horrifically destructive eight-year Iran-Iraq War and the death of Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini in the late 1980s, the new leadership of Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani and Ali Khamenei chose to ratchet down revolutionary rhetoric and militant adventurism and create a period of calm to allow desperately needed economic reconstruction. Perhaps the new Revolutionary Guard-led Iran would do the same.
A de-escalatory deal between Iran and the Saudi-led bloc would pose a challenge to Israel and its allies in the region, primarily the United Arab Emirates, as well as to U.S. conceits about determining the terms of regional order. Netanyahu has been using apocalyptic language about the urgency of finishing the job. Indeed, a U.S.-Israeli attack might be motivated less by fear of Iranian escalation or nuclear progress than by fear of a new regional order consolidating in which Iran has reconciled with a Saudi coalition incorporating most of the major powers in the broadly defined region.
The Trump administration no doubt sees itself in an intractable situation from which it can still escalate to win. That is an illusion. Iran is once again perfectly capable of seeing the blow coming. It will not be taken by surprise and has already signaled its intentions to respond. The regime has strong grounds to believe that it (if not the Iranian people) will survive and prosper even under another round of intense military attack. The possible strike would simply accelerate the escalatory dynamics and reinforce emergent Arab convictions that the United States has become a destabilizing force and an unreliable partner that cannot be counted on to provide security or order. When the smoke once again clears, in Iran and in the countries that bore the costs of its retaliation, the renewed campaign may well prove to have been the final blow not to the Iranian regime but to U.S. leadership in the region.
