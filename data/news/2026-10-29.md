SEOUL, South Korea — A Russian network of brokers sponsored by Pyongyang has taken North Koreans to work in Russia in exploitative conditions and in violation of U.N. sanctions, some at factories that turn out items Moscow uses in the Ukraine war , a rights group said.
The neighbors have greatly strengthened military and political ties since pledging renewed cooperation at a 2023 summit of their leaders, while a mutual defense pact is a cornerstone of a partnership treaty signed the following year.
In its report , the Hague-based group, Global Rights Compliance (GRC), said North Korea reaps funds from the operation, taking much of the wages earned by its workers who are sent to Russia and made to stay under threat of retribution.
The group cites open-source information on the operation run by a Russian businessman, facial recognition technology to identify North Korean officials, and evidence gathered by its members posing undercover as prospective employers.
“This investigation exposes a dangerous convergence between two states’ military ambitions,” said the group’s legal adviser, Elise Lauriot Prevost.
These were “North Korea’s use of overseas forced labor to finance its weapons program, and Russia’s growing reliance on labor to sustain its war economy,” she added.
Russia’s foreign ministry and the North Korean Embassy in Moscow did not respond to emails seeking comment.
The rights group said it contacted two Russians identified as key figures in the network, but only one replied, denying the accusation. It did not say whether the other responded.
Since 2024, North Korea has sent more than 10,000 troops to fight alongside Russians in the Ukraine war, as well as weapons to replenish Moscow’s dwindling armory while gaining valuable battlefield experience, South Korea’s intelligence agency has said.
To evade U.N. sanctions that ban member nations from hiring North Korean workers, the latter enter Russia on student visas in the guise of enrolling at colleges, GRC said.
Then they are contracted out to Russian employers, with brokers receiving a commission of up to 150,000 rubles ($1,860) for each, it added.
GRC investigators posed undercover as prospective employers and found the workers were hired at production sites, including a military rations factory.
The workers are forced to stay on the job through coercion and threats of retribution against them and their families.
One advertisement assured prospective employers that workers would not “run away,” the report said. Workers labor 12-hour days with two days off each month, it added, citing such ads.
After North Korea’s sixth and last nuclear test in 2017, a U.N. Security Council resolution put a blanket ban on the hiring of its workers overseas and mandated their return home.
Russia and China , two permanent members of the Security Council that voted for the ban, have since been accused by South Korea and the West of noncompliance with resolutions.
Such measures, dating back to 2006, banned the flow of goods, energy, weapons and workers with North Korea.
In previous years, Russia employed North Korean laborers in its far east while many North Koreans staffing restaurants in China sent home wages that were channeled to the state treasury, rights groups have said.
North Korea is directly involved in running the network supplying workers in Russia, with officials from its Moscow embassy identified as inspecting work sites for prospective employment, the GRC report said.
“These workers do not come cheap, yet little, if any, of the money paid for their labor reaches them,” said Dylan Carter, a GRC lead investigator.
“Instead, it lines the pockets of the DPRK state,” he added, referring to North Korea by a short version of its official name, the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea.

U.S. natural gas production is on track to close a second consecutive year with record-high natural gas output as producers respond to demand signs both at home and overseas.
Soaring demand for powering data centers in the United States and rising liquefied natural gas exports amid expanding liquefaction capacity, as well as surging global demand for spot LNG cargoes in the absence of Qatar’s gas due to the Iran war, are pushing American natural gas production higher this year.
Output is on track to beat in 2026 the record set in 2025 as more associated gas is being produced in the Permian basin along with oil, and operators boost gas output in the Haynesville basin, which is close to LNG export terminals and major domestic industrial gas users, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) says .

The&#xA0; Federal Reserve on Wednesday held its benchmark interest rate steady amid elevated inflation as policymakers opted to wait for additional economic data after hiking rates in September.
Fed policymakers voted 10-2 to leave the federal funds rate at its target range of 3.75% to 4%. The move follows a 25-basis-point rate hike in September, which was the central bank&apos;s first interest rate increase since July 2023.
The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), the central bank&apos;s panel responsible for monetary policy moves, noted that economic activity "is expanding at a strong pace" despite uncertainty that is in part due to geopolitical developments, with strong productivity growth and capital investment, while job growth has "kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has changed little."

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh said the central bank remains focused on returning inflation to the 2% target. (Al Drago/Bloomberg via Getty Images)
There were two dissenters from the FOMC decision to leave interest rates unchanged: Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack and Fed Governor Lisa Cook each voted in favor of hiking interest rates by 25 basis points.
Federal Reserve Chair&#xA0; Kevin Warsh said that policymakers go through a process of "interrogating reality" as they assess economic conditions at each FOMC meeting, adding that the process has been complicated by high levels of investment, a steady labor market and elevated inflation.
Warsh noted that the labor market conditions are "quite positive" on that side of the Fed&apos;s dual mandate, which is to promote full employment, but added that on the price stability side of the mandate "Americans, of course, are still dealing with inflation that has been running above the committee&apos;s objective for close to six years. It&apos;s our responsibility to get inflation down."
He said that since the Fed hiked rates by 25 basis points in September, policymakers have seen that financial and credit conditions have tightened for some businesses and households "much as we would expect."&#xA0;
Warsh added that market participants are "becoming somewhat more discerning in their capital allocations. And while overall financial conditions are tighter, I&apos;m unwilling and unable to call them tight. But broadly, these developments go in the direction of achieving a longer, more durable period of economic growth and price stability."
FOX Business White House correspondent Edward Lawrence reports on the Federal Reserves decision as the central bank leaves rates unchanged.
The Fed chair also offered an update on the FOMC&apos;s review of its meeting schedule, saying that starting next year the FOMC will hold still hold eight meetings per year, but one of those meetings in 2027 "will focus exclusively on broad monetary policy strategy, with neither a vote on the federal funds rate nor a policy statement anticipated. The rest will be regular FOMC policy-setting meetings."
Warsh added that in 2028 he expects the FOMC will have two meetings dedicated to strategy discussions, with six related to policy decisions.

KYIV, Ukraine — Like many tech entrepreneurs in Ukraine, Yaroslav Azhnyuk turned to developing drone warfare after Russia's 2022 invasion.
Drones have transformed the way wars are fought. Azhnyuk says artificial intelligence is making drones faster and more capable.
"When the other side has orders of magnitudes higher capabilities, it gives you an asymmetric advantage," he says. "It can make you 10,000 times more effective."
Azhnyuk runs the Ukraine-based defense firms The Fourth Law and Odd Systems. They are among the top companies producing AI-powered drones in Ukraine. Dozens of Ukrainian military units already use their autonomous drone, called the Lupynis-10-TFL-1.
Russia is also deploying AI-enabled drones against Ukraine. AI warfare is part of a high-tech arms race that has many worried, including Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He has warned that AI could lead decisions on the battlefield as soon as next year.
Azhnyuk told NPR that he acknowledges the concern over destructive weapons but says it's not specific to AI.
"It has been with humanity since the invention of fire or steel," he says. "Any kind of tool can be used for good or for bad. And in the end, it's the human that decides how to use that tool."
Azhnyuk is 37, a gregarious computer scientist who hails from a family of scholars and scientists. NPR meets him in a conference room in Kyiv; for security reasons he asked us not to reveal much else about the location.
He worked for years in Silicon Valley and started several companies, including Petcube , which makes smartphone-powered cameras that use artificial intelligence to watch and even entertain pets while they're alone.
"These cameras allow pet parents to watch pets when they're not at home," he says, "and fling treats to them and shine a laser toy for them. We sold a million of these devices that are quite popular in the U.S."
After Russia launched its full-scale war, Azhnyuk, by then living in Ukraine, pivoted to the defense space.
"I was like, OK, I've got to move on from Petcube and do something to protect my country," he says. "And so now we're making not cameras that fling treats but cameras that fling explosives at people who came to occupy our land."
The cameras he's talking about are part of an image recognition system incorporated into first-person-view or FPV drones.
"We are leaders in autonomous drones and thermal cameras," he says.
A Lupynis-10-TFL-1 drone is seen in the air at a test site in central Ukraine.
The autonomous Lupynis TFL-1 drone combines a small, affordable FPV drone with an autonomous terminal guidance unit. That means the drone has an onboard computer and AI technology that takes control during the last 500 meters (or 0.3 miles) of the flight.
"But it's very different from, say, the kind of AI that sits on those data centers that power Claude," he says. "Or even the kind of AI that does self-driving for your Tesla."
Azhnyuk says this AI technology uses simple tools to recognize images and make smart software "which then allows you either to navigate autonomously without a human or to choose a target autonomously or to fly toward the target autonomously."
The Fourth Law's autonomous drone is named after the 20th century Ukrainian poet and political activist Anatoliy Lupynis, who endured decades of Soviet imprisonment and psychiatric torture. On the battlefield, the drone would carry an explosive to hit the car. The test drone doesn't have one.

If Ukraine loses, he says, "there's no way for us to argue about the ethics of AI."
