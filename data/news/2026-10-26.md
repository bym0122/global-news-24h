Although past events can't guarantee short-term directional moves for Wall Street's major stock indexes, history has a knack for foreshadowing the future.
For example, the stock market entered 2026 at its second-priciest valuation in history. A stock market crash is possible, but perspective is what's important
Based on several historically accurate indicators, the odds of a stock market crash and/or significant decline do appear to be above average. But this doesn't guarantee that equities will crash.
What's even more important than these dire historical outlooks in the wake of a roughly 700-point single-session decline in the Nasdaq Composite is maintaining perspective.
On the one hand, stock market corrections, bear markets, and even crashes are a normal part of the investing cycle. We may not enjoy red arrows in our portfolios, but no amount of fiscal or monetary policy maneuvering can prevent downturns from happening every now and then.
However, downturns share an important trait: they resolve quickly.
Since the start of the Great Depression in September 1929, data from Bespoke Investment Group show that the average S&P 500 bear market (i.e., a 20% or greater decline) reached its trough after 286 calendar days, or roughly 9.5 months.
Conversely, the typical bull market endured for 1,023 calendar days, or approximately 3.6 times as long.
Statistically, it pays to be a long-term optimist on Wall Street. When things look their direst is typically when opportunity presents itself for investors.

It&#x27;s no secret the S&P 500 ( ^GSPC +0.70% ) has enjoyed an unprecedented rally in recent years, fueled mostly by enthusiasm surrounding artificial intelligence (AI) and investments in technology infrastructure. Ironically enough, it&#x27;s times like these that usually have contrarian investors like Warren Buffett sitting on the sidelines instead of following the crowd.
Buffett&#x27;s favorite valuation tool suggests his concerns may be justified. With stocks now trading well above levels Buffett views as extreme, history offers some important clues about what could happen next.
In an article published by Fortune in December 2001, Buffett explained how investors could assess whether the overall stock market was reasonably valued . He described the relationship between total U.S. stock market capitalization and the country&#x27;s gross national product (GNP) as "probably the best single measure of where valuations stand at any given moment."
Imagine the combined market capitalization of every publicly traded American was $200 trillion while the U.S. economy produced $100 trillion in annual output. Dividing those figures generates a Buffett indicator of 200%. In other words, investors are valuing the stock market at twice the country&#x27;s annual economic output.
There is one important distinction to note when calculating the Buffett indicator. Buffett originally referred to gross national product (GNP), which measures economic output attributable to U.S. residents including income earned through overseas activity. However, a more common way of calculating the Buffett indicator substitutes GNP with gross domestic product (GDP). GDP measures economic activity occurring within U.S. borders, regardless of who produces it.
According to GuruFocus, the current U.S. market-cap-to-GDP ratio hovers around 233%. Buffett says that when this indicator approaches 200% investors are "playing with fire." While the current excess in the market doesn&#x27;t guarantee a crash is imminent, it does suggest investors are paying historically high prices for stocks.
History offers a stark warning when valuations become expensive
Two periods are particularly relevant today&#x27;s bull market: the dot-com bubble and the market downturn between 2021 and 2022. During the late 1990s, investors were captivated by the internet. Although they were correct about the internet&#x27;s revolutionary potential, many technology businesses at the time commanded unusually high valuations that proved unsustainable given the lack of revenue and profit from these companies.
Prior to the dot-com bubble bursting, the Buffett indicator peaked around 143%. Between March 2000 and October 2002, the S&P 500 declined approximately 49% while the Nasdaq Composite ( ^IXIC +0.82% ) plunged nearly 78%. What&#x27;s even more devastating is that the S&P 500 did not recover its pre-dot-com high until June 2007.
Something similar happened nearly two decades later. In December 2021, the Buffett indicator was sitting just about Buffett&#x27;s 200% warning sign. This was reflective of elevated stock valuations during the pandemic-era rally.
However, by mid-2022 inflation had started to surge, causing the Federal Reserve to aggressively raise interest rates. Naturally, investors began reassessing growth stocks. Between January and October 2022, the S&P 500 declined as much as 25%.
These episodes illustrate how stretched valuations often precede meaningful corrections in the market . In addition, the timing and severity of those declines are impossible to predict using the Buffett indicator in isolation.
The Buffett indicator&#x27;s current reading deserves attention, but I wouldn&#x27;t interpret it as absolute proof that a crash is coming.
Today&#x27;s market is considerably different from the dot-com era. AI leaders such as Nvidia , Microsoft , Alphabet , Amazon , and Meta Platforms generate substantial profits because their businesses are supported by durable revenue streams rather than speculative ideas.
Meanwhile, many American companies now generate significant revenue internationally. Since the Buffett indicator compares worldwide market capitalizations with U.S. GDP, globalization can make the ratio appear higher relative to prior decades.
Nevertheless, overvalued stocks will always be vulnerable when expectations become unrealistic. If AI infrastructure spending slows, interest rates continue rising, or earnings miss expectations, investors will likely reassess the premiums they&#x27;re currently paying.
This is why the smartest approach is to focus on high-quality companies with competitive advantages, strong balance sheets, consistent cash flow, and diversified business models.
Ultimately, the Buffett indicator isn&#x27;t a crystal ball. Could a tough stance toward China solve Europeâs own problems?
By Xiong Rui and Wang Shuo Published: Oct 26, 2026 08:42 PM
Recently, as the EU contemplates expanding restrictions on Chinese imports, "Europe getting tough on China" has become a headline pushed by several Western media outlets, with some even urging Washington to follow Europe's steps.
For Europe, a tough stance toward China reflects a deep-seated anxiety over its shifting relative strength and growing worries about its domestic and international environment.
Currently, Europe is locked in structural economic headwinds. Although its economy gradually recovered following the sovereign debt crisis, deep-rooted issues like a lack of growth momentum have proven difficult to resolve. High energy costs, underinvestment and market fragmentation remain intertwined.
As Chinese firms gain ground in Europe's traditional strongholds and the US leverages policies to draw global capital, Europe finds itself caught in a double squeeze: terrified of its domestic industries losing their competitive edge, while fearful of capital, factories and jobs fleeing overseas at the same time.
Solving these structural dilemmas demands sustained investment, the dismantling of entrenched interest barriers and the delicate reconciliation of internal divisions. By contrast, scapegoating its troubles on a "China shock" requires far less political capital than pushing through painful domestic reforms. This largely explains why some European figures are so eager to point the finger at competitors for "not playing by the rules," while failing to reflect on their own problems.
Europe faces the dual pressure of status anxiety and security reliance. Certain European leaders worry that a relative decline in economic clout will render Europe increasingly powerless in major-power geopolitical competition.
In the calculations of some European strategists, taking a hard line on China appears to offer a catch-all solution: Domestically, framing it as safeguarding economic security addresses protectionist calls and secures support from voters and special interest groups; externally, aligning on China policy creates common ground with Washington, demonstrating to the US that Europe still holds strategic value in exchange for security guarantees.
Yet, the fatal flaw in this strategy is that a temporary alignment between Washington and Brussels on Beijing cannot paper over their divergences regarding tariffs, industrial policy and defense burden-sharing. Even if their direction of "de-risking" from China aligns on paper, endless disputes remain over who builds new capacity, who captures market share and who sets the global rules.
Furthermore, Washington has its own geopolitical calculus when managing ties with Beijing. While Europe escalates its pressure on China, the US quietly retains room for engagement and deal-making. If Europe blindly sacrifices its cooperation with China merely to prove its loyalty, it is unlikely to secure the firm security commitments it craves in return.
Taking a tough stance on China will not solve Europe's problems; it risks creating far more. Evading domestic reform by building external walls will fail to remedy Europe's competitiveness deficit. Squeezing the space for cooperation with China risks depriving European businesses of crucial market access and supply chain synergies, while piling on higher production costs and green transition burdens.
The starting point for Europe's China policy ought to be firmly grounded in its own long-term interests. While protecting industrial interests is entirely legitimate, trade protectionism must be rooted in facts and international rules. Fair market competition never guarantees that any single player will win forever.
Europe's own history demonstrates that enduring peace cannot exist without mutual respect for interests, nor without dedicated efforts to translate shared interests into reality. This historic lesson should not be tossed aside when China is involved, nor should the vast common interests between Beijing and Brussels be selectively ignored in a bid to pander to Washington. For the transatlantic relationship, casting China as a common adversary may buy a fleeting moment of alignment, but it will never foster true, long-term cohesion.
If Europe wishes to command respect and achieve genuine strategic autonomy, it must rely on its own concrete actions.

Mortgage rates tend to move with the 10-year Treasury yield, which serves as a benchmark because mortgages are typically paid off or refinanced well before their 30-year term. And there are reasons to think those longer-term borrowing costs could remain elevated.
One is persistently high inflation. When inflation remains elevated, investors generally demand higher yields on long-term bonds to compensate for the declining purchasing power of the money they&#x27;ll eventually be repaid. Inflation, as measured by the Fed&#x27;s preferred PCE price index, hasn&#x27;t been below the central bank&#x27;s 2% target since February 2021 .

Banks have become more reluctant to hold mortgage-backed securities since the 2022-23 rate spike produced steep losses on bond portfolios, says Tom Graff, chief investment officer at wealth management firm Facet.
