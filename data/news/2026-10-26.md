These two factors hurt corporate earnings, and earnings drive stock prices over the long term.

Simply put, he would buy stocks aggressively during downturns in the broader market, because he knew the upward trend would always prevail over the long term.
Buffett also consistently reminded investors that stocks aren&#x27;t just financial instruments that move up and down -- they represent real businesses with real fundamentals. A person wouldn&#x27;t sell their house on a whim just because its value dropped during a given year, so selling a stock for the same reason makes very little sense. As long as the underlying business is healthy, a decline in stock price should be used as an opportunity to buy more.

Inflation Is the Big Reason Rates Aren't Coming Down
The September inflation report from the Bureau of Labor Statistics explains a lot. Consumer prices rose 0.6% in a single month and 3.7% over the past year. That's up from 3.4% in August, and it's far above the Fed's 2% target.

After years of record-breaking growth, major market indexes have stagnated. The S&P 500 ( ^GSPC -0.52% ) and the Dow Jones Industrial Average ( ^DJI -1.23% ) have each dipped by around 2% from their all-time highs, while the Nasdaq Composite ( ^IXIC -0.21% ) has slipped by close to 6%.

Nearly 46% of investors believe stock prices will fall over the next six months, according to the latest weekly survey from the American Association of Individual Investors, while 37% are bullish about the future, and the remaining 17% expect little change.
While it&#x27;s impossible to predict how stocks will perform in the coming months, the market is sending a warning signal that has only been seen during the dot-com bubble. Here&#x27;s how investors can prepare right now.
In the late 1990s, tech stocks were booming as excitement about the internet reached fever pitch. Many tech companies saw record-breaking initial public offerings (IPOs), reaching valuations that were astronomical for the time. Between 1995 and 1999 alone, the S&P 500 soared by close to 200%.
Another metric that was reaching record highs, though, was the S&P 500 Shiller cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio. This ratio measures the S&P 500&#x27;s long-term valuation, with higher figures suggesting that the index is overvalued.
Historically, higher ratios have been followed by a drop in stock prices, and this metric has predicted market crashes such as the 1929 Great Depression. In early 1999, the ratio surpassed 40 for the first time in history as the dot-com bubble grew. Just over a year later, in March 2000, the bear market officially began.
Now the CAPE ratio is elevated again. The metric surpassed 40 in May of this year, where it&#x27;s remained ever since. This is only the second time in history that it&#x27;s stayed this high for months at a time.
To be clear, this doesn&#x27;t necessarily mean we&#x27;re headed toward a dot-com-style crash. This metric provides a snapshot of the market&#x27;s valuation over time, but it can&#x27;t predict when the next bear market will begin, how severe it might be, or how long it might last.
That said, it does suggest that stocks are incredibly richly valued, making this the priciest market in decades.
If a recession or stock market crash is coming, the best move investors can make is to buy quality stocks and hold them for the long term.
Companies with weak foundations will have a harder time rebounding from downturns, and some may not recover at all. Countless tech stocks soared to record valuations in the 1990s, for example, only to go bankrupt just a few years later when the bubble popped. The tech stocks that survived were those with healthy finances, a durable competitive advantage, and capable leadership teams.
Even healthy companies can experience severe short-term volatility, which is why a long-term outlook is key. Microsoft , for example, plunged by 60% during the dot-com bear market. Apple lost more than 51% of its value in a single trading day in 2000. And Amazon sank by a staggering 94% between 1999 and 2001.
Now, all of these stocks are industry-leading giants, and the investors who reaped the greatest rewards were those who held on and continued investing even during the bleakest moments.
No two bear markets are identical, but even if history is repeating itself and we&#x27;re staring down another market crash, the smartest investors aren&#x27;t panicking. Rather, they&#x27;re loading up on healthy stocks with robust fundamentals , and they&#x27;re giving those investments plenty of time to generate life-changing wealth.

Oil Prices Rise Despite Middle East Export Recovery
Middle Eastern oil exports are recovering, with Hormuz flows reaching 11.7 million bpd in October, approaching pre-war levels despite continued disruptions.
Escalating tanker attacks and record freight costs threaten supply stability, keeping oil prices elevated despite improving export volumes.
Global oil markets remain vulnerable, as costly shipping workarounds, depleted inventories, and persistent geopolitical risks prevent a return to normality.
Reports about recovering oil flows in the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea have dominated headlines over the past couple of weeks. Yet oil prices have only moved higher, suggesting barrel numbers alone are not enough to convince the world everything’s back to normal. It isn’t.
According to the latest Kpler numbers, October oil exports out of the Middle East in total are in fact higher than they were before the U.S. and Israel launched their first strikes on Iran at the end of February. Flows out of the Strait of Hormuz alone have averaged 11.7 million barrels daily this month, Kpler estimated, up from 9.8 million barrels daily in September, and close to the pre-war average of 14.5 million barrels daily in February, Reuters’ Ron Bousso reported .
However, these numbers are the product of a lot of effort, expenditure, and risk—because the reports of rebounding oil exports out of the Persian Gulf are not the only news out of the region. The other big development there is Iran’s intensification of strikes on tankers. According to data from the UK Maritime Trade Operations and Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps statements, at least 25 ships were hit in Hormuz in the first 20 days of October. Yet, outlets tracking these incidents note that many do not report if they get attacked, for various reasons, which suggests the number could be higher—as high as 40-50 vessels. Most of these are tankers. Related: Middle East Physical Crude Slides While Brent Hits Five-Month High
Meanwhile, Kpler itself reported a sharp drop in tanker traffic via Hormuz this month as a result of the strikes. “Crude crossing the strait fell 27% from a wartime high the week before, to at least 10.1 million barrels a day,” two Kpler analysts reported in early October, as the firm counted only seven commodity carriers traversing the strait on October 6th. This was the lowest figure since late July, the analysts said.
Further, Reuters earlier this week reported  at least 18 tanker attacks over just the last two weeks, based on data from Kpler. The firm reported earlier this week that the daily average flow of oil out of the Persian Gulf had shrunk to 8.5 million barrels, from “more than 20 million barrels daily” for two weeks ago. This is quite a sharp and deep drop in export rates.
This sort of conflicting data from the same sources does not really contribute to a sense of security about oil supply from the Middle East, especially when the reports about strikes on tankers continue at a steady trickle. Meanwhile, a lot of the oil coming out of the Persian Gulf is doing so via ship-to-ship transfers that have pushed freight rates to record highs and kept them there. This has become such a serious problem that President Trump was said to be considering lifting sanctions on some tankers used to transport Russian oil to alleviate a shortage of oil carriers due to the expansion in STS off the coast of Oman.
That will not help, however. Because the supply of so-called shadow fleet tankers is also limited—because they are busy carrying that sanctioned oil. “Sanctions relief does not equal immediately usable mainstream tanker capacity,” Rahul Kapoor, global head of shipping analytics and research for S&P Global Energy, said as quoted by Bloomberg earlier this week. “The number of vessels that could realistically migrate back into mainstream trading would likely be materially smaller than the overall sanctioned fleet.”
With all these factors at play, and with the chance of a peace deal between the United States and Iran as remote today as it was three months ago, it is hardly a wonder that prices are moving higher—at least on the futures market. In the physical market, prices are actually down, Bloomberg reported this week. The reasons: that notorious rebound in Hormuz outflows, despite the tanker attacks, and a slight dip in tanker rates, which is a very welcome development.
“There’s still plenty of headline risk at play. Oil stocks are quite low and it’s difficult to see them rebuilding given current dynamics,” a senior commodity analyst at Marex told Bloomberg. Ryan Fitzmaurice added that there was still hope for lower heating demand this winter, if El Niño lives up to expectations. Lower heating demand, in turn, would bring some relief to prices. Betting on the weather, however, is not the soundest strategy, which is why chances are prices will remain elevated until at least the end of the year.
What the latest developments in the Middle East have shown is that there is always a workaround, even for a severe problem such as the closure of Hormuz—but these workarounds come at a price and are not a replacement for business as usual. One way or another, the world is adapting to this new situation in energy security. It has no other choice, really.
