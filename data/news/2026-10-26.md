US paying most for Hormuz oil shock with world’s largest fuel bill
War has added $468bn to the world’s fuel bill. The biggest oil producer is paying the most.
The US has faced a higher cost than any other country from the global Hormuz oil shock, with the crisis adding $160 billion to its fuel bill &#8211; around a third of the global total. Since the US and Israeli attacks on Iran began at the end of February, the world has paid an extra $468 billion for diesel and gasoline, equivalent to around $60 per person globally. Despite being the world’s largest oil producer, the US has faced an additional cost of $471 per person, the highest of any country.
This is because of America’s exceptional oil consumption due to high reliance on inefficient, oil-burning private vehicles and a slow transition away from petrol and diesel &#8211; leaving households, truckers and farmers particularly exposed to global fuel market shocks.
Other economies with high ownership and use of private cars, such as Canada and Australia, have also faced high costs per person.
American consumers have borne the brunt at the pump as the typical gasoline car driver has paid $440 more &#8211; assuming average mileage and fuel consumption &#8211; since the crisis began. US drivers that have managed to make the switch to EVs are now reaping the benefits, experiencing only an increase of $17 in charging costs since the beginning of March.
This is based on CREA analysis of gasoline and diesel prices and consumption in 169 countries, comparing the prices in January–February, before the disruption of shipments through the Strait of Hormuz, to realized prices at the pump from the beginning of March to the present.
At the national level, the biggest portion of the bill has landed on the US at $160 billion, a third of the total. China follows with $54 billion and Canada with $17 billion. Diesel is typically up by a third, with prices rising in 149 of 169 countries. Petrol is up by more than a fifth, rising in 148 of 170.
Fuel prices have risen for two reasons: increased price of crude oil, and a shortage of refining capacity. Refining capacity has shrunk as the disruptions to Hormuz traffic and Iran’s strikes on refineries have shut Gulf refining capacity off the market, and Ukraine’s counterattacks on Russia have affected refining capacity in the country.

The European natural gas price is now at its highest levels since the autumn of 2022, when Europe faced the first winter without most of the Russian pipeline gas supply. The current price is also equivalent to more than $30 per million British thermal units (MMBtu).
The TTF price rallied by 7% last week as several EU countries began withdrawing gas from storage amid lower temperatures.
EU storage sites were only 74.62% full as of October 25, per data from Gas Infrastructure Europe. That's well below the five-year average of about 86% for this time of the year so close to the start of the heating season.
European gas storage levels are at a historical low this autumn as the Middle East crisis has cut off most LNG flows out of the Persian Gulf. Israel election 2026: War, security and coalition math
Israel votes Tuesday in its first election since the Oct. 7, 2023, Hamas attacks and since the U.S.-Israeli war with Iran began in February.
Recent polls show neither Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc nor the main opposition bloc reaching the 61 seats needed for a Knesset majority, leaving smaller and Arab parties potentially pivotal.
Security, the wars in Gaza and Iran, ultra-Orthodox military conscription and the economic cost of prolonged conflict are among the issues shaping the vote.
Israeli voters will head to the polls Tuesday in an election with implications beyond the country’s borders.
It is Israel’s first election since Hamas’ Oct. 7, 2023, attacks, which killed around 1,200 people and saw more than 250 abducted. The Palestinian Ministry of Health says Israel’s subsequent military campaign in Gaza has killed more than 74,500 people . It is also the first vote since the U.S. and Israel began their joint war against Iran in February.
Israelis vote for parties rather than directly electing a prime minister, with the Knesset’s 120 seats allocated through proportional representation. Parties must win at least 3.25% of the national vote to enter parliament.
Because outright majorities are rare, parties typically form coalitions. The president asks a Knesset member — usually the leader best placed to assemble a 61-seat majority — to form a government. If repeated attempts fail, another election can be called.
Israel&#x27;s Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu (C) waves to his supporters during a Likud Party meet-and-greet held as part of the campaign ahead of Israel&#x27;s legislative election, at King Solomon Hotel in Netanya on October 22, 2026. (Photo by JACK GUEZ / AFP via Getty Images)
Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu , who leads the right-wing Likud party, returned to power in late 2022 with a coalition including far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties. The alliance has since been strained by disputes over a Gaza ceasefire and the conscription of ultra-Orthodox men.
Recent polls put both Netanyahu&#x27;s bloc and the main opposition bloc, led by centrist former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, short of the 61 seats needed to govern, making smaller parties potentially decisive.
Arab parties are also in the running, but most of the main Jewish parties have ruled out alliances with them. More than 20% of Israel&#x27;s population is Arab, but Arab parties have rarely joined governing coalitions.
The impending vote is effectively a referendum on Netanyahu, with the Hamas attacks having "transformed Israeli society," according to László Csicsmann, a Middle East expert at Corvinus University of Budapest in Hungary.
"Israel has been at war since October 7, 2023, and the so-called &#x27;seven-front war&#x27; launched by Netanyahu has undoubtedly worn down Israeli society," he told CNBC. "The side that can better convince voters that it can protect the population is likely to win. Other campaign issues include mandatory military service, which is particularly sensitive for religious Jewish parties and Haredi communities."
Military service is compulsory for most Jewish, Druze and Circassian Israelis over 18, while most other Arab Israelis are exempt. The conscription of ultra-Orthodox men remains a major legal and political dispute.
The economic toll of the war is also a major issue, Csicsmann said.
"Due to the war and high military spending, economic issues are also dominating the political campaign," he told CNBC, noting that Israel currently spends around 8% of its gross domestic product on military expenditure. "In addition, nearly every family has a soldier or reservist who has been called up and has left their job for months."
A Netanyahu defeat would make Iran war harder for U.S., says analyst
Defense and security are also at the forefront of the election, said Daniel Schatz, a political scientist and former visiting scholar at Harvard, Stanford and Georgetown.

A stock market crash is possible, but perspective is what's important
Based on several historically accurate indicators, the odds of a stock market crash and/or significant decline do appear to be above average. But this doesn't guarantee that equities will crash.
What's even more important than these dire historical outlooks in the wake of a roughly 700-point single-session decline in the Nasdaq Composite is maintaining perspective.
On the one hand, stock market corrections, bear markets, and even crashes are a normal part of the investing cycle. We may not enjoy red arrows in our portfolios, but no amount of fiscal or monetary policy maneuvering can prevent downturns from happening every now and then.
However, downturns share an important trait: they resolve quickly.
Since the start of the Great Depression in September 1929, data from Bespoke Investment Group show that the average S&P 500 bear market (i.e., a 20% or greater decline) reached its trough after 286 calendar days, or roughly 9.5 months.
Conversely, the typical bull market endured for 1,023 calendar days, or approximately 3.6 times as long.
Statistically, it pays to be a long-term optimist on Wall Street. When things look their direst is typically when opportunity presents itself for investors.

There is one important distinction to note when calculating the Buffett indicator. By Xiong Rui and Wang Shuo Published: Oct 26, 2026 08:42 PM
Recently, as the EU contemplates expanding restrictions on Chinese imports, "Europe getting tough on China" has become a headline pushed by several Western media outlets, with some even urging Washington to follow Europe's steps.
For Europe, a tough stance toward China reflects a deep-seated anxiety over its shifting relative strength and growing worries about its domestic and international environment.
Currently, Europe is locked in structural economic headwinds. Although its economy gradually recovered following the sovereign debt crisis, deep-rooted issues like a lack of growth momentum have proven difficult to resolve. High energy costs, underinvestment and market fragmentation remain intertwined.
As Chinese firms gain ground in Europe's traditional strongholds and the US leverages policies to draw global capital, Europe finds itself caught in a double squeeze: terrified of its domestic industries losing their competitive edge, while fearful of capital, factories and jobs fleeing overseas at the same time.
Solving these structural dilemmas demands sustained investment, the dismantling of entrenched interest barriers and the delicate reconciliation of internal divisions. By contrast, scapegoating its troubles on a "China shock" requires far less political capital than pushing through painful domestic reforms. This largely explains why some European figures are so eager to point the finger at competitors for "not playing by the rules," while failing to reflect on their own problems.
Europe faces the dual pressure of status anxiety and security reliance. Certain European leaders worry that a relative decline in economic clout will render Europe increasingly powerless in major-power geopolitical competition.
In the calculations of some European strategists, taking a hard line on China appears to offer a catch-all solution: Domestically, framing it as safeguarding economic security addresses protectionist calls and secures support from voters and special interest groups; externally, aligning on China policy creates common ground with Washington, demonstrating to the US that Europe still holds strategic value in exchange for security guarantees.
Yet, the fatal flaw in this strategy is that a temporary alignment between Washington and Brussels on Beijing cannot paper over their divergences regarding tariffs, industrial policy and defense burden-sharing. Even if their direction of "de-risking" from China aligns on paper, endless disputes remain over who builds new capacity, who captures market share and who sets the global rules.
Furthermore, Washington has its own geopolitical calculus when managing ties with Beijing. While Europe escalates its pressure on China, the US quietly retains room for engagement and deal-making. If Europe blindly sacrifices its cooperation with China merely to prove its loyalty, it is unlikely to secure the firm security commitments it craves in return.
Taking a tough stance on China will not solve Europe's problems; it risks creating far more. Evading domestic reform by building external walls will fail to remedy Europe's competitiveness deficit. Squeezing the space for cooperation with China risks depriving European businesses of crucial market access and supply chain synergies, while piling on higher production costs and green transition burdens.
The starting point for Europe's China policy ought to be firmly grounded in its own long-term interests. While protecting industrial interests is entirely legitimate, trade protectionism must be rooted in facts and international rules. Fair market competition never guarantees that any single player will win forever.
Europe's own history demonstrates that enduring peace cannot exist without mutual respect for interests, nor without dedicated efforts to translate shared interests into reality. This historic lesson should not be tossed aside when China is involved, nor should the vast common interests between Beijing and Brussels be selectively ignored in a bid to pander to Washington. For the transatlantic relationship, casting China as a common adversary may buy a fleeting moment of alignment, but it will never foster true, long-term cohesion.
If Europe wishes to command respect and achieve genuine strategic autonomy, it must rely on its own concrete actions.
