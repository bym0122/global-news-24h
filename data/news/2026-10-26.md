Stocks generally trend higher over the long term, but it&#x27;s getting harder to ignore the growing chorus of risks that could derail the current bull market. High interest rates can be a major headwind for the stock market, particularly now, when so many tech companies are borrowing money to build AI infrastructure.

A series of global geopolitical challenges are roiling the energy markets right now. The U.S. and Iran have been locked in a conflict since late February, resulting in the closure of the Strait of Hormuz waterway, through which 25% of the world&#x27;s seaborne oil normally transits each day. The ongoing war between Saudi Arabia and Yemen also took a turn for the worse recently, while Ukraine and Russia continue to target one another&#x27;s energy infrastructure, knocking even more supply offline.
As a result, the price of a single barrel of West Texas Intermediate crude oil has surged by 72% this year to $98, driving up the cost of gasoline, diesel, and even jet fuel. That means consumers are paying more for every product that travels by truck, boat, or plane, which includes groceries, retail goods, and more.
This is stoking inflation in a very big way. In fact, the Consumer Price Index (CPI) rose at an annualized rate of 3.7% in September, substantially higher than the Federal Reserve&#x27;s 2% target. The Fed raised the federal funds rate (overnight interest rates) by 25 basis points at its September meeting to put the brakes on the economy and cool prices down, and chairman Kevin Warsh warned there might be more hikes to come.
Rising interest rates could derail this bull market
The Fed&#x27;s last rate-hiking cycle spanned from March 2022 to August 2023, when policymakers were trying to tame the previous surge in inflation. The S&P 500 suffered a bear market decline of 20% during that period, and history could repeat.
Rising interest rates are bad for stocks for a few reasons. First, they force consumers to allocate more of their household income to debt repayments, leaving less room for discretionary spending. Second, high rates make companies think twice about borrowing money to fund their expansion plans, limiting their growth potential. These two factors hurt corporate earnings, and earnings drive stock prices over the long term.
Third, rising interest rates increase the yield on risk-free assets like cash and government Treasury bonds , giving investors an alternative to stocks. These assets are very popular during periods of uncertainty, because they give investors a guaranteed return.
The S&P 500 is trading at a Shiller cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio of 40.9 as I write this (Oct. 22), a shade below its all-time high of 44.2 from the peak of the dot-com bubble in 2000. Had you invested just $500 in Berkshire stock when he took the helm and never sold, it would have been worth a whopping $24 million by the time he stepped down. The same investment in the S&P 500 would have grown to just $199,851 over the same period, so Buffett had a knack for picking winning stocks.
In his 1986 letter to Berkshire&#x27;s shareholders, Buffett said, "We simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy only when others are fearful." Simply put, he would buy stocks aggressively during downturns in the broader market, because he knew the upward trend would always prevail over the long term.
Buffett also consistently reminded investors that stocks aren&#x27;t just financial instruments that move up and down -- they represent real businesses with real fundamentals. A person wouldn&#x27;t sell their house on a whim just because its value dropped during a given year, so selling a stock for the same reason makes very little sense. As long as the underlying business is healthy, a decline in stock price should be used as an opportunity to buy more.
As a result, while stock market turbulence can be unnerving, investors might want to get ready to put some money to work.

Obama blames Trump’s Iran war for rising fuel prices
“The current president chose to make a series of bad decisions,” Obama said in Cleveland, Ohio, where he rallied in support of former Democratic Senator Sherrod Brown.
“Most importantly, he decided to start an unnecessary war that nobody asked for,” Obama said.
The former president defended the 2015 nuclear agreement reached under his administration, which restricted Tehran’s nuclear programme in exchange for sanctions relief.
“Iran didn’t have nuclear weapons because I had put a deal in place that everybody acknowledged kept them from getting nukes, and I didn’t have to fire a missile to do it. I just had to engage in diplomacy,” Obama said.
Obama blamed the conflict for rising oil and diesel prices, citing attacks on US military bases, American casualties and disruptions to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz.
“Our allies and our bases have been attacked. Americans have been killed, hundreds of Americans have been wounded. We have run down our stock of missiles, and Iran now has veto power over the Strait of Hormuz where oil goes through to the world market,” he said.
The disruptions in oil flow through the strait, located between Iran and Oman, have pushed up energy prices worldwide. Before the war, a fifth of global oil and gas passed through the strait, which Iran has used as leverage in the war.
“This is the reason why the price of oil is up … This is the reason the price of diesel is up.”
The spike in diesel and petrol prices will likely cost the Republican Party, as the majority of Americans hold Trump responsible for starting the war and the subsequent increase in prices.
Obama also mocked Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over his focus on military culture and the Pentagon’s plans to livestream the execution of Nidal Hasan, convicted of killing 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009.
Tariffs are making life more expensive for Americans
Obama criticised Trump’s tariffs, saying Americans were paying more for goods, including products made in the US using imported parts.
“When you impose tariffs, consumers pay the tab,” he said.
“It’s not just the goods that you use directly that have been imported … It’s also the car you build here in America. They might need a part from somewhere else, and now suddenly there’s a tariff on it.”
He questioned Trump’s trade dispute with Canada, a major US trading partner.
The two countries have been locked in a trade dispute, with Trump threatening 50 percent tariffs on Canadian vehicles and auto parts from January 2027.
“And the big trade war he’s starting now is with that well-known evil empire Canada,” Obama said sarcastically.
“Canadians are the nicest people … they’re like our friendly neighbours to the north, and he’s like picking a fight with them.”
Obama also pointed to the growing US national debt, which has reached $40 trillion.
“Instead of bringing down deficits like he said with these tariffs, we’re now running a debt to the tune of $40 trillion.”
The figure refers to the total federal debt, which has accumulated over successive administrations, rather than the annual budget deficit.

“After two years of chaos and cruelty and corruption and incompetence, the likes of which we have not seen in the modern era, you get a chance to say, ‘Enough is enough,&#x27;” Obama declared. “We are voting for change right here in Ohio.”

“The only bill they passed of any significance in the last two years was a trillion-dollar tax break that mostly benefitted the richest Americans,” he said.

Oil Prices Rise Despite Middle East Export Recovery
Middle Eastern oil exports are recovering, with Hormuz flows reaching 11.7 million bpd in October, approaching pre-war levels despite continued disruptions.
Escalating tanker attacks and record freight costs threaten supply stability, keeping oil prices elevated despite improving export volumes.
Global oil markets remain vulnerable, as costly shipping workarounds, depleted inventories, and persistent geopolitical risks prevent a return to normality.
Reports about recovering oil flows in the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea have dominated headlines over the past couple of weeks. Yet oil prices have only moved higher, suggesting barrel numbers alone are not enough to convince the world everything’s back to normal. It isn’t.
According to the latest Kpler numbers, October oil exports out of the Middle East in total are in fact higher than they were before the U.S. and Israel launched their first strikes on Iran at the end of February. Flows out of the Strait of Hormuz alone have averaged 11.7 million barrels daily this month, Kpler estimated, up from 9.8 million barrels daily in September, and close to the pre-war average of 14.5 million barrels daily in February, Reuters’ Ron Bousso reported .
However, these numbers are the product of a lot of effort, expenditure, and risk—because the reports of rebounding oil exports out of the Persian Gulf are not the only news out of the region. The other big development there is Iran’s intensification of strikes on tankers. According to data from the UK Maritime Trade Operations and Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps statements, at least 25 ships were hit in Hormuz in the first 20 days of October. Yet, outlets tracking these incidents note that many do not report if they get attacked, for various reasons, which suggests the number could be higher—as high as 40-50 vessels. Most of these are tankers. Related: Middle East Physical Crude Slides While Brent Hits Five-Month High
Meanwhile, Kpler itself reported a sharp drop in tanker traffic via Hormuz this month as a result of the strikes. “Crude crossing the strait fell 27% from a wartime high the week before, to at least 10.1 million barrels a day,” two Kpler analysts reported in early October, as the firm counted only seven commodity carriers traversing the strait on October 6th. This was the lowest figure since late July, the analysts said.
Further, Reuters earlier this week reported  at least 18 tanker attacks over just the last two weeks, based on data from Kpler. The firm reported earlier this week that the daily average flow of oil out of the Persian Gulf had shrunk to 8.5 million barrels, from “more than 20 million barrels daily” for two weeks ago. This is quite a sharp and deep drop in export rates.
This sort of conflicting data from the same sources does not really contribute to a sense of security about oil supply from the Middle East, especially when the reports about strikes on tankers continue at a steady trickle. Meanwhile, a lot of the oil coming out of the Persian Gulf is doing so via ship-to-ship transfers that have pushed freight rates to record highs and kept them there. This has become such a serious problem that President Trump was said to be considering lifting sanctions on some tankers used to transport Russian oil to alleviate a shortage of oil carriers due to the expansion in STS off the coast of Oman.
That will not help, however. Because the supply of so-called shadow fleet tankers is also limited—because they are busy carrying that sanctioned oil. “Sanctions relief does not equal immediately usable mainstream tanker capacity,” Rahul Kapoor, global head of shipping analytics and research for S&P Global Energy, said as quoted by Bloomberg earlier this week. “The number of vessels that could realistically migrate back into mainstream trading would likely be materially smaller than the overall sanctioned fleet.”
With all these factors at play, and with the chance of a peace deal between the United States and Iran as remote today as it was three months ago, it is hardly a wonder that prices are moving higher—at least on the futures market. In the physical market, prices are actually down, Bloomberg reported this week. The reasons: that notorious rebound in Hormuz outflows, despite the tanker attacks, and a slight dip in tanker rates, which is a very welcome development.
“There’s still plenty of headline risk at play. Oil stocks are quite low and it’s difficult to see them rebuilding given current dynamics,” a senior commodity analyst at Marex told Bloomberg. Ryan Fitzmaurice added that there was still hope for lower heating demand this winter, if El Niño lives up to expectations. Lower heating demand, in turn, would bring some relief to prices. Betting on the weather, however, is not the soundest strategy, which is why chances are prices will remain elevated until at least the end of the year.
What the latest developments in the Middle East have shown is that there is always a workaround, even for a severe problem such as the closure of Hormuz—but these workarounds come at a price and are not a replacement for business as usual. One way or another, the world is adapting to this new situation in energy security. It has no other choice, really.
