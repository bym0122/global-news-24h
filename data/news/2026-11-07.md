Wall Street’s “Energy Transition” Funds Are Packed With Natural Gas
Amazon in Texas, Nvidia and SoftBank in Ohio, and Microsoft with Chevron are all tied to huge gas-fired power projects for data centers.
Wall Street's "energy transition" funds hold natural gas and data center companies, and Blackstone calls gas essential to the transition.

As we head into the United Nations’ 31st annual flagship climate conference (COP31), to be held next week in Antalya, Türkiye, greenwashing presents a large and growing problem in the private sector. As the tech sector’s greenhouse gas emissions explode against the backdrop of the artificial intelligence boom and the clean energy incentives dry up under the Trump administration, Big Tech and Wall Street alike are funding a boom in natural gas, all under the guise of funding the future of clean energy.
“ On Wall Street, the ‘energy transition’ includes natural gas and data centers ,” blared a Grist headline published earlier this week. The article details how private equity funds are making a killing on “energy transition” funds as electrification takes hold on a global scale and solar and wind power continue to see runaway growth trends, both current and projected. But many of these prominent energy transition funds prominently feature a fossil fuel and major contributors to greenhouse gas emissions on a global scale: natural gas.
“‘Energy transition’ is a flexible term,” says Dan Cohn, an energy finance analyst with the energy think tank Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis. “And I’m unsurprised to see either the gas or electrical equipment and data center-related companies in some of these funds. That is perfectly consistent with how I know some investors think about the energy transition,” he went on to say.
And those investors are not alone. Natural gas has been heavily promoted by the fossil fuel sector as a ‘bridge fuel’, serving as a stepping stone in the transition away from oil and coal toward 100 percent renewables. However, environmental experts question the wisdom of this approach and scientists have pushed back on the idea that natural gas is any cleaner than other fossil fuels, and calls for the abandonment of the ‘bridge fuel’ narrative have gained momentum in recent years.
Methane, the principal ingredient in natural gas, is an extremely potent greenhouse gas. While methane breaks up much more quickly in the atmosphere than the better-known carbon dioxide, it is 80 times more potent at warming over a 20-year period. And peer–reviewed studies (like this one , this one , and this one ) are increasingly indicating that natural gas produces much, much more methane over its life cycle than previously thought. Plus, some studies even indicate that natural gas could be worse for the climate than coal when you include the emissions associated with the energy-intensive cooling, storage, and shipping processes associated with liquefied natural gas.
However, the ‘bridge fuel’ argument still seems to hold major currency on Wall Street and in Silicon Valley. “I know a lot of the investors out there do consider gas a transition fuel,” Ryan Loughead, a senior associate and former climate solutions analyst for BloombergNEF, told Grist. For example, New York-based Blackstone, one of the world’s largest private equity firms, “is explicit in its view that methane gas, a fossil fuel and the primary component of natural gas, is essential to the energy transition,” reports Grist.
Meanwhile, Big Tech firms are causing a major natural gas boom around the country at the same time that they tout their backing of next-gen clean energy research, development, and adoption. Some of the biggest names in tech are building historically huge natural gas plants as we speak. Amazon is constructing a gas-fired power plant in Texas that will be the single-biggest source of power-related emissions in the entire United States according to current specs, which include seven gas plants. And in August, Nvidia announced that it would partner with Japan’s SoftBank and the federal government to build the largest fossil-fuel plant in the United States to power an Ohio project by OpenAI, the firm behind ChatGPT. And Microsoft just signed a 20-year deal with Chevron to provide 2.67 gigawatts of natural-gas fired power for a proposed West Texas data center.
Together, these developments greatly imperil the world’s ability to keep global warming within manageable limits. The United Nations says that it is now an inevitability that the world will fail to meet the legally binding goals set forth by the Paris climate accord as a result of “ decades of insufficient climate action ”, but notes that here is “ample opportunity to accelerate action”, and concludes that “countries can still limit peak warming, shorten the duration of overshoot and keep the goals of the Paris Agreement within reach”. Removing natural gas as a pillar of transition agendas and portfolios is clearly one such opportunity.

Stock futures explained: how Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 contracts work
Stock futures let traders hedge or speculate on the Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 nearly around the clock, and these five core concepts explain how they work
Hours before the opening bell rings in New York at 9:30 a.m. Eastern, financial news sites are already reporting whether Wall Street looks set to rise or fall. Those early readings do not come from the regular stock session, which has not started yet. They come from stock futures, contracts that keep changing hands while the main trading day for individual shares is closed.
Each one is a standardized agreement tied to the value of a stock index rather than to any single company&#x27;s shares. They let a trader take a position on an entire benchmark without buying any of the companies inside it. The best-known U.S. contract families track the Dow Jones Industrial Average, the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq $NDAQ -100. CME $CME Group, the Chicago-based exchange operator, lists the most widely followed versions.
The three indexes are built differently. The Dow is price-weighted, so a stock&#x27;s share price determines its influence. The S&P 500 is weighted by market capitalization. The Nasdaq -100 uses a modified version of market-cap weighting. Those design choices help explain why Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq futures do not always move in lockstep, even when they react to the same headline.
The contracts serve several groups at once. Portfolio managers use them to hedge stock holdings against a decline. Speculators use them to take a view on market direction. Because futures keep trading after the cash market closes, they also work as a price-discovery tool. They absorb earnings releases, economic data and news from abroad as it arrives.
A single premarket number leaves out much of how these contracts behave, including leverage, expiration dates and the gap that can open between a futures price and its index.
The five slides that follow cover a selection of core concepts. They explain how a stock index futures contract works, how the Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq -100 contracts differ from one another and why futures trade overnight. The last slide also covers how to read them before the open. Each slide stands on its own. A reader can go straight to the contract or question at hand.
What stock index futures are and how a futures contract works
A stock index future is a standardized contract to buy or sell the value of a stock index at a set price on a set future date. The contract is settled in cash, and no shares ever change hands.
Cash settlement is what separates index futures from buying stock. A trader holding an index futures contract does not own shares of any company in the index. The position gains or loses money based only on where the index level ends up compared with the price agreed in the contract.
Every contract carries a multiplier set by the exchange. Multiply the index level by that multiplier, and the result is the contract&#x27;s notional value. That figure is the total market exposure one contract represents. A larger multiplier means each point the index moves is worth more to the contract holder.
Traders do not pay that full notional value up front. They post margin instead, a good-faith deposit held against possible losses. The exchange and the broker set the required amount, and it changes over time. The deposit covers only a fraction of the contract&#x27;s value. That difference creates leverage. A modest move in the index produces a gain or loss that is large relative to the cash put up.
Hedging, speculation and price discovery are the three main purposes. A portfolio manager worried about a decline can sell index futures to offset losses in a stock portfolio without selling the shares themselves. A speculator buys or sells contracts to take a position on market direction. Prices that update with every trade give the market a running estimate of what the index is worth.
U.S. equity index futures follow a quarterly calendar. Contracts expire in March, June, September and December, on the third Friday of the contract month. A trader who wants to keep a position past expiration rolls it into the next quarter&#x27;s contract before the current one expires.
A futures price rarely matches the cash index exactly. The gap reflects fair value, also called cost of carry. Fair value adjusts the index level for interest rates and expected dividends. That adjustment explains why futures can trade slightly above or below the index they track.
S&P 500 futures began trading at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange in 1982. The smaller E-mini S&P 500 contract followed in 1997.
How Dow futures work: the E-mini Dow contract and its 30 stocks
"Dow futures" usually refers to the E-mini Dow, a contract with the ticker symbol YM that trades on the Chicago Board of Trade, part of CME $CME Group. Its value equals $5 times the level of the Dow Jones Industrial Average.
That multiplier sets how sensitive the contract is to the index. Each point the Dow gains or loses changes the value of one YM contract by $5, either in the holder&#x27;s favor or against it.
A smaller version exists for traders who want less exposure. The Micro E-mini Dow, ticker MYM, is one-tenth the size of the standard contract. It is valued at $0.50 times the index. Both contracts follow the same benchmark, so they rise and fall together. The difference is the dollar amount riding on each point.
The Dow Jones Industrial Average is a narrow benchmark. It holds 30 large U.S. companies. Charles Dow first published the average in 1896. It is one of the oldest stock market indexes still in use. A committee at S&P Dow Jones Indices chooses its members rather than a fixed formula. Transportation and utility companies are tracked in separate Dow averages.
What sets the Dow apart is how it counts each company. The index is price-weighted. It adds up the share prices of its members rather than their total market values. A stock trading at a higher price per share pulls the index harder than one trading at a lower price. A smaller company with an expensive stock can move the Dow more than a far larger company whose shares trade at a lower price.
This method also means a stock split can shrink a company&#x27;s influence. When a company splits its shares, its share price falls. Its weight in the Dow falls with it. The index relies on a figure called the Dow divisor. That divisor is adjusted after splits and membership changes so the index level stays continuous.
The weighting explains why Dow futures can move differently from S&P 500 or Nasdaq $NDAQ -100 futures on the same day. A sharp move in one high-priced Dow stock can push YM noticeably while broader index futures barely react. The reverse also happens. A large move in a company that weighs heavily in the S&P 500 or Nasdaq -100 but sits outside the Dow can lift those futures while Dow futures lag behind.
How S&P 500 futures work: the E-mini S&P 500 and market-cap weighting
The E-mini S&P 500, ticker symbol ES, is the main futures contract tied to the S&P 500 index. Each contract is valued at $50 times the index level. It trades on CME $CME Globex, the electronic platform run by CME Group.
The multiplier ties the contract to the benchmark. When the S&P 500 rises or falls, the value of each ES contract moves with it, scaled by $50 per index point.
A smaller version is also listed. The Micro E-mini S&P 500, ticker symbol MES, is one-tenth the size of the standard contract, at $5 times the index. Both follow the same index. The difference is how much market exposure a single contract carries. The smaller contract lets a trader adjust a position in finer steps.
The index behind both contracts covers 500 large U.S. companies. S&P Dow Jones Indices maintains it. Companies are weighted by market capitalization, the total market value of their shares. The index uses a float-adjusted version of that measure, which counts only shares available for public trading.
Under this method, bigger companies carry more weight. A company&#x27;s share price on its own does not decide its influence. A company with a modest share price but a very large total market value counts for more than one with a high share price and a smaller total value. That separates the S&P 500 from the price-weighted Dow.
The weighting shapes how S&P 500 futures behave. A sharp move in one of the largest companies shifts the index more than a move of the same percentage in a smaller member. A price swing in ES therefore leans most heavily on the biggest U.S. companies by market value.
Breadth is the index&#x27;s other defining feature. The S&P 500 includes companies from all 11 sectors of the Global Industry Classification Standard, from information technology and health care to energy and utilities. That spread makes it a common reference for the U.S. stock market as a whole. News reports frequently cite ES as a gauge of how Wall Street may open.
A move in ES reflects sentiment toward large U.S. companies as a group, rather than toward one industry or a short list of stocks.
How Nasdaq-100 futures work and why they react to tech news
Nasdaq $NDAQ futures track the Nasdaq -100 index, not the broader Nasdaq Composite. The benchmark contract is the E-mini Nasdaq -100, ticker NQ, which is valued at $20 times the index level.
The two Nasdaq indexes are easy to confuse in a market report. The Nasdaq Composite includes nearly every common stock listed on the Nasdaq Stock Market, from the largest companies to much smaller ones. The Nasdaq -100 is narrower. It holds the 100 largest non-financial companies listed on that exchange. A news report that says Nasdaq futures are higher is referring to contracts on this smaller group.
Nasdaq launched the index in 1985. It is also the benchmark behind the Invesco QQQ Trust, an exchange-traded fund that holds the index&#x27;s stocks. The fund and the futures contract follow the same group of companies through different instruments.
The exclusion of financial companies shapes what the index contains. Banks, insurers and similar firms are left out by design. What remains is a mix of technology, consumer, health care and industrial businesses. Large technology names carry the heaviest weight within that mix.
The Nasdaq -100 uses a modified market-capitalization weighting. Larger companies count for more, as they would in a standard market-cap index. The modification adds rules that limit how much any single company, or the largest group of companies, can dominate the index. Nasdaq reviews the index&#x27;s membership once a year, in December.
That tilt toward technology shapes how NQ tends to behave. Nasdaq -100 futures often react more strongly to technology news than contracts on broader or differently built indexes. Quarterly earnings from a major technology company can register more sharply in Nasdaq -100 futures than in Dow or S&P 500 futures. On a day when technology shares rise while banks and energy companies fall, Nasdaq -100 futures can be up while Dow futures are down.
A smaller version of the contract also trades. The Micro E-mini Nasdaq -100, ticker MNQ, is one-tenth the size of the standard contract, at $2 times the index. It tracks the same 100 companies with a smaller contract value per point of movement.
Why stock futures trade overnight and how to read them before the open
Equity index futures trade nearly around the clock on CME $CME Globex, from Sunday evening to Friday afternoon Eastern time. A short daily break runs from about 5 p.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern, and the contracts react to news while the regular stock session is closed.
The regular U.S. stock market session runs from 9:30 a.m. to 4 p.m. Eastern. Outside that window, futures tied to the Dow Jones Industrial Average, the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq $NDAQ -100 keep trading through most of the night. Investors get a live price for the broad market during hours when the opening bell is still far off.
That schedule puts futures among the first markets where overnight news shows up in prices. Many U.S. companies report quarterly earnings after the 4 p.m. close or before the 9:30 a.m. open. Several major economic releases also land before the bell. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the monthly U.S. jobs report and the consumer price index at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. Stock markets in Asia and Europe trade while New York is closed, too. Futures can move on any of these developments hours before individual shares change hands in regular trading.
Reading premarket futures starts with the reference point. News reports usually describe the contracts as up or down compared with the previous settlement or close. A line saying Nasdaq futures are lower means the contract is trading below that earlier mark. The figure measures movement since the last session ended. It does not predict where the market will close.
Reports often give the move in both index points and percentage terms. The percentage is the more useful number for comparing the three contracts. The Dow, the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq -100 sit at very different index levels. A move of the same number of points therefore represents a different percentage change for each one.
Futures are an indicator, not a guarantee. The market open can land somewhere different from where futures pointed earlier in the morning. Fresh news can arrive in the minutes before 9:30 a.m. and shift prices again.
Overnight trading is also thinner than trading during regular hours. With fewer buyers and sellers active, a single large order can push the price further than it would during the regular session. An overnight quote can be a less complete reading of where the broader market stands.
Individual investors can trade these contracts through brokers that offer futures accounts. Futures carry leverage. Losses on a position can exceed the initial deposit placed with the broker.

Global stocks fall amid ‘big worries over AI’ after Democrats’ midterms triumph
Asian and European markets follow US tech rout with many analysts predicting more waves of selling to come
European and Asian stock markets fell sharply on Friday after the Democrats’ decisive victory in the midterms triggered a rout in US tech stocks a day earlier.
A sell-off on Wall Street on Thursday was followed by sharp falls in Asian stock markets on Friday. The investor sentiment then reached London, where the market was, at one stage, poised for its worst day since March.
The FTSE 100 index, which tracks blue-chip shares listed in London, was down as much as 254 points, or 2.35%, at 10,528 points during a wave of selling in morning trading. But by the close of the day it had clawed back some of its losses to be down 1%, helped by a rise in US stocks when Wall Street reopened.
Germany’s Dax index fell by 2%, France’s Cac 40 dropped by 0.8% and Spain’s Ibex lost 0.6%.

Susannah Streeter, the chief investment strategist at Wealth Club, said: “The tech sell-off has intensified, with the pain of the brutal sell-off on Wall Street carried straight through to Asia and knocking sentiment in Europe .
“Big worries are colliding – the build-out of datacentres looks set to slow, which risks shredding the spending spree underpinning the boom, right at the time that investors have become super nervous about returns on this massive investment.”
However, there were signs of easing jitters on Wall Street on Friday, where tech stocks rose, lifting the S&P 500 share index by 0.3% and the tech-heavy Nasdaq closed up 1% – which only recovered a portion of their previous day’s losses.
That followed a stronger-than-expected employment report that showed the US economy added 165,000 new jobs in October, pulling the unemployment rate down to 4.1%.
On Thursday, the Nasdaq share index had its worst day in 19 months after slumping 5.5%.
Chip stocks led the slump on Thursday, with the memory maker SanDisk tumbling by 22% and the hard drive maker Western Digital falling by 17%. Wall Street’s “fear index”, the Vix, jumped by more than 60% to its highest levels since the early weeks of the Iran war, in a sign of rising market volatility.
The heavy falls continued in Asia on Friday, with Japan’s Nikkei 225 index sinking 6% and South Korea’s Kospi index tumbling more than 6.5%.
“It’s actually a bit of a disaster,” said Christopher Forbes, the head of Asia and Middle East at CMC Markets.
“[Clients] were long index, long stocks, and they all got carried out. All night, all day – we had client margin calls, client complaints … because it’s a very emotional thing, right? People are wedded to their positions and views on a stock or outcome, and you’re telling them: ‘Look, you’ve just lost money.’”
Chris Beauchamp, the chief market analyst at the investing and trading platform IG, said: “Yesterday’s [Thursday’s] brutal session marked a dramatic turnaround from Wednesday, but with AI fears still prominent there is a risk that there is much more selling to come.
“Investors remain on watch for a potential resumption of the Iran conflict, and are nervously watching the oil price after its slump earlier in the week. All in all it remains a very febrile atmosphere.”
However, despite the sharp losses suffered on global stock markets around the world, most indices are still up for the year. The Nasdaq Composite index remains up 6.35% since the start of January – even though it is more than 10% below its peak.
The FTSE 100 has gained almost 7.5% in 2026 – but it is now more than 300 points below the record high set in July.
Mohit Kumar, an economist at the investment bank Jefferies, said that while he did not expect a quick rebound from Thursday’s losses in the US, he also did not believe it marked the “start of the unwind of the AI trade”.
He said: “AI investment has been a key driver of US growth and we do not see the Democrats as giving up on this growth engine. Datacentres and AI infrastructure also create jobs and Democrats would be reluctant to let those jobs move on to other states or outside the country.”
Stock market tremors have also hit the London Metal Exchange, with copper poised for its biggest weekly fall since March.
Benchmark three-month copper, a key metal used in the semiconductor, electronics and automotive industry, has dropped 0.61%, leaving it positioned for a 2.79% fall this week.
Craig Lang, a principal analyst at the consultancy CRU, said the price of copper was increasingly influenced by the AI market and “sentiment towards datacentre expansion”.
