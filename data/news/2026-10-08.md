For Alberta independence supporter Keith Wilson, the western Canadian province is in a league of its own.
With its wealth of rich oil and gas reserves, a significant agricultural sector and a young and skilled workforce, its economy is one to be reckoned with, says Wilson.
The province will hold a referendum on 19 October, giving residents two options: vote to stay in Canada, or vote to move ahead with a formal binding referendum on independence at a later date.
The vote, despite not being a cut and dry "stay or leave", still stands to be among the most consequential in recent Canadian history, and a significant test of national unity.
One key issue has become central to the debate: Would Alberta be richer if it were to become an independent country?
"Alberta&#x27;s economy is unique. is fundamentally different than the rest of Canada&#x27;s - we have the people, the institutions, the infrastructure to excel," he says.
Alberta separatists have long argued that the province has been short-changed by being part of Canada, and that more of the oil and gas wealth would be kept within its borders instead of being shared with Ottawa, delivering it tens of billions in savings.
Alberta Premier Danielle Smith, who opposes independence, predicts a more sober outcome. She says the province could risk paying C$400bn ($283bn; £213bn) in transition costs alone, while bleeding billions more in lost investments and trade due to the political upheaval.
A report commissioned , external by her government released earlier this month calculated the costs of separation as between C$50bn and C$170bn over five years - and a highly unpredictable outlook over the long term.
The report cautions that the to-do list for a newly independent Alberta would be long and costly.
It would have to set up agencies to manage taxes and national security, develop its own constitution, legal and court systems, and pension plans, and negotiate the division of federal assets like national parks and military bases.
At Canada&#x27;s biggest rodeo, the starting gun is fired in the fight over Alberta separation
Alberta will vote on whether to remain part of Canada. What now?
Lennie Kaplan, a former finance official in Alberta, says the province would be expected to take on a share of Canada&#x27;s national debt, among many other fiscal challenges and responsibilities.
A report on Alberta independence by the CanadaWest Foundation , external , a non-profit Alberta-based think tank, estimates that the province could be stuck with additional debt ranging from C$258bn to C$333bn.
With a projected hit to Alberta&#x27;s GDP and the additional costs, the report estimates separation could hit Albertans&#x27; bottom line and reduce their disposable income by 5.8% on average.
"Why do we have to create all this uncertainty that might impact and impair the province&#x27;s fiscal position going forward? Why wouldn&#x27;t we just work within Canada to address these issues?" Kaplan asks.
Opinion polling indicates that around 20% to 25% of Albertans plan to vote in favour of moving ahead with a binding separation referendum, with higher support among younger, rural and conservative voters.
Behind the separatist push is the belief that Alberta is misunderstood and overlooked by decision-makers in Ottawa. For decades, that sentiment fuelled a sense of "western alienation" in the prairie province.

There was anger over environmental and political pushback that killed proposed pipelines from landlocked Alberta to coastal waters.
A decade of Liberal government in Ottawa has also caused frustration in reliably conservative Alberta. And, in addition, there is also leftover distrust of the federal government over what some Albertans saw as excessive lockdowns during the Covid-19 pandemic.
Over the past year, separatist organisers held townhalls across the province to gauge interest from the public. They then launched a citizen-led petition to separate earlier this year, which got more than 300,000 signatures.

Alberta is home to Canada&#x27;s oil and gas sector, with oil reserves estimated to be the fourth-largest in the world. Crude oil is by far Canada&#x27;s most profitable commodity, accounting for C$142bn in export value in 2025 alone.
The province has the highest GDP per capita in the country, and it contributes billions a year to the federal tax pool because of its strong economy.

Calculations by Tegan Hill and Nathaniel Li, economists at the Fraser Institute think tank, show that Alberta&#x27;s total net contribution to Ottawa since 2007 has been C$322bn, or an average of around $17bn per year.
"That&#x27;s nearly four times that of British Columbia, more than four times Ontario," Hill tells the BBC. "The other seven provinces were net recipients, meaning Ottawa spent or transferred more money to those provinces than it collected."
Hill explains that the amount Alberta contributes to the rest of Canada is one of the main frustrations cited by those in favour of separation.
The sentiment, she says, is that: "We&#x27;re paying to support these other provinces, and if we just went our own way, we could keep all that wealth for ourselves."

Calling this a symptom of a “destructive, outdated foreign policy” centred on overseas missions which have often made the globe less safe, the report says the imminent uptick in spending should be accompanied by a change of stance, with a much bigger focus on domestic defence.

The government is committed to spending 3.5% of GDP on defence by 2035 amid a defence investment plan (Dip) unveiled in July at the tail end of Keir Starmer’s premiership.
Starmer’s then defence secretary, John Healey, resigned over a refusal to commit to reaching 3% by 2030, but now that he is chancellor that target has been shelved for now .
It would involve a doubling of the defence budget in nominal terms from £70bn in 2026 to £151bn in 2035, according to the Transition Security Project, a research centre which was spun out of Common Wealth, a thinktank with close links to Andy Burnham .

Washington and Beijing have developed clear—and sharply divergent—diplomatic strategies to influence how other nations will develop, deploy, and govern AI systems and establish global AI leadership in the coming decades. Washington has emphasized strategic competition and American market dominance, bundling access to advanced chips, cloud services, AI models, compute power, applications, and financing into a “full-stack” AI export package that features U.S. industry and would be available to favored nations.
Beijing has promoted a vision for widespread collaboration based in AI as a development priority, with a focus on offering AI models that can be locally owned and operated, adapted for local needs, and supported by access to training and capacity building, a new multilateral AI body, and Chinese applications. Washington’s strategic partnerships have been designed to position the United States as the undisputed winner of an AI race with China. China’s partnerships have been designed to emphasize multilateral inclusion and equitable access to the benefits of AI, particularly for middle- and low-income countries.
China has been taking steps to move its foreign policy from rhetoric to practice, even though announcements still outpace implementation. On the eve of China’s World Artificial Intelligence Conference (WAIC) in July, representatives from 29 countries signed an agreement establishing the World AI Cooperation Organization ( WAICO ) as an “independent” intergovernmental organization headquartered in Shanghai. (Additional countries are now reported to have signed on, according to Chinese media outlets.) The following day, Chinese President Xi Jinping pledged 5,000 AI training and seminar opportunities for developing countries over five years. He also announced plans for international AI application centers with leading regional organizations such as BRICS, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), the African Union, and the Arab League, as well as assistance for 30 countries to use China’s AI-powered Mazu meteorological early-warning system.
It remains unclear exactly how WAICO’s commitments would play out in practice. The July announcement described its status and aims but did not clarify a financing mechanism, offer a delivery structure for its commitments, or clarify how the organization’s independence would be protected and assured. It is clear that WAICO is designed to help China realize its stated AI strategy. For example, China’s July AI cooperation and development action plan called for accessible computing services for developing countries and support for adapting open AI models to local needs, a sentiment Xi echoed in his speech at the WAIC. (Open-weight models allow developers a model they can copy and adapt for their own purposes, without requiring them to train a model from scratch. This can make AI systems cheaper and more affordable and can be particularly useful for nations that need to build systems to operate across highly specific, local environments.)
Meanwhile, China’s existing cooperation with ASEAN may illustrate how regional partnerships would operate in practice: The ASEAN-China Artificial Intelligence Industry Innovation Center, inaugurated in Beijing on May 24, forms part of the two sides’ digital cooperation agenda. Its planned work pairs practical demonstrations of AI applications in sectors such as healthcare and agriculture with training for ASEAN officials, company employees, and students, connecting access to AI technologies with the skills needed to use them. With more than 3,000 exhibits from over 1,100 companies, the WAIC displayed the industrial breadth that could underpin Chinese AI applications.
Washington has begun implementing its own competing multilateral AI offer through Pax Silica , which seeks to secure the supply chains needed to build and power AI systems and has garnered support from approximately two dozen U.S. partners and allies. Efforts like the American AI Exports Program support U.S. consortia selling systems abroad, giving members priority access to federal financing and export-license processing. The departments of commerce and state have announced a range of new programs aimed at strengthening ties between American technology companies and foreign markets, including mechanisms to connect U.S. suppliers to foreign buyers, credential AI supply chains, and finance commerce-designated export packages.
While the U.S. approach offers components of an AI ecosystem strategy, no U.S. program creates a soft-power offering similar to China’s development offerings. The U.S. has also expressly rejected proposals for any form of independent multilateral body that would support broader AI governance or cooperation.
After the WAICO announcement, Washington indicated that it might even go a step further toward isolationism by positioning Pax Silica as an all-or-nothing proposition for its signatories. Reuters reported a draft letter from the State Department announcing that membership in Pax Silica “[could not] be held alongside membership in duplicative initiatives whose expectations conflict with our own.” While Reuters could not establish if or when such a letter would be sent, it did quote an unnamed U.S. official who said the letter was drafted to clarify that governments “​can’t have it both ways.”
China, meanwhile, has publicly defended countries’ right to choose AI partners according to their development needs, allowing Beijing to present its assistance as compatible with keeping other partnerships open. That stance aligns more closely with what other governments are seeking when it comes to AI. On Sept. 21, Finland and Norway, along with 19 other nations and the European Commission, issued a joint statement urging global collaboration to address emerging frontier AI risks. The signatories included members of Pax Silica, WAICO participants, and nonaligned states.
Nations are not only aligned in seeking more multilateral mechanisms; they are also aligned in their desire to avoid dependency on any individual nation’s technology stack. On Sept. 14, European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde warned that reliance on foreign AI raises questions about whose laws govern sensitive data and whether access to essential services could be withdrawn or used as leverage in political negotiations. Governments weighing those risks lose nothing by increasing collaboration with Beijing, and pressure from Washington to forgo alternative partnerships and deepen dependencies on the U.S. as a technology partner seems increasingly foolhardy in a geopolitical environment where partners’ and allies’ trust in the U.S. has hit all-time lows.
It is hard to see how the U.S. can “win” any AI race by forcing governments around the world to choose between the two leading powers. Governments need support that fits the systems they already use and leaves them room to adopt AI on their own terms. They will want, and need, to build systems that incorporate both American and Chinese AI technologies as they seek to harness the benefits of AI transformation. They may also look for broader support than the limited offerings that the U.S. currently provides.
WAICO gives Beijing a standing organization through which it can organize this cooperation. Its appeal to partner governments is the prospect of practical help as their needs change, with membership in an institution devoted to AI development. A government could begin with one public service application and return for support adapting it to another language or extending it to other agencies. Whether WAICO can provide that assistance, and how much influence members will exercise over its priorities, remains unclear.
In addition, governments seeking to import and scale AI systems need to know whether a system will work with their data, meet local requirements, and remain affordable to operate—requirements that China has named explicitly in its technology diplomacy strategy. AI export packages that meet those needs early in adoption will secure a critical first-mover advantage for the nation (or companies) behind them.
Training programs will shape local engineers’ familiarity with specific software or hardware tools, and procurement rules built to support early purchases will determine which technologies can be deployed, how, and by whom.
First-mover advantages won’t go to the AI leader with the most cutting-edge models. It will go to the country that can best support partner nations in connecting infrastructure, models, applications, financing, capacity building, and operational standards into a coherent offering that allows leaders to scale AI adoption while maintaining their right to mix and match systems as needed.
The world is waiting to see what, if any, agreements the U.S. and China might strike on managing AI risks. For the United States, any agreement undercuts its ability to demand that partners and allies refrain from collaboration or trade with China regarding their own AI needs. For China, a stated commitment to openness and inclusion will be challenged by a singular agreement with the United States that excludes all other nations from access to critical information sharing on AI risks and safety standards. If the U.S. and China can agree to cooperate on critical AI safety risks, world leaders will breathe a collective sigh of relief. And then, with their next breath, they will ask, “Where do we come in, and what does this mean for us?”
At the moment, Beijing is far better positioned to answer that question than Washington.

The computer scientist behind groundbreaking research that helped land Nasa&#x27;s astronauts on the Moon, has died aged 90.
Margaret Hamilton was a trailblazer in her field, leading the team who developed the software for Nasa&#x27;s Apollo missions, which coined the term "software engineering".
Hamilton&#x27;s former employer, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), confirmed her death, adding she died on 30 September.
"To say Hamilton was a pioneer - to say she was ahead of her time - would be a dramatic understatement," MIT said in its statement, before acknowledging that she was a software engineer before it was mainstream.
Hamilton worked at MIT from 1959 until the mid-1970s and managed the Software Engineering Division.
It was in this division that she led a team of engineers writing and testing all onboard in-flight software for Nasa&#x27;s Apollo 11 project that guided US astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin to land safely on the Moon.
"I was attracted both by the sheer idea and the fact that it had never been done before. I was the first programmer to join and the first woman they hired," she told the Guardian in 2019.
In 1969, moments before the historic Apollo 11 Moon landing, an onboard computer raised an alarm, signalling an error was occurring.
The error detected the computer was overloaded, and for a moment it looked like the complex landing might not be achieved.
But Hamilton and her team had planned for such an error by engineering "priority-driven software" to shut down any background tasks and favour the most important - the Moon landing - according to MIT.
The team&#x27;s famous software engineering was immortalised in a 1969 photograph of Hamilton standing beside the stack of printed code that towered beside her.
Image caption, Hamilton with the printed computer code in 1969
In 2016, Hamilton&#x27;s life work was honoured by Barack Obama with the Presidential Medal of Freedom for her contribution to science and space travel.
At the time, Obama said Hamilton "symbolises that generation of unsung women who helped send humankind into space".
Hamilton, born in Indiana in 1936, first studied mathematics at the University of Michigan in 1955 before moving to Earlham College where she got a BA in mathematics in 1958.
She then moved to Boston, Massachusetts, a year later with her husband and originally took the job at MIT to support her husband during his law degree.
From the 1970s onwards, Hamilton used her expertise to found her own software businesses, including Hamilton Technologies, which is still based in Massachusetts.
"We are saddened by her death, but grateful for the opportunity to have celebrated her extraordinary achievements throughout her long life", Earlham College said in a statement on social media.
"Her pioneering spirit and remarkable legacy will continue to inspire generations of Earlhamites," the statement said.
Hamilton is survived by her daughter, a son-in-law, two grandsons and four great-grandchildren, according to MIT.
