SpaceX is seeking approval to build a 32.4-mile natural gas pipeline in Florida that would connect directly to the interstate gas network and supply the company's expanding launch operations at Cape Canaveral.
The proposed 16-inch pipeline would run through Brevard County and connect SpaceX operations directly to the Florida Gas Transmission system , giving the company a dedicated source of natural gas as its launch activity increases.
SpaceX subsidiary Coastal Connect Services filed for approval of a tariff with the Florida Public Service Commission. SpaceX would contract for all of the pipeline's proposed capacity, according to regulatory filings.
SpaceX currently depends heavily on LNG delivered by truck for its Florida launch operations. The company told regulators that its operations can require hundreds of truckloads of LNG for a single launch , while existing local pipeline infrastructure cannot provide the volumes it expects to need as launch activity increases.
The Florida project is part of a wider push by SpaceX to secure more direct control over the natural gas supply needed for its launch sites. In June, the company proposed an eight-mile natural gas pipeline at its Starbase facility near Boca Chica, Texas.
SpaceX is also moving into gas procurement. The company has begun hiring natural gas traders to manage fuel purchases for its launch sites in Florida and Texas, adding another piece of the energy supply chain to operations it controls internally.
The Florida proposal would connect directly with Florida Gas Transmission, an interstate pipeline system serving the state. Public Service Commission staff recommended approval of Coastal Connect's proposed tariff ahead of the commission's October 6 meeting.
The pipeline would reduce the number of LNG trucks needed to supply SpaceX operations in Florida and provide considerably more capacity as the company increases the pace of launches. The commission has not yet issued a final order on the tariff in the regulatory records reviewed for this story.

It is clear that WAICO is designed to help China realize its stated AI strategy. For example, China’s July AI cooperation and development action plan called for accessible computing services for developing countries and support for adapting open AI models to local needs, a sentiment Xi echoed in his speech at the WAIC. (Open-weight models allow developers a model they can copy and adapt for their own purposes, without requiring them to train a model from scratch. This can make AI systems cheaper and more affordable and can be particularly useful for nations that need to build systems to operate across highly specific, local environments.)
Meanwhile, China’s existing cooperation with ASEAN may illustrate how regional partnerships would operate in practice: The ASEAN-China Artificial Intelligence Industry Innovation Center, inaugurated in Beijing on May 24, forms part of the two sides’ digital cooperation agenda. Its planned work pairs practical demonstrations of AI applications in sectors such as healthcare and agriculture with training for ASEAN officials, company employees, and students, connecting access to AI technologies with the skills needed to use them. With more than 3,000 exhibits from over 1,100 companies, the WAIC displayed the industrial breadth that could underpin Chinese AI applications.
Washington has begun implementing its own competing multilateral AI offer through Pax Silica , which seeks to secure the supply chains needed to build and power AI systems and has garnered support from approximately two dozen U.S. partners and allies. Efforts like the American AI Exports Program support U.S. consortia selling systems abroad, giving members priority access to federal financing and export-license processing. The departments of commerce and state have announced a range of new programs aimed at strengthening ties between American technology companies and foreign markets, including mechanisms to connect U.S. suppliers to foreign buyers, credential AI supply chains, and finance commerce-designated export packages.
While the U.S. approach offers components of an AI ecosystem strategy, no U.S. program creates a soft-power offering similar to China’s development offerings. The U.S. has also expressly rejected proposals for any form of independent multilateral body that would support broader AI governance or cooperation.
After the WAICO announcement, Washington indicated that it might even go a step further toward isolationism by positioning Pax Silica as an all-or-nothing proposition for its signatories. Reuters reported a draft letter from the State Department announcing that membership in Pax Silica “[could not] be held alongside membership in duplicative initiatives whose expectations conflict with our own.” While Reuters could not establish if or when such a letter would be sent, it did quote an unnamed U.S. official who said the letter was drafted to clarify that governments “​can’t have it both ways.”
China, meanwhile, has publicly defended countries’ right to choose AI partners according to their development needs, allowing Beijing to present its assistance as compatible with keeping other partnerships open. That stance aligns more closely with what other governments are seeking when it comes to AI. On Sept. 21, Finland and Norway, along with 19 other nations and the European Commission, issued a joint statement urging global collaboration to address emerging frontier AI risks. The signatories included members of Pax Silica, WAICO participants, and nonaligned states.
Nations are not only aligned in seeking more multilateral mechanisms; they are also aligned in their desire to avoid dependency on any individual nation’s technology stack. On Sept. 14, European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde warned that reliance on foreign AI raises questions about whose laws govern sensitive data and whether access to essential services could be withdrawn or used as leverage in political negotiations. Governments weighing those risks lose nothing by increasing collaboration with Beijing, and pressure from Washington to forgo alternative partnerships and deepen dependencies on the U.S. as a technology partner seems increasingly foolhardy in a geopolitical environment where partners’ and allies’ trust in the U.S. has hit all-time lows.
It is hard to see how the U.S. can “win” any AI race by forcing governments around the world to choose between the two leading powers. Governments need support that fits the systems they already use and leaves them room to adopt AI on their own terms. They will want, and need, to build systems that incorporate both American and Chinese AI technologies as they seek to harness the benefits of AI transformation. They may also look for broader support than the limited offerings that the U.S. currently provides.
WAICO gives Beijing a standing organization through which it can organize this cooperation. Its appeal to partner governments is the prospect of practical help as their needs change, with membership in an institution devoted to AI development. A government could begin with one public service application and return for support adapting it to another language or extending it to other agencies. Whether WAICO can provide that assistance, and how much influence members will exercise over its priorities, remains unclear.
In addition, governments seeking to import and scale AI systems need to know whether a system will work with their data, meet local requirements, and remain affordable to operate—requirements that China has named explicitly in its technology diplomacy strategy. AI export packages that meet those needs early in adoption will secure a critical first-mover advantage for the nation (or companies) behind them.
Training programs will shape local engineers’ familiarity with specific software or hardware tools, and procurement rules built to support early purchases will determine which technologies can be deployed, how, and by whom.
First-mover advantages won’t go to the AI leader with the most cutting-edge models. It will go to the country that can best support partner nations in connecting infrastructure, models, applications, financing, capacity building, and operational standards into a coherent offering that allows leaders to scale AI adoption while maintaining their right to mix and match systems as needed.
The world is waiting to see what, if any, agreements the U.S. and China might strike on managing AI risks. For the United States, any agreement undercuts its ability to demand that partners and allies refrain from collaboration or trade with China regarding their own AI needs. For China, a stated commitment to openness and inclusion will be challenged by a singular agreement with the United States that excludes all other nations from access to critical information sharing on AI risks and safety standards. If the U.S. and China can agree to cooperate on critical AI safety risks, world leaders will breathe a collective sigh of relief. And then, with their next breath, they will ask, “Where do we come in, and what does this mean for us?”
At the moment, Beijing is far better positioned to answer that question than Washington.

- **发生了什么**: Since late September students across France have been holding protests against underfunding in education, and have been blocking access to many high schools.
The movement has turned violent in places – hundreds of students, teachers and police officers have been injured. Thousands of people – including an unknown number of outside troublemakers – have been arrested.
The protesters are complaining about staff shortages and dilapidated facilities in the country&#x27;s lycées, schools which are attended by 15-18-year-olds.
"Sometimes our teachers are absent for several weeks without being replaced," Madalena, a Paris student, told the BBC on Tuesday as demonstrations brought out hundreds of thousands across France . Her school buildings, she added, were "boiling hot" during the recent heat waves.
There is a variety of other complaints. Some resent the banning of mobile phones or school days that last until 18:00. "You get home at 7 pm, and have no time to sleep or do your homework," one lycéen told France 2.
France&#x27;s online platform that allocates places in higher education, Parcoursup, is another sore point.
The frustration has deep roots. About 80% of each cohort now passes the baccalauréat, which entitles students to a university place - though not necessarily their preferred choice. This leads to a scramble for desirable courses, and an anxiety focused on Parcoursup, which many regard as opaque.
Figure caption, Watch: Riot police clash with school protesters and use tear gas on Tuesday
Those grievances are real. But do they by themselves explain the form the protest movement has taken? How does France compare with other countries?
According to a recent report by the OECD, a club of developed nations, the French government spends more per lycée student than the EU average for upper-secondary schools.
Teacher shortages are indeed acute, but no worse than elsewhere. By OECD measures they are actually milder – fewer posts go unfilled – although French teachers receive relatively low pay.
Schooldays that stretch into the late afternoon are indeed a problem for French pupils. This is the result mostly of long lunch breaks and complex schedules rather than making up for holidays, which are not especially long compared with other EU countries.
The extended day has always been a sad fact of French school life and can hardly account for the present eruption. Neither can pressure created by a dysfunctional higher-education system.
In most European countries, 80-90% of young people complete secondary education, and competition for good university places creates stress elsewhere too.

To understand the pupils&#x27; tactics, one must look beyond the barricaded school gates to a political tradition. In France, the power has often resided on the street as much as in elected bodies. Students know this as well as anyone else.

On several occasions student protests defeated government reforms – such as a university overhaul in 1986 and a lower youth minimum wage in 1994.

Probably the most striking display of student power came in 2006, with the withdrawal of a law designed to loosen hire-and-fire rules for those under 26 to try to tackle youth unemployment.

French adults have shown how blockading roads can prevail over elected officials.
The gilets jaunes (yellow vests) uprising of 2018-2019 – which saw shops ransacked and government buildings attacked across the country – led the government to drop a rise in fuel taxes and approve €10bn in spending.
In 1968 – a heroic precedent cited by every French protest movement – a student uprising was actually brought to an end by election. But the lycéens are heirs to a political culture in which demonstrators compete with elected officials to be seen as the legitimate voice of the people.

But that very weakness could prove an asset in his showdown with students. Faced with rising borrowing costs, a deficit of 5.4% of GDP and a public debt at its highest level since the 1940s, Lecornu is financially as well as politically hamstrung.
Unlike his predecessors under intense pressure from the street, he has nothing to give.

Margaret Hamilton, the computer pioneer whose software powered the first moon landing, has died, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) announced on Wednesday. She was 90.
In an era before titles such as software engineer and computer scientist became mainstream, Hamilton, who died on 30 September, learned to program under the tutelage of a renowned MIT meteorologist.
She continued to perfect the craft in an MIT laboratory, writing software that could detect enemy aircraft. When the institute received a Nasa contract to develop the Apollo onboard flight software, she jumped at the opportunity.

In 1969, minutes before the Apollo 11 spacecraft was set to make history by landing on the lunar surface, an onboard computer’s alarm rang, indicating an emergency.
Faced with a decision of whether to abort the mission, the spacecraft continued on. The software was working just as Hamilton and her team intended.
“It quickly became clear the software was not only informing everyone that there was a hardware-related problem but was compensating for it – restarting and re-establishing the highest priority tasks. The error detection and recovery mechanisms had come to the rescue,” Hamilton said.
Barack Obama referenced the incident when he presented her with the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2016.
“Our astronauts didn’t have much time, but thankfully they had Margaret Hamilton,” he said.
Hamilton is survived by her daughter, a son-in-law, two grandsons and four great grandchildren, according to MIT.
Reflecting on the mission, in a 2009 interview with MIT News, Hamilton said: “Looking back, we were the luckiest people in the world; there was no choice but to be pioneers; no time to be beginners.”

By Robbie Meredith ,  Education and arts correspondent   and   Aimee Bell ,  BBC News NI
No child should be expected to learn in a cold classroom because of budget pressures, according to Northern Ireland&#x27;s commissioner for children.
Chris Quinn was speaking after it emerged that staff and pupils at schools across Northern Ireland have been told to "wear more clothes" to cut heating bills.
Schools have been told by the Education Authority (EA) that increasing costs will affect school energy bills and to reduce heating and lighting "wherever possible".
Quinn said: "Children should not be left to pick up the pieces of a problem that they did not create".
The EA has said that school gas and electricity bills are set to rise by about £8m by March 2027.
But principals have told BBC News NI it is not straightforward to cut heating costs, with one describing her school&#x27;s energy bills as "ferocious".
Rising energy costs are already affecting households across Northern Ireland.
The impact of the US-Israel war with Iran is one of the factors behind the increase.
The letter from the EA to head teachers said that "recent geopolitical events are expected to have a substantial impact on energy costs... during the 2026/27 financial year".
It said that total school gas bills were expected to go up by 60% from £10.7m to £17m by March 2027.
Meanwhile, total school electricity bills are expected to rise from £23.8m to £25.1m in the same time period.
"Given the scale of the anticipated cost increases, we are asking all schools to review their energy usage and identify opportunities to reduce consumption wherever possible," the EA said.
Among the measures suggested to schools are cutting heating use by an hour a day and switching off classroom lights to use natural daylight instead.
Quinn told BBC Radio Ulster&#x27;s Evening Extra programme that the department and EA had a "duty to properly fund schools and not to pass this burden onto children".
Quinn said he had visited schools where young people were already wearing extra jumpers and teachers were buying heaters to keep pupils warm and in extreme cases, some pupils were using hot water bottles as well.
"Learning in a cold classroom will have a detrimental effect on the child&#x27;s ability to learn but also, their health," he said.
He said the burden of savings were being put on the wrong people.
"That line about wearing extra clothes, in no other industry, in no other workplace, would you expect that people put on an extra jumper or wear a coat in doors", he said.
Image caption, Kirsty Logan-Hall says her school is in an older building which presents energy efficiency challenges
The principal of Tullygally Primary School in Craigavon, Kirsty Logan-Hall, said that the school was "committed to promoting responsible energy consumption and regularly reinforce good practice with staff and pupils".
"However, our ability to achieve significant reductions in energy usage is constrained by the nature of our estate," she said.
"The school operates from an older building which presents inherent energy efficiency challenges.
"In addition, our site is particularly spread out due to the accommodation and operational requirements associated with our specialist provision.
"This necessitates the heating, lighting and ongoing use of multiple areas across the site throughout the school day to ensure appropriate learning environments, accessibility and pupil welfare.
"It is important to recognise that there is limited scope for reducing energy consumption without adversely impacting the quality of provision, staff working conditions or the wellbeing of pupils, including those with additional needs."
Image caption, Claire Robinson says heating can&#x27;t just be "turned off" in some parts of the school
Meanwhile, the principal of Holy Evangelists Primary School in Dunmurry, Claire Robinson, said that energy costs were "ferocious".
Parts of the school are relatively new and have underfloor heating.
"Our classrooms can&#x27;t drop below a certain temperature so we have to keep the heating on.
"We can&#x27;t just turn the heating off in some areas of the school."
Robinson said that although the school was relatively new it had been given an energy efficiency rating of D due to the costs involved in running the heating system.
Image caption, Brian Guthrie is principal of Ebrington Primary School in Londonderry
Brian Guthrie, principal of Ebrington Primary School in Londonderry said asking children to sit in a class wearing extra clothes is "ridiculous".
"I&#x27;m not going be asking children and staff to put coats and hats on during the winter time.
"We will be running the heat as we need it but we do look at all other energy saving costs as we can," he told BBC Radio Foyle&#x27;s North West Today programme.
Guthrie said his school building is around ten years old and is, at present "very energy efficient".
The installation of 100 solar panels last year, he added, should "have paid itself back" over the next five years.
Children need to be taught in "an ambient classroom", he said. Adding the EA should "look at other ways of saving in schools".
Meanwhile, Emma Quinn from Rathcoole Primary told BBC Good Morning Ulster that the "lack of maintenance" has an impact on schools, adding that pupils have a right to come into a warm school after being outside.
"Putting this pressure onto us at the minute is ridiculous, when we are eco schools, we are doing all of these, we are turning off lights, but yet this lands in on our doorstep.
