But that advantage will narrow, he said, potentially leaving equities more vulnerable as financial conditions tighten.
"We&#x27;re in the part of the cycle where interest rates can rise without sending the equity market down because there&#x27;s enough earnings growth and there&#x27;s enough expected return," Dalio told CNBC&#x27;s Sri Jegarajah at the Milken Institute Asia Summit in Singapore on Thursday.
"But when that cushion comes down, then you&#x27;re coming later into that cycle. So that&#x27;s where we are."
His warning comes as U.S. Treasury yields hover near multi-decade highs. Investors are grappling with large government deficits, persistent inflation and rising borrowing tied to artificial intelligence investment.
Dalio said equities entered the current cycle offering significantly higher expected returns than bonds, helping sustain demand for stocks even as borrowing costs rose.
But as stock prices climb and bond yields increase, that relative advantage is diminishing, leaving less room for equities to absorb higher rates.
"Because of that change in pricing, that cushion has come down, and so now you&#x27;re starting to see credit spreads start to widen," he said.
Dalio also cautioned that investors may be overlooking the risk of deteriorating corporate cash generation, even as headline earnings continue to improve.
Asked whether companies could sustain strong profit growth in the third quarter, Dalio said investors should focus on free cash flow rather than earnings alone.
"I think you have to pay attention to free cash flows. ... Not just earnings. Because if you&#x27;re earning and then you&#x27;re investing and you&#x27;re not getting money out of that, you have a liquidity issue that&#x27;s evolving," he said.
"While earnings should continue to be improving, I would expect the free cash flows, I think, will be deteriorating."
Dalio stopped short of predicting an earnings decline or an imminent stock market correction. He said financial conditions have not yet tightened enough to significantly curb credit and spending.
Dalio also said he expected the global bond sell-off to continue as governments and companies compete for capital, as mounting debt issuance puts pressure on investor demand.
Governments are borrowing heavily to finance fiscal deficits, while companies are raising funds to invest in emerging technologies, creating an imbalance that could keep upward pressure on interest rates.
"We are in a bond bear market, that&#x27;s I think, pretty clear, and I think that there&#x27;s more to go would be my guess," he said.
Higher borrowing costs will eventually force a reduction in credit and spending, weighing on economic activity and potentially spilling over into equity markets, Dalio said.

The computer scientist behind groundbreaking research that helped land Nasa&#x27;s astronauts on the Moon, has died aged 90.
Margaret Hamilton was a trailblazer in her field, leading the team who developed the software for Nasa&#x27;s Apollo missions, which coined the term "software engineering".
Hamilton&#x27;s former employer, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), confirmed her death, adding she died on 30 September.
"To say Hamilton was a pioneer - to say she was ahead of her time - would be a dramatic understatement," MIT said in its statement, before acknowledging that she was a software engineer before it was mainstream.
Hamilton worked at MIT from 1959 until the mid-1970s and managed the Software Engineering Division.
It was in this division that she led a team of engineers writing and testing all onboard in-flight software for Nasa&#x27;s Apollo 11 project that guided US astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin to land safely on the Moon.
"I was attracted both by the sheer idea and the fact that it had never been done before. I was the first programmer to join and the first woman they hired," she told the Guardian in 2019.
In 1969, moments before the historic Apollo 11 Moon landing, an onboard computer raised an alarm, signalling an error was occurring.
The error detected the computer was overloaded, and for a moment it looked like the complex landing might not be achieved.
But Hamilton and her team had planned for such an error by engineering "priority-driven software" to shut down any background tasks and favour the most important - the Moon landing - according to MIT.
The team&#x27;s famous software engineering was immortalised in a 1969 photograph of Hamilton standing beside the stack of printed code that towered beside her.
Image caption, Hamilton with the printed computer code in 1969
In 2016, Hamilton&#x27;s life work was honoured by Barack Obama with the Presidential Medal of Freedom for her contribution to science and space travel.
At the time, Obama said Hamilton "symbolises that generation of unsung women who helped send humankind into space".
Hamilton, born in Indiana in 1936, first studied mathematics at the University of Michigan in 1955 before moving to Earlham College where she got a BA in mathematics in 1958.
She then moved to Boston, Massachusetts, a year later with her husband and originally took the job at MIT to support her husband during his law degree.
From the 1970s onwards, Hamilton used her expertise to found her own software businesses, including Hamilton Technologies, which is still based in Massachusetts.
"We are saddened by her death, but grateful for the opportunity to have celebrated her extraordinary achievements throughout her long life", Earlham College said in a statement on social media.
"Her pioneering spirit and remarkable legacy will continue to inspire generations of Earlhamites," the statement said.
Hamilton is survived by her daughter, a son-in-law, two grandsons and four great-grandchildren, according to MIT.

Unlike his predecessors under intense pressure from the street, he has nothing to give.

According to an analysis by researchers at the Transition Security Project , Britain has the most overseas-oriented military among European nations, with almost a quarter of the defence budget spent on commitments across five continents.
Calling this a symptom of a “destructive, outdated foreign policy” centred on overseas missions that have often made the world less safe, the report says ministers should prioritise changing their approach rather than immediately increasing spending, with resources shifted from overseas commitments to domestic defence.
