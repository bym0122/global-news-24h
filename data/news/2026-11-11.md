LNG Traffic Through Hormuz Hits Highest Level Since Iran War Began
Exports of liquefied natural gas via the Strait of Hormuz last month reached the highest total since February, at 31 tankers, compared with 27 in September, according to data from Kpler, as cited by Reuters.
What is perhaps more impressive is that most of these cargoes were loaded from Qatar's Ras Laffan hub, despite it still being under force majeure. In two cases, per Kpler, LNG cargoes loaded at Ras Laffan were then transferred to other tankers once the original vessels had cleared the Strait of Hormuz.
Despite the increase in loadings, the total from Qatar is far from pre-war levels. In February, Qatar's total LNG exports hit close to 6.2 million tons, Kpler said, while the October total stood at 2.7 million tons.
Unlike Qatar, the UAE has managed to restore its exports of liquefied natural gas to pre-war levels, the data suggests. October loadings from Das Island hit seven, unchanged from September.
Iranian attacks on U.S. allies in the Gulf took out 17% of Qatar's export capacity and caused an estimated loss of $20 billion in annual revenue, QatarEnergy said in March. Since then, the company has been scrambling to find ways to service its contracts, despite the force majeure.
Recently, in an unusual twist , Qatar became the largest buyer of U.S. liquefied gas, after sealing two long-term deals with Cheniere Energy to buy up to 4 million tons per year of LNG from Cheniere's Sabine Pass and Corpus Christi export facilities, with deliveries expected to begin in 2031 and continue through 2054.
Despite the recovery in LNG flows out of the Persian Gulf, Rystad Energy earlier this week warned that this winter in the Northern Hemisphere will be extremely uncertain in terms of energy security, with a high likelihood of much higher gas prices because of the tight supply.

Gasoline was an exception to the missing survey data. IAEA chief Grossi told the UN Security Council in late October that the body was unable to verify Iran’s nuclear material because Tehran “has not provided the requested information and access”.
Ali Vaez, deputy director of the Middle East and North Africa programme at the International Crisis Group, said Tehran is keeping one foot inside the NPT and the other near the exit.
“Allowing inspections at Bushehr is a low-cost gesture designed to keep diplomacy alive and hardliners at bay, while preserving leverage over the underground nuclear sites that matter most,” he told Al Jazeera.
Those sensitive sites include the Natanz, Fordow and Isfahan facilities, among others.
Seyed Hossein Mousavian, a visiting researcher at Princeton University and a former Iranian nuclear negotiator, said Iran’s position “reflects a distinction between honouring its NPT commitments and permitting inspections of facilities that have been bombed”.
Mousavian said the IAEA had no established inspection protocol for nuclear facilities damaged by military attacks, and pointed out that the Iranians fear that IAEA access to sensitive sites would lead to information being shared with the US and Israel that could then be used in future attacks.
“Tehran views restrictions on broader inspections as a security concern rather than necessarily a rejection of its NPT obligations,” he said.
Iran has had one of the most scrutinised nuclear programmes in history, and its international obligations are governed by several treaties and UN resolutions.
The NPT establishes the fundamental rules against nuclear proliferation, while recognising the right to peaceful nuclear energy. A separate safeguards agreement with the IAEA sets out how the agency monitors Iran’s nuclear activities to make sure it isn’t moving towards weapons development. Subsidiary arrangements, UN Security Council resolutions and nuclear agreements have fulfilled additional requirements.
As a party to the NPT, Iran’s peaceful nuclear-use rights are recognised, and it is allowed to undertake peaceful exchanges and receive assistance with nuclear development.
In exchange, the country commits not to receive, manufacture or otherwise acquire nuclear weapons, and to accept safeguards and information sharing in its territory.
Withdrawal requires a notice after a decision has been made that “extraordinary events” have “jeopardised the supreme interests” of the country.
However, the treaty does not protect from conventional military attack, and this gap between promised peaceful-use benefits and actual vulnerability has been at the centre of the appeal by domestic hardliners to leave the NPT.
But such considerations have not been limited to a handful of hawkish figures.
Iran’s security chief Mohsen Rezaei has publicly deliberated the possibility of abandoning the treaty on multiple occasions. Hossein Mohebbi, spokesman of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), said earlier this week that the organisation “will create the necessary preparations” if the Islamic Republic goes through with the decision to change its nuclear doctrine.
Regional mediators like Qatar and Pakistan continue to relay messages between Iran and the US to prevent another all-out war, although analysts have warned that escalation is likely over the coming months.
Tehran has maintained that it will not allow its highly enriched uranium to be taken out of the country, but remains open to downblending it under international supervision inside Iran. It has also said it will not abandon enrichment altogether, but can accept a limit on enrichment as it did with the 2015 nuclear deal that US President Donald Trump unilaterally abandoned in 2018, during his first term in office.
Arriving at a deal that thoroughly addresses Iran’s nuclear programme would also require a resolution to hostilities in the Strait of Hormuz and beyond, something that is unlikely to materialise in the foreseeable future.
“Tehran might be willing to open the door a crack in exchange for tangible American concessions, but it is unlikely to surrender its most valuable nuclear bargaining chips upfront,” the Crisis Group’s Vaez said.
The expert added that a preliminary deal could potentially unlock limited, phased inspections based on the 2025 Cairo agreement between Iran and the IAEA.
The agreement came several months after the US and Israel bombed Iran’s nuclear facilities during the 12-day war of June 2025. It envisaged restored access for agency inspectors to all Iranian facilities, if case-by-case permission is granted by the country’s Supreme National Security Council.
But it was never enforced, and was followed by tensions that led to the US and Israel attacking Iran again on February 28.
“Restoring meaningful transparency over Iran’s nuclear programme will require a larger bargain on enrichment, stockpiles and sanctions relief,” Vaez said.

“Such cooperation would require a comprehensive agreement that addresses the overall security and nuclear issues; without it, inspections of the bombed sites are unlikely,” he said.
