I Saw Ukraine’s Rapid Adaptation Cycle in Action, and This is What the US Military Should Learn from It
During my time in Ukraine I saw more than a country at war. I saw a defense innovation ecosystem in which the daily imperative to survive drove rapid learning and adaptation. It also exposed a serious weakness in the American model of military technology development. A Ukrainian drone can be jammed, modified to counter the jamming, and sent back into the fight in less time than it takes the US defense system to process a request. That gap is more than a bureaucratic frustration. It is a strategic vulnerability.
When Russian forces jammed a drone’s signal, Ukrainian engineers often developed a fix within days, and sometimes within hours. They changed frequencies, replaced antennas, or adopted new navigation methods. Once the solution worked, it got back to the front quickly. In many cases the drone was flying again within a day or two. As I watched this responsiveness in action, I kept comparing it to how the same problem would move through our system. A US unit that identifies an urgent requirement for change submits a request. The issue enters a process built for review and control. If it is serious enough it may be classified as a Joint Urgent Operational Need or a Joint Emergent Operational Need. Even on that accelerated track it can take up to a year to field an initial solution and as long as two years to deploy it fully. By then the threat may have changed several times. The system will have done what it was designed to do, but too slowly to matter.
The problem is that we do not evaluate our defense industry, or our own institutions, based on how well they adapt when conditions change. We evaluate them based on how well they perform against requirements written in advance. The central flaw is not a lack of talent or technology. It is that we reward compliance over adaptation. We write a requirement, give it to industry, and then judge the final product against that same requirement. In effect we provide the answer key and then reward companies for following it. That approach may work for predictable procurement. It does not work for war.
Even our existing rapid acquisition pathways, including the Middle Tier of Acquisition and urgent operational needs processes, still largely judge solutions against requirements written in advance. They can accelerate delivery of a planned solution, but they do not systematically measure or reward a company’s ability to adapt when the threat changes after the requirement is locked. War is dynamic. Adversaries do not announce their next moves, and the most dangerous threats are often the ones that did not exist when the requirement was drafted. The jamming frequencies used against US systems in a future conflict will not match the ones described in a document written years earlier. They will be the ones an adversary developed last week. Yet we continue to favor companies skilled at preparing for planned events rather than those capable of solving new problems quickly. These skill sets are fundamentally different. One produces compliance. The other fosters adaptation. Innovation experts describe this as the adaptability gap , the distance between how much an organization claims to value adaptability and how much it actually invests in it. In the US defense sector this gap has become structural.
If the threat changes in real time, the Department of Defense’s evaluation model must change with it. Rather than judging fixed products against static standards alone, the department should assess a company’s broader innovation ecosystem and its ability to adapt capability at operational speed. To implement this change, the department should update DFARS 215.304 . That provision already governs evaluation factors used in departmental source selections and provides precedents for assessing attributes beyond the immediate end item, including small business participation, manufacturing readiness, supply chain risk, sustainment, and supplier risk. The regulation therefore already recognizes that a company’s ability to deliver capability depends on more than whether a product meets a point-in-time specification. The central question should be whether its engineers, software teams, production lines, legal staff, and product designers can absorb new battlefield data and convert it into a workable solution in weeks rather than years.
That shift requires new criteria. Contracts could specify maximum two-week turnaround for software updates. This timeline is fast enough to drive real change from today’s multimonth cycles, yet realistic enough to assess responsiveness. Procurement processes could include demonstration phases in which companies must design, test, and field modifications within defined periods using recent threat scenarios. The goal is not to weaken standards. It is to operationalize them in alignment with the demands of modern conflict.
The Department of Defense already has both an immediate mechanism to implement this change and an enduring organization to scale it. Under Project Agincourt, the Direct Reporting Portfolio Manager for Unmanned Systems (DRPM-UxS) is directed to lead the near-term transition and develop the pathway to the Autonomous Warfare Command . While carrying out its current responsibilities, the DRPM-UxS can pilot a revised acquisition model for testing adaptability.
Consider how we often test autonomous systems today. Requirements may state that platforms must operate in a contested electronic warfare environment. Manufacturers arrive at the test range already knowing the frequencies they will face. They prepare for those expected conditions, pass the test, and move toward procurement. A more realistic evaluation would expose the system to the latest electronic warfare signals captured from active battlefields, signals that may not have existed when the requirements were drafted. The system will likely fail. That is not the end of the test. It is the beginning of the real one. At that point we should measure how quickly a company can diagnose the problem, rewrite the software, validate the fix, and scale it into production. If that timeline is measured in years rather than weeks, the company should not win the contract.
Some will argue that the United States is not at war and therefore lacks access to the right battlefield data. That is not a sufficient excuse. Ukrainian partners confront these threats every day. They offer two clear ways to make our testing more realistic. The first is data. Ukrainian partners can share current threat information, from captured jamming signals to evolving enemy tactics, that can keep US testing relevant. The second is access to the environment itself. Ukraine’s Brave1 defense technology cluster runs a program called Test in Ukraine that allows systems to be exposed to live Russian threats in operational conditions. That model is already expanding. New initiatives linking Ukrainian defense firms with NATO partners are creating pathways for allied companies to test systems against real-world threats and learn from one of the most combat-tested innovation ecosystems in the world. The infrastructure for more realistic testing is not hypothetical. It is already taking shape.
This approach comes with legal, security, and intelligence-sharing challenges. Sensitive battlefield data cannot simply move across borders without safeguards. While such concerns are valid, they are implementation problems, not reasons for inaction. With the right protocols and close coordination, realistic testing is possible.
This approach will fail, however, if the Department of Defense demands adaptability from industry while exempting itself from the same standard. The department is emphasizing adaptation, but meanwhile, regulations and policy lag. Today’s incentive structures do not reward program managers for learning quickly. They reward them for clean audits, predictable timelines, and avoiding budget turbulence.
During my time in Special Forces acquisition we had a simple motto: Fail fast. Learn quickly whether something works, stop funding what does not, and move resources to what does. Scaling that mindset across the broader defense enterprise will not be easy, but it is necessary. Performance metrics should track how quickly teams respond to emerging threats and how effectively they adapt. Pilot programs should reward timely iteration, even when it includes short-term failure. Promotions and recognition should go to leaders who move quickly and extract useful lessons from rapid testing, not just those who hit traditional milestones. If we are serious about closing the adaptability gap, we must be willing to accept the risk and invest the necessary money, time, and people.
Testing for adaptability does involve risk. Some tests will fail. Some funding will go to efforts that do not succeed. That is not waste. It is the cost of learning faster than the adversary. A culture that treats every failed test as a scandal will produce a military that looks efficient on paper and loses in practice.
Ukraine did not build an adaptive defense industrial base because it had perfectly written requirements. It built one because it had no alternative. The old model would have cost it the war, so Ukraine replaced it with an ecosystem that learns under pressure. The United States still has time to reform how it tests, fields, and rewards innovation. But that window is narrowing. Russia is adapting in real time, and China is building the industrial and mobilization capacity to outproduce and outlast its opponents in a high-intensity fight—a scale the United States is not yet built to match. Beijing’s evolving industrial playbook is taking shape in Morocco.

In recent years, Europe’s auto industry has reeled from the influx of low-cost, high-quality Chinese electric vehicles (EVs). Even as Brussels imposes tariffs on Chinese-made EVs, their market share only continues to grow. As of December, more than 9 percent of all new automobiles sold in Europe were made in China, with some estimates expecting Chinese EVs to account for roughly 20 percent of the Western European market by 2028.
Brussels’s retaliatory tariffs reflect a common Western view that high-value Chinese products are competitive in global markets largely because of state subsidies . To be sure, China’s EV industry has benefited immensely from Beijing’s subsidies, easy credit, and tax relief. That explanation, however, is far too narrow and risks severely underestimating the scale of the challenge ahead.
The more consequential story—that will likely make Chinese EVs even more competitive in the near future—is Beijing’s evolving approach to overseas industrial policy. At the core of this approach is an increasingly coordinated model of public- and private-sector activity.
State-owned enterprises (SOE) lay the groundwork for favorable investment environments abroad by undertaking massive infrastructure projects and securing access to strategic resources. Boosted by Beijing’s diplomatic ties and international clout, private companies leverage these state-backed platforms as a springboard for trade by bringing in investment, production capacity, and industry-specific expertise.
The advantages of this approach for Beijing—and the challenge that it poses to foreign companies—are already on full display in Morocco, where years of existing strategic investment, infrastructure development, and geopolitical maneuvering are pushing China closer to vertically integrating EV supply chains, bypassing tariffs, and further reshaping Europe’s auto industry.
It is only in recent years that Morocco has emerged as a key node in China’s EV empire.
For decades, Beijing maintained much closer ties with Morocco’s neighbor and rival, Algeria, but that began to change in the early 2010s as the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) took off. In 2016, Morocco joined the growing ranks of countries signing “strategic partnerships” with China, making mutual commitments to expand economic cooperation and recognize each other’s core interests.
The next year, Morocco officially entered the BRI, and China has since poured millions of dollars into Morocco’s energy and transportation sectors . Almost a decade later, that economic relationship has expanded dramatically: In March, China’s committed investment in the country exceeded $10 billion , concentrated mostly in the EV and energy sectors.
China’s EV manufacturing capacity in Morocco builds on years of large-scale, state-backed investment in transportation infrastructure and natural resources. Over the past decade, Chinese SOEs have collaborated on major infrastructure and development projects in Morocco, including bridges , highways , and smart cities . Some of these are directly relevant to the EV industry, such as the chemical processing unit in Khouribga, home to one of the world’s biggest reserves of phosphate, which is critical for EV battery production .
This SOE-led scramble for resources and construction-heavy projects paved the way for private Chinese companies in the EV sector to establish footholds in Morocco and ship to overseas markets. Tanger Tech City, China’s SOE-led flagship collaboration with Morocco, is located less than 30 miles from Tanger Med, the largest container port in Africa. Taking advantage of no-fee business registration and tax-free dividends, private Chinese companies—including the global battery giant BTR New Material Group as well as tire manufacturers Sentury Tire and Guizhou Tyre—have established or announced plans to establish production bases in the industrial zone.
Elsewhere in the country, major private investments include battery manufacturer Gotion’s $1.3 billion factory and a $2 billion joint-venture battery materials factory between China’s CNGR Advanced Material and the Moroccan private equity firm Al Mada.
This isn’t to deny the role of subsidies and preferential financing, which Beijing sees as key tools for supporting companies that are expanding internationally. A 2024 directive , for instance, called on Chinese financial institutions to improve credit approval and lending procedures and to enhance cross-border settlement to “better support companies expanding into international markets.” Closer collaboration among Chinese banks, semi-official trade organizations, and Moroccan banks has also helped to streamline financing and channel investment into the African market.
But these policies are useful to Chinese firms because they are operating in an industrial landscape that SOEs have already shaped, with relatively easy access to necessary resources and infrastructure.
China’s success with this approach has only been possible because it also advances Rabat’s desire to expand domestic manufacturing capability and position Morocco as Africa’s next industrial powerhouse. Chinese investment is expected to generate sorely needed jobs; Morocco’s unemployment rate has hardly dipped below 10 percent for the past decade, and youth unemployment in cities stands at a staggering 47 percent. Officials claim that the Gotion factory alone will create 17,000 local jobs, including 2,300 high-skilled positions.
Beijing has coupled this economic engagement with a subtle diplomatic shift in favor of Rabat’s claims over Western Sahara . For decades, China has avoided explicitly backing either Morocco or Western Sahara, though its rhetoric often leaned toward the latter—and thus to the latter’s main sponsor, Algiers . With one exception in 2018, it consistently voted in favor of United Nations Security Council resolutions extending the peacekeeping mission in Western Sahara and affirming support for the Sahrawi people’s right to self-determination.
However, in recent years, China has done an about-face. In 2024, its Ministry of Foreign Affairs implicitly acknowledged the importance of the Western Sahara question for Rabat, stating , “China supports Morocco in maintaining national security and stability and is ready to work with Morocco to continue firmly supporting each other on issues concerning their respective core interests.” Then, last fall, Beijing abstained on the most recent U.N. resolution on the matter, which for the first time endorsed Rabat’s proposal to formally establish Western Sahara as an autonomous region under Moroccan sovereignty.
The full payoff of China’s Moroccan gambit will materialize if and when localized EV manufacturing matures, which could take years. That is still a tall order, as evidenced by the recent shelving of a BYD assembly plant project that had been stalled since 2017 due to budget issues and limited local charging infrastructure, according to a company executive.
Though Chinese automakers are not yet building assembly plants in Morocco, the country matters for everything “upstream” of assembly. It is becoming an integrated ecosystem for the production of batteries, tires, and other components, which reduces Chinese EV companies’ reliance on direct shipments from China and lowers costs for Chinese assembly plants in Europe and Africa.
Morocco also offers Chinese firms convenient access to the European market through the Tanger Med port and—more importantly—a rules-based pathway through Brussels’s tightening trade defenses . Currently, the EU imposes duties of up to 35 percent on EVs manufactured in China. However, vehicles that qualify as Moroccan in origin can enter the European market duty-free. That includes vehicles assembled in Morocco, so long as foreign parts make up less than 45 percent of the finished vehicle’s ex-works price.
In short, if Chinese EV companies succeed in establishing vehicle assembly factories and fully integrating supply chains in Morocco, they will be able to sell vehicles to Europe under a “Made in Morocco” label, avoiding both the 10 percent EU standard automobile tariff and the additional punitive tariff on Chinese-made EVs—reducing the total tariff effect from as much as 45 percent to zero. This would pose an unprecedented threat to what remains one of the EU’s most important industries .
China is rolling out this coordinated approach to overseas industrial policy elsewhere to varying degrees.
In Thailand, for example, China is a key investor in the Eastern Economic Corridor, a flagship regional development initiative anchored by a high-speed railway that will connect several Thai cities and seaports to China. The corridor has, in turn, provided an attractive environment for private Chinese investment. China is now the corridor’s largest source of foreign direct investment, primarily in the EV sector and related industries. Like Tanger Tech City, the corridor offers private Chinese companies major tax incentives and more convenient access to the Association of Southeast Asian Nations’ market.
The United Arab Emirates offers another example. In 2017, Chinese SOE Jiangsu Provincial Overseas Cooperation and Investment Company Limited (JOCIC) partnered with Abu Dhabi Ports to develop an industrial and special economic zone inside the Khalifa Port’s free trade zone. JOCIC set up a wholly owned UAE subsidiary to manage the park, which lets it attract Chinese private capital while insulating the parent company from project-level and geopolitical risk. The park’s location provides Chinese companies with a convenient base for expansion into the Persian Gulf and wider Middle Eastern markets, along with exemptions from value-added tax and other financial incentives . As of 2025, more than 40 Chinese companies had collectively committed more than $700 million to the park, with a focus on EVs and renewable energy.
Beijing has long seen its SOEs as tools for advancing its broader economic interests. As China in recent years has emphasized “small and beautiful” projects , the SOE-led megaprojects are often viewed as commercially disappointing holdovers from an earlier and more profligate era of BRI. Nevertheless, under this coordinated strategy, Beijing has been able to capitalize on the advantages secured by past infrastructure projects and deepen its presence in the overseas market.
As governments in industrialized economies respond to China’s surge of high-value manufactured exports, they must recognize how the sources of Chinese firms’ advantages are changing. Today, those advantages come not just from subsidies and cheap credit at home but also from integrated ecosystems abroad, built through coordination between public and private enterprise.
This means that Brussels’s retaliatory tariffs and the recently announced limits on hybrid EVs imported from China, on their own, are unlikely to stem the tide of EVs made by Chinese firms operating around the world. China’s rivals—even in industrialized economies—will struggle to compete unless they can coordinate across borders and build supply chains and infrastructure that leverage or, far less plausibly, completely sidestep what China has already put in place.

Warren Buffett built one of the largest fortunes in history by picking stocks. Yet the advice he has given ordinary investors for decades sounds almost boring next to his own record. That boring advice keeps giving. The S&P 500 set a fresh record high in early October, and Buffett's go-to recommendation continues to pay off for patient investors. The bigger story is how many people have taken his words to heart.

He repeated the message at Berkshire's 2020 annual meeting. "In my view, for most people, the best thing to do is owning the S&P 500 index fund," Buffett said. He added that nobody knows what the market will do tomorrow, next week or next year.
