Despite the Iran war and the Hormuz crisis, the Middle East’s low-cost chemicals production is set to retain its long-term advantage over other regions.
Near-term shifts in the sourcing of polymers are taking place due to the current constraints in the Persian Gulf’s industrial base, benefiting U.S. chemicals companies.
But in the long run, the Middle East is likely to retain its buyers’ base because it still has the two levers to be competitive on the global market—an extensive production base and, most of all, low-cost production with abundant naphtha supply, industry analysts and executives tell Reuters .
Many buyers have moved to diversify their polymer supply due to the Middle East crisis and the fewer volumes they have received through the Strait of Hormuz since the Iran war began at the end of February.
But many customers see the diversification as protection against the current crisis, not a replacement in the long run, as the low prices of the Middle East’s polymers trump risks to supply for most.

If you want to position your portfolio to recover in less than half the time it takes for others, here&#x27;s exactly what you should do.
It doesn&#x27;t take any groundbreaking portfolio strategy to produce rapid recoveries in the event of a stock market crash. Investors can hold a simple 60/40 stock/bond portfolio with periodic rebalancing and achieve superior results in maintaining portfolio stability.
Since 1988, the 60/40 portfolio has recovered from drawdowns of 20% or more in just 11 months on average, according to data compiled by J.P. Morgan analysts. Meanwhile, the S&P 500 took an average of 24 months to recover, even with dividends reinvested.
It&#x27;s worth noting that the 60/40 portfolio only stands out against an all-stock portfolio in terms of recovery when the portfolio drops at least 20%. That said, that&#x27;s exactly the kind of protection an investor should hope for from bonds : a stabilizing force amid big long drops without providing too much drag amid shorter pullbacks.

Since 1989, the 60/40 portfolio has produced a positive return 99% of the time over any five-year period and 100% of the time over 10 years. The S&P 500 alone only achieves positive returns 87% of the time over five years and 93% of the time over 10 years.
Maintaining a 60/40 ratio between stocks and bonds in your portfolio requires periodic rebalancing. When more volatile stocks crash, you&#x27;ll end up with a lower ratio of stocks to bonds in your portfolio. You&#x27;ll have to sell some bonds and buy some stocks to return the portfolio to the 60/40 balance.
This act of rebalancing means you automatically buy stocks when they go down. The reverse is also true; you&#x27;ll sell stocks when they climb higher or if bonds decline. That means you can be opportunistic in buying and selling stocks , which ultimately produces more stable returns and helps a portfolio recover quickly after a crash.

Google Teases Synaptics Edge AI Chip, Cuts Power 40% [2026]
Google used its annual Research@ event in Mountain View, California, on October 14, 2026, to preview a new low-power AI chip built with Synaptics, setting up a formal unveiling at Synaptics' Tech Day in San Jose on October 20. The chip pairs Google's Coral NPU M2 with Synaptics' Torq NPU and Arm CPU cores, and the companies say it cuts energy use by 40% compared with existing edge AI processors. Synaptics CEO Rahul Patel said the chip will not stay confined to lab demos. According to a report from R&D World , Patel told attendees it will also be built into Google devices, though neither company named which products or when they would ship.
The preview lands at a moment when chipmakers are racing to put AI inference directly on wearables, smart glasses, and industrial sensors instead of routing every request to the cloud. Google and Synaptics are betting that sub-6-milliwatt inference, paired with an open compiler stack, can pull developers away from the proprietary microcontroller software they have relied on for years. The timing also puts pressure on rivals already shipping edge AI silicon at scale, from Qualcomm's Snapdragon line to smaller microcontroller specialists like Ambiq, NXP, and STMicroelectronics.
Google Research's Research@ event is the company's internal showcase for work still moving from lab to product, and this year's edition gave Synaptics a stage to describe a chip that had been in development for roughly 18 to 24 months. The device belongs to Synaptics' Astra line of AI-native processors and microcontrollers, a product family the company has built specifically for local, low-power AI workloads rather than general-purpose computing.
Patel used the Mountain View appearance to lay out the chip's capabilities without disclosing full specifications, a sequencing choice that let Google tie the story to its broader research narrative before Synaptics controls the commercial unveiling six days later. The chip can process voice, vision, motion, and contextual sensing data simultaneously, and it adds GPU and multimedia processing along with hardware-rooted security, according to the companies' public statements cited in a report from SBS .
The headline number from the preview is a claimed 40% cut in energy use for Google's Coral NPU M2, the successor to the Coral neural processing unit Google introduced the prior year. Reporting tied to the announcement, including coverage from ChosunBiz , describes M2 running on-device inference at an average power draw of less than 6 milliwatts. Neither Google nor Synaptics has specified the exact workloads used for that comparison, or which prior-generation processor served as the baseline, so the 40% figure should be read as a vendor claim rather than an independently benchmarked result.
That caveat matters because power-efficiency claims in the chip industry are notoriously workload-dependent. A processor that looks dramatically more efficient on a simple keyword-spotting task can lose much of its edge on continuous multimodal sensing, which is exactly the use case Google and Synaptics are targeting. Until Synaptics publishes a full datasheet, outside engineers will not be able to verify the 40% number against a defined test suite.

Synaptics did not simply license Google's silicon design and ship it unchanged. The Astra chip combines the Coral NPU M2 with Synaptics' own Torq NPU and standard Arm CPU cores, giving the company a path to differentiate on top of Google's open accelerator work. That architecture lets Synaptics target a wide span of constrained devices, from battery-powered wearables to fixed industrial cameras, with one underlying platform rather than a separate chip for each category.
A less flashy but arguably more consequential part of the announcement is software. Synaptics and Google Research spent roughly 18 months building an open-source compiler on top of MLIR, the Multi-Level Intermediate Representation framework, so the chip can run standard machine learning frameworks and model formats rather than locking developers into a proprietary toolchain. Patel framed this as a break from the walled-garden software that has defined microcontroller development for years, a point echoed in R&D World's account of his remarks. Developers can already prototype against the underlying Coral NPU using Coralboard, a development platform Synaptics launched earlier this year that pairs the NPU with Astra processors ahead of the full chip's release.
Coral's first milestone was a front end built on RISC-V, the open instruction set architecture that has gained ground across the chip industry as an alternative to proprietary cores. M2 marks the second milestone and adds what Google describes as the first Coral vector processor. Google Research has also said its next design, M3, is already complete and adds native 32-bit floating-point support so the chip can run Gemma 3 270M, a compact 270-million-parameter language model, directly on-device. Neither company has given a shipping date for M3-based silicon, which means the chip debuting this month is already one generation behind Google's internal roadmap.
Rahul Patel's Pitch: From Lab Bench to Google Hardware
Patel's most direct claim from the Research@ appearance was that the chip will not stay in the lab and will also be built into Google devices, a statement widely picked up across outlets covering the preview. He also highlighted the microcontroller's support for high-density DRAM, describing memory capacity as a source of scalability for future products built on the same platform. Synaptics plans to formally unveil the chip at its Tech Day event on October 20 at 1151 McKay Drive in San Jose, which the company has billed as including a noon press and analyst briefing followed by a demonstration showcase.
What Patel did not say is just as notable. He declined to identify which Google hardware lines would adopt the chip or offer any release window, leaving a gap between the marketing claim and a verifiable product commitment. That gap is where skepticism from hardware analysts tends to concentrate, since chipmakers routinely tout future design wins that take years, or never materialize, to reach shelves.
Why Google Keeps Investing in Edge Silicon Partnerships
Google's data center strategy has long rested on designing its own Tensor Processing Units alongside the models that run on them, and the company is now pushing the same co-design philosophy to the edge. The logic, as described in reporting on the Research@ event, is that existing edge chips consume too much energy and respond too slowly for always-on, ambient AI experiences that keep personal data on the device instead of sending it to the cloud. A fragmented market of proprietary microcontroller designs compounds the problem by making it harder for developers to build once and deploy across multiple vendors' hardware.
That framing explains why Google chose a partnership model rather than building its own edge chip from scratch. Synaptics brings decades of microcontroller and sensor-fusion experience along with existing customer relationships in wearables and industrial equipment, while Google supplies the Coral accelerator design and compiler work it has already validated internally.
Billy Rutledge Makes the Case for Co-Designed Hardware
When Google and Synaptics first announced their collaboration on edge AI for the Internet of Things, Billy Rutledge, Director of Systems Research at Google Research, laid out why the two companies paired up in the first place. "Synaptics' embrace of open software and tools and proven AI hardware makes the Astra portfolio a natural fit for our ML core as we ramp to meet the uniquely challenging power, performance, cost, and space requirements of Edge AI devices," Rutledge said in the companies' joint statement announcing the partnership.
Rutledge closed that announcement with a line that reads differently now that the chip has reached a public preview. "We look forward to working together to bring our capabilities to the broad market," he said. Nearly two years of engineering separate that statement from this month's Research@ demonstration, which is roughly in line with the 18-to-24-month development window Patel cited for the project.

Target Devices: Wearables, Smart Glasses, and the Industrial Floor
The use cases Google and Synaptics are chasing span two very different markets. On the consumer side, smart glasses, watches, and connected cameras need to recognize speech, gestures, and context without draining a small battery in a few hours. On the industrial side, vision and voice systems on factory floors need similar always-on sensing but with tighter requirements around reliability and hardware-rooted security, since these devices often sit on networks that also touch safety-critical equipment.
Covering both markets with one chip platform is an ambitious bet. It means Synaptics has to hit aggressive power targets for battery-powered wearables while also meeting the durability and security expectations of industrial buyers, all on the same underlying silicon. If the company pulls it off, Astra could become a default reference design that OEMs reach for instead of commissioning custom edge AI silicon.

Google's interest in specialized AI silicon traces back to a 2017 Google research paper on its first-generation Tensor Processing Unit, which reported performance-per-watt gains of 30 to 80 times over the Intel Haswell CPUs and Nvidia K80 GPUs it was benchmarked against in production neural-network inference workloads, per R&D World's account of the comparison. The full quarter looks better. The S&P 500 has averaged a 6.6% gain in the fourth quarter of midterm years , finishing higher in 16 of 19.
2026 is a midterm year, and Americans vote on Tuesday, November 3.
