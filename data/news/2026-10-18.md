Middle East Chemicals Keep Their Cost Edge Despite Hormuz Crisis
Middle Eastern petrochemical producers retain a major cost advantage, despite the Iran war disrupting exports and forcing buyers to diversify supplies.
U.S. chemical companies are benefiting from the crisis, with plastics prices surging over 50% and Dow reporting a 27% jump in quarterly plastics segment sales.
- The Gulf's dominance is unlikely to fade, as abundant low-cost feedstocks and massive production capacity keep buyers dependent on the region.
Despite the Iran war and the Hormuz crisis, the Middle East’s low-cost chemicals production is set to retain its long-term advantage over other regions.
Near-term shifts in the sourcing of polymers are taking place due to the current constraints in the Persian Gulf’s industrial base, benefiting U.S. chemicals companies.
But in the long run, the Middle East is likely to retain its buyers’ base because it still has the two levers to be competitive on the global market—an extensive production base and, most of all, low-cost production with abundant naphtha supply, industry analysts and executives tell Reuters .
Many buyers have moved to diversify their polymer supply due to the Middle East crisis and the fewer volumes they have received through the Strait of Hormuz since the Iran war began at the end of February.
But many customers see the diversification as protection against the current crisis, not a replacement in the long run, as the low prices of the Middle East’s polymers trump risks to supply for most.

The Middle East is the world’s biggest exporter of polyethylene, the most used plastic globally.
Exports of polyethylene from the Middle East accounted for 43% of all global exports of the polymer last year, data from the International Trader Publications showed earlier this year. The Middle East exported 18.7 million tons out of total global exports of 43.8 million tons in 2025.
North America was the second-largest polyethylene exporter last year with 15.6 million tons. Because of secure feedstocks and ample supply, North America is set to fill some of the supply gap amid the Hormuz crisis, according to International Trader Publications.

Egypt’s parliament is convening on Sunday after President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi requested an emergency session to approve defensive military aid to Saudi Arabia, if Riyadh asks for support in its war with Yemen’s Houthis.

El-Sisi declared that Egypt will “carry out any measure to protect” Saudi Arabia, in a speech on Saturday, but said that he “hopes” the parliament will give him authorisation to proceed.
On Sunday, Egypt said it would provide “all necessary and possible support to Saudi Arabia to defend its safety and stability”. The National Defence Council, chaired by President el-Sisi, said it “stands fully with” Saudi Arabia to defend its resources, territorial integrity and Islamic holy sites.
But what would Egyptian assistance look like? And what capacity does it have to “protect” Saudi Arabia in its war with the Houthis?
What’s behind el-Sisi’s decision to offer help to Saudi Arabia?
Saudi Arabia has long been a strategic and economic partner of Egypt, and el-Sisi said in a statement last month that Saudi security is “integral” to Egypt’s security.
Political analyst and Gulf studies expert Thomas Bonnie James told Al Jazeera that the Houthi security threat to the kingdom is one of the “converging” pressures that have led to this decision. The other one, is economics.
“Houthi pressure on [Bab] al-Mandeb threatens the Suez Canal , which Egypt’s foreign minister says has lost about $11bn since the attacks began in 2023,” he said, referring to Houthi attacks on Israeli-linked ships passing through the narrow strait, carried out in solidarity with Gaza.
James suggested that the decision is also important to Cairo, because “millions of Egyptians live and work in Saudi Arabia, which is Egypt’s largest single source of remittances. An Egyptian resident was killed, and others were injured in the attacks on Riyadh’s airport.”
He also said that Egypt does not want to be seen as the regional ally that “stood aside while others defended the kingdom,” as Turkiye and Pakistan have made their own pledges after activating the Mecca defence pact signed in August amid the deteriorating security situation in the Middle East.
Hostilities in Yemen continue to escalate. Two air raids by the Saudi-led coalition hit Hodeidah in the southwest on Sunday.
Saudi forces also intercepted drones near Riyadh and Eastern provinces. The capital’s King Khalid international airport has come under attack at least 20 times since fighting flared between the internationally recognised government and the Houthis, also known as Ansar Allah – Arabic for Supporters of God.
The Saudi-backed forces are fighting the Houthis on several fronts, including Taiz and Marib, and some analysts argue the Houthis are stretched thin after their summer gains.
But James told Al Jazeera “that defeating them outright is a tall order”.
“Ground forces backed by air power can retake the coastline; stopping missiles launched from the mountains is much harder,” he said.
They will be convening on Sunday at 1pm local time (10am GMT).
Under the Egyptian law, the President cannot declare war or send the military on foreign missions without the approval from the parliament.
For the authorisation to be granted, at least two-thirds of the politicians in the House of Representatives must vote in favour of the proposal.
Does Egypt have security cooperation with Saudi Arabia?
Not exactly. Egypt and Saudi Arabia have not signed a formal bilateral or multilateral pact on defence cooperation. But Cairo has a long history of cooperation with Riyadh.
Egypt is a founding member of the Multinational Maritime Defense Coalition, which Saudi Arabia launched in July. It aims to protect shipping in the Red Sea and Bab al-Mandeb – territories on Yemen’s coastline. Houthis captured towns and cities near Bab al-Mandeb last month, but the internationally recognised government has since wrested control of some of the areas from the Iran-backed group.
Egypt also participated in joint military exercises with Saudi Arabia in 2024.
Cairo was also part of a 2015 coalition , cobled together after the Houthi group captured large areas of north and western Yemen, including the capital Sanaa in September 2024. Egypt contributed naval and air forces to help secure maritime corridors in the Red Sea against the Houthis.
But James, the Gulf studies expert, told Al Jazeera that what el-Sisi is proposing is different from the maritime defence coalition Egypt recently joined.
“He is hoping for authorisation to deploy Egyptian forces to help defend Saudi territory itself,” he said. “It would move Egypt from a multilateral maritime framework to a bilateral military commitment.”
What will be the nature of Egyptian assistance to Saudi Arabia?
El-Sisi explicitly said that he is only proposing “defence” and that he isn’t “speaking in order for us to fight anyone or attack anyone”.
Military Defence Analyst Alexandru Hudisteanu said that the use of counter-drones and anti-missile support is a high probability if the decision is authorised.
“It would be the most urgent need at this point to make sure that the defence of Saudi Arabia is backed up and reinforced,” he told Al Jazeera.
Simultaneously, he added that the request itself is also a strategic move of deterrence.
“We know Saudi Arabia has one of the most prolific militaries out there in terms of capacities and capabilities. It’s a matter of the territory which Saudi Arabia needs to cover at any given moment,” Hudisteanu said.
James said that any contribution by Egypt would focus on the protection of Saudi territory, using “air and missile defence around critical infrastructure, intercepting drones, naval escort and intelligence sharing”.
James said that it is unlikely Egypt will deploy troops to Yemen’s front lines.
In the 1960s, former Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser deployed tens of thousands of troops in the Yemen Civil War. Saudi Arabia backed the rival side. Egypt suffered heavy casualties and Saudi-Egyptian ties were strained.
“What Egypt will not want is to send troops into Yemen. Nasser’s intervention there in the 1960s sends a clear message: defend the kingdom, don’t take ownership of Yemen,” he told Al Jazeera.

President Donald Trump signed an executive order Friday that waives many of the sanctions in the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, which Congress passed in the late senator’s name last month. The waiver, coming a week after Trump announced a diesel deal with Russia, also underscores fraying relations between the U.S. and Ukraine.
The law, which was set to take effect Oct. 18, called for sanctions targeting Russian officials, financial institutions, and other entities, alongside tariffs of up to 100% on goods imported into the U.S. from major buyers of Russian crude oil or natural gas. Those measures were intended to restrict the financial resources supporting Russia’s war against Ukraine.
The U.K.’s Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office estimated in June 2025 that international sanctions had deprived the Russian state of at least $450 billion in war funds since February 2022, including through lost oil tax revenues and immobilized central-bank reserves.
While Trump’s waiver leaves existing sanctions in place, it blocks the additional pressure the new measures were intended to exert.
Trump has also pushed Ukraine to scale back its own campaign against Russia’s energy sector, repeatedly urging Kyiv to halt strikes on Russian fuel infrastructure and suggesting that those attacks are driving up U.S. fuel prices. But Ukrainian strikes are only one factor affecting fuel prices; disruptions linked to the war with Iran are largely responsible for constraining global supplies, while reduced Russian refinery activity has added pressure to diesel markets.
On Oct. 11, Trump claimed that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an “ energy ceasefire .” But Ukrainian officials reported Russian strikes on energy infrastructure hours after his announcement, and Zelensky said the purported agreement was news to him.
That same day, Trump called for Ukraine to replace President Volodymyr Zelensky . Withdrawing sanctions within a week of those statements, and thereby improving Russia’s financial position,  points to an increasingly uneven display of support from the U.S.
Jeff Colgan, a professor of political science at Brown University, tells TIME that the decision is basically “is like trading [Ukrainian] democracy for diesel.”
A diesel deal with Russia, announced Oct. 9, is one of the many recent actions Trump has taken in an effort to reduce fuel prices for Americans ahead of midterm elections on Nov. 3. The deal came with an easing of sanctions as the Treasury Department issued a temporary license authorizing transactions involving Russian diesel, including imports into the U.S., through April 7, 2027.
In his announcement of the deal on Truth Social, Trump said that Russia had agreed to supply global markets with more than 4.8 million tons of diesel, claiming  that it would bring diesel prices down “IN RECORD NUMBERS.”
But experts question whether the deal would actually have any material impact on the price of fuel for American consumers.
“There could be a modest short-term effect on wholesale prices, but only a small and delayed benefit, if any, would reach consumers at the pump,” says Tatiana Mitrova, a fellow at Columbia University&#x27;s Center on Global Energy Policy.
The waiver signed Friday, in comparison, could potentially prevent additional disruption to the global energy market . It allows Russian oil to continue flowing to importers like China and India, preventing further market volatility.
“The broader sanctions waiver may prevent additional market disruptions, but it does not itself create new supply,” Mitrova says. “The potential economic benefit to American consumers looks rather modest compared with the geopolitical cost of weakening sanctions pressure.”
Reducing that pressure—which has widely been interpreted as a component of the diesel deal, though it has not been explicitly identified as such—likely benefits Russia more than the monetary value of the diesel deal itself, Mitrova points out.
“The much bigger financial benefit for Moscow is avoiding additional sanctions that could have undermined its much larger crude oil export revenues,” she says.
And while Trump has touted the benefits of the deal, Colgan questions whether they will be tangible to any country other than Russia itself.
“Easing the sanctions probably won&#x27;t move global prices very much (big market), but could mean substantial extra revenue for Russia,” he says.
But the potential consequences extend beyond Russia’s balance sheet. The waiver also raises questions about Washington’s commitment to Ukraine as relations between the two countries deteriorate.
Beyond its impact on global diesel prices, Trump’s sanctions waiver could deepen a growing rift between the U.S. and Ukraine—raising further questions about Washington’s support for Kyiv.
Konstantin Sonin, a Russian economist and a professor at the University of Chicago Harris School of Public Policy, reads the move as a symbolic gesture of support for Russian President Vladimir Putin. He suggests that it could send a message to U.S. allies that extends beyond economic considerations.
“I think this will be read by Ukraine and Ukraine leadership—and European leadership—as another step in the United States withdrawing from being an active European player,” he tells TIME.
In addition to bolstering Russia’s finances and giving it latitude in its invasion of Ukraine, Sonin says that the waiver of additional sanctions could also “boost morale” for Putin.
“It’s another bit of signaling that the United States is not as supportive of Ukraine as they were historically,” he adds. “You still cannot say that the United States is on Putin&#x27;s side, but it is another bit of diminishing support for Ukraine.”
Mitrova agrees that the biggest benefit to Russia might be more strategic than financial.
“For Putin, the most valuable outcome may not be additional diesel revenues but the perception that Washington is increasingly prioritizing domestic energy prices over sustained pressure on Russia,” she says. “That could reinforce the Kremlin’s belief that it can outlast Western support rather than make meaningful concessions.”
Zelensky criticized the decision to block the new sanctions.
“The decision to undo all of this, to block all of it, seems wrong to me,” the Ukrainian President wrote on X on Saturday. “And it definitely helps only the Russians. It only fuels their satisfaction and their desire to keep killing people. This certainly does not stop the war.”
Mitrova argues that in easing sanctions, the U.S. also compromises its position with European leadership that has continued to align closely with Zelensky and Ukraine.
“Europe is still tightening sanctions against Russia, while Washington is now moving in the opposite direction,” she says. “By suspending sanctions that were designed to discourage major countries from buying Russian energy, Washington risks undermining a central pillar of the Western strategy toward Russia.”
Why Republicans and Democrats are criticizing the waiver
Trump’s waiver earned a flood of criticism from both sides of the aisle, particularly since it undermined a widely supported act of Congress. The Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026 cleared Congress on Sept. 16 with overwhelming bipartisan support: a vote of 86-11 in the Senate and a vote of 262-159 in the House.
North Carolina Republican Sen. Thom Tillis, who is leaving office in January, called the decision “beyond irresponsible” on Saturday.
Suggesting that the Trump Administration is overtly snubbing the will of the legislative branch, Tillis wrote on X : “It’s unconscionable that this administration is capitulating to a Russian dictator who illegally invaded Ukraine.”
Texas Republican Sen. John Cornyn reacted by saying “Lindsey would be appalled,” referencing the late Republican Sen. Lindsey Graham—a staunch Ukraine supporter after whom the sanctions act was named. Graham had spent over a year petitioning for the move before his death on July 11.
Massachusetts Sen. Elizabeth Warren, a Democrat, said in a statement that Trump was “enabling Vladimir Putin to continue his war of aggression.”
Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer released a statement Saturday saying that “by giving the Russian economy life support, Trump’s deal with the devil keeps getting dirtier.”
Trump was able to waive the sanctions through a safety valve provision embedded in the legislation. The law allowed Trump to waive its sanctions and tariffs after submitting a written certification to Congress that doing so was in the national interest, along with a report explaining that determination. In Friday’s order, Trump said he had submitted both.
“I am hereby waiving the application of such sanctions, restrictions, and duties provided for in the Graham Act,” Trump wrote in the order. “I have submitted to the Congress a certification that the issuance of such a waiver at this time is in the national interests of the United States.”
A White House spokesperson tells TIME that enforcing the sanctions would impede U.S. efforts to negotiate peace, but did not elaborate.
"Enforcing the sanctions provisions now would impede ongoing U.S. efforts to negotiate a durable and comprehensive peace agreement between Ukraine and Russia," the spokesperson says. "The President retains the ability to exercise these authorities at any time if he determines that doing so would advance national interests.”
Darline Graham, the late senator’s sister who was appointed to fill his seat in South Carolina and is running for election this November to keep it, said at a campaign stop on Saturday that the decision was Trump’s to make; however, she voiced disapproval, saying that she would prefer to “keep the pressure on Russia.”
“I don’t trust Putin and I don’t trust Russia,” she said.

The last few years have been absolutely fantastic for stock investors. The bull market of the last four years has pushed the S&P 500 ( ^GSPC -1.09% ) 116% higher while the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite ( ^IXIC -2.15% ) is up an even more impressive 156%.
While the bull market has been strong and supported by stellar earnings growth and improving outlooks, many investors can&#x27;t help but notice that stock market valuations are particularly high. That&#x27;s not a sustainable position for stocks: Either earnings have to grow fast enough to bring valuations lower, or stock prices have to come down.
In fact, it&#x27;s a practical inevitability that stock prices crash 20% or more in the future. The markets move in cycles, and eventually, some event will trigger a big sell-off in the stock market. If you want to position your portfolio to recover in less than half the time it takes for others, here&#x27;s exactly what you should do.
It doesn&#x27;t take any groundbreaking portfolio strategy to produce rapid recoveries in the event of a stock market crash. Investors can hold a simple 60/40 stock/bond portfolio with periodic rebalancing and achieve superior results in maintaining portfolio stability.
Since 1988, the 60/40 portfolio has recovered from drawdowns of 20% or more in just 11 months on average, according to data compiled by J.P. Morgan analysts. Meanwhile, the S&P 500 took an average of 24 months to recover, even with dividends reinvested.
It&#x27;s worth noting that the 60/40 portfolio only stands out against an all-stock portfolio in terms of recovery when the portfolio drops at least 20%. Smaller drops don&#x27;t exhibit much difference in how long it takes the portfolio to recover. That said, that&#x27;s exactly the kind of protection an investor should hope for from bonds : a stabilizing force amid big long drops without providing too much drag amid shorter pullbacks.
The stability of the 60/40 portfolio is further evidenced by the likelihood that it will produce a positive return over any given five- or 10-year period. Since 1989, the 60/40 portfolio has produced a positive return 99% of the time over any five-year period and 100% of the time over 10 years. The S&P 500 alone only achieves positive returns 87% of the time over five years and 93% of the time over 10 years.
Maintaining a 60/40 ratio between stocks and bonds in your portfolio requires periodic rebalancing. When more volatile stocks crash, you&#x27;ll end up with a lower ratio of stocks to bonds in your portfolio. You&#x27;ll have to sell some bonds and buy some stocks to return the portfolio to the 60/40 balance.
This act of rebalancing means you automatically buy stocks when they go down. The reverse is also true; you&#x27;ll sell stocks when they climb higher or if bonds decline. That means you can be opportunistic in buying and selling stocks , which ultimately produces more stable returns and helps a portfolio recover quickly after a crash.
While some have concerns about whether bonds can truly act as a good diversifying asset for stocks after both asset classes fell sharply in 2022, that situation was more of an anomaly than a new normal. Bonds produced positive returns in 21 of the last 25 years in which the S&P 500 declined. As such, investors should remain confident that bonds can provide diversification when it matters most.
With bond prices sinking lower in 2026, right now could be an opportune time to increase your allocation to the asset class. The Vanguard Total Bond Market ETF ( BND +0.01% ) , which tracks the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond index, currently offers a yield to maturity of 5.5%.

However, investors should understand that bonds have more limited upside than stocks, and they could drag down performance somewhat over the long run. On average, the 60/40 portfolio has produced an annualized return of 9.4% since 1950, versus 11.7% for the S&P 500. That&#x27;s the price of downside protection. But with bond values sinking lower this year, that downside protection doesn&#x27;t look as expensive as in the past.

While companies like Nvidia and  AMD may get most of the headline glory in the AI realm for their powerful processors, the reality is these companies only design some of the chips that go into data centers. The fabrication muscle in the sector lies in a handful of chip foundry businesses, with Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing ( TSM -2.28% ) and Micron ( MU -5.63% ) being the two largest in the world by market cap.
But which one makes for the better buy? Let&#x27;s dig in, as these two are very different investments.
Micron and Taiwan Semiconductor aren&#x27;t competitors
On the surface, Micron and Taiwan Semiconductor are both chip foundry companies. Both take in client orders, then create their chips. However, that&#x27;s where the similarities end.
Micron and Taiwan Semiconductor produce two entirely different types of chips; Micron designs and makes memory chips, and Taiwan Semiconductor makes logic chips designed by other companies. Both are required in accelerated computing units like GPUs, but each serves a different role.
Furthermore, the technologies behind each one are radically different. There isn&#x27;t a ton that separates one memory chip from another. Take basic computer RAM, for example. Nobody cares a lot about which brand of RAM is in a laptop, only what capacity that RAM has. That&#x27;s because most memory chips are practically the same, although Micron is challenging that notion. It recently agreed to design an industry-first custom memory chip for Nvidia&#x27;s GPUs, which could be a different avenue to lock in better long-term contract deals.

Because one manufacturer&#x27;s memory chips are much like another&#x27;s, they are subject to commodity-like pricing. This year, demand for memory chips has steeply outpaced supply, causing prices to soar. The shortage is expected to get worse in 2027 and 2028, which would allow Micron to keep turning huge profits on its chips.
The logic chips that come out of Taiwan Semiconductor&#x27;s foundries are completely different. Companies like Nvidia and AMD design processors, and their various devices can deliver competitive edges on a range of criteria as the chipmakers work to improve speed, energy efficiency, transistor density, and more. Choosing a reliable chip manufacturer is a big part of this design process, and with TSMC&#x27;s massive production facilities, proven track record, and leading technology, it&#x27;s the partner that is most often selected. Another area where Micron holds an edge is valuation. Investors today can pick up Micron shares at a much cheaper 5.3 times forward earnings compared to TSMC&#x27;s 24.
Why the huge price discrepancy? It all has to do with Micron operating in what has historically been a highly cyclical industry. Every time memory demand outpaces supply, the chipmakers start to build new fabs. By the time they come online, the demand waves have often slackened, supply leaps ahead, and prices tumble.
The market isn&#x27;t convinced that the current wave of intense memory chip demand driven by the AI infrastructure build-out will last, and it&#x27;s discounting it as a result. But Micron and others in the memory industry have said the shortage is going to get worse before it gets better. If Micron&#x27;s stock rises throughout the year due to strong demand, it could deliver incredible returns.
TSMC is also a solid investment, and because the logic chip industry is not as cyclical (it still is, but not to the same degree), the market is not as concerned about its outlook, and therefore is not valuing the stock at a discount.

Neither Google nor Synaptics has specified the exact workloads used for that comparison, or which prior-generation processor served as the baseline, so the 40% figure should be read as a vendor claim rather than an independently benchmarked result.
That caveat matters because power-efficiency claims in the chip industry are notoriously workload-dependent. A processor that looks dramatically more efficient on a simple keyword-spotting task can lose much of its edge on continuous multimodal sensing, which is exactly the use case Google and Synaptics are targeting. Until Synaptics publishes a full datasheet, outside engineers will not be able to verify the 40% number against a defined test suite.

Synaptics did not simply license Google's silicon design and ship it unchanged. The Astra chip combines the Coral NPU M2 with Synaptics' own Torq NPU and standard Arm CPU cores, giving the company a path to differentiate on top of Google's open accelerator work. That architecture lets Synaptics target a wide span of constrained devices, from battery-powered wearables to fixed industrial cameras, with one underlying platform rather than a separate chip for each category.
A less flashy but arguably more consequential part of the announcement is software. Synaptics and Google Research spent roughly 18 months building an open-source compiler on top of MLIR, the Multi-Level Intermediate Representation framework, so the chip can run standard machine learning frameworks and model formats rather than locking developers into a proprietary toolchain. Patel framed this as a break from the walled-garden software that has defined microcontroller development for years, a point echoed in R&D World's account of his remarks. Developers can already prototype against the underlying Coral NPU using Coralboard, a development platform Synaptics launched earlier this year that pairs the NPU with Astra processors ahead of the full chip's release.
Coral's first milestone was a front end built on RISC-V, the open instruction set architecture that has gained ground across the chip industry as an alternative to proprietary cores. M2 marks the second milestone and adds what Google describes as the first Coral vector processor. Google Research has also said its next design, M3, is already complete and adds native 32-bit floating-point support so the chip can run Gemma 3 270M, a compact 270-million-parameter language model, directly on-device. Neither company has given a shipping date for M3-based silicon, which means the chip debuting this month is already one generation behind Google's internal roadmap.
Rahul Patel's Pitch: From Lab Bench to Google Hardware
Patel's most direct claim from the Research@ appearance was that the chip will not stay in the lab and will also be built into Google devices, a statement widely picked up across outlets covering the preview. He also highlighted the microcontroller's support for high-density DRAM, describing memory capacity as a source of scalability for future products built on the same platform. Synaptics plans to formally unveil the chip at its Tech Day event on October 20 at 1151 McKay Drive in San Jose, which the company has billed as including a noon press and analyst briefing followed by a demonstration showcase.
What Patel did not say is just as notable. He declined to identify which Google hardware lines would adopt the chip or offer any release window, leaving a gap between the marketing claim and a verifiable product commitment. That gap is where skepticism from hardware analysts tends to concentrate, since chipmakers routinely tout future design wins that take years, or never materialize, to reach shelves.
Why Google Keeps Investing in Edge Silicon Partnerships
Google's data center strategy has long rested on designing its own Tensor Processing Units alongside the models that run on them, and the company is now pushing the same co-design philosophy to the edge. The logic, as described in reporting on the Research@ event, is that existing edge chips consume too much energy and respond too slowly for always-on, ambient AI experiences that keep personal data on the device instead of sending it to the cloud. A fragmented market of proprietary microcontroller designs compounds the problem by making it harder for developers to build once and deploy across multiple vendors' hardware.
That framing explains why Google chose a partnership model rather than building its own edge chip from scratch. Synaptics brings decades of microcontroller and sensor-fusion experience along with existing customer relationships in wearables and industrial equipment, while Google supplies the Coral accelerator design and compiler work it has already validated internally.
Billy Rutledge Makes the Case for Co-Designed Hardware
When Google and Synaptics first announced their collaboration on edge AI for the Internet of Things, Billy Rutledge, Director of Systems Research at Google Research, laid out why the two companies paired up in the first place. "Synaptics' embrace of open software and tools and proven AI hardware makes the Astra portfolio a natural fit for our ML core as we ramp to meet the uniquely challenging power, performance, cost, and space requirements of Edge AI devices," Rutledge said in the companies' joint statement announcing the partnership.
Rutledge closed that announcement with a line that reads differently now that the chip has reached a public preview. "We look forward to working together to bring our capabilities to the broad market," he said. Nearly two years of engineering separate that statement from this month's Research@ demonstration, which is roughly in line with the 18-to-24-month development window Patel cited for the project.
Synaptics spokesperson Patrick Mannion gave the clearest public explanation of the division of labor between Google's open core and Synaptics' own hardware in remarks made around CES 2025. "What we're doing is taking their open ML core and adding our own hardware engine as an enhancement," Mannion said. He added that the goal was tuning that shared foundation for the specific operators Synaptics' engine supports: "We're making that open core run better for the set of operators that we support in the engine."
Mannion also confirmed the architectural lineage underpinning the whole platform in a brief, direct answer about the subsystem's design. "It's RISC-V-based," he said, a detail that lines up with Google's own description of Coral's first milestone as a RISC-V front end before M2 added the vector processor on top of it.
Target Devices: Wearables, Smart Glasses, and the Industrial Floor
The use cases Google and Synaptics are chasing span two very different markets. On the consumer side, smart glasses, watches, and connected cameras need to recognize speech, gestures, and context without draining a small battery in a few hours. On the industrial side, vision and voice systems on factory floors need similar always-on sensing but with tighter requirements around reliability and hardware-rooted security, since these devices often sit on networks that also touch safety-critical equipment.
Covering both markets with one chip platform is an ambitious bet. It means Synaptics has to hit aggressive power targets for battery-powered wearables while also meeting the durability and security expectations of industrial buyers, all on the same underlying silicon. If the company pulls it off, Astra could become a default reference design that OEMs reach for instead of commissioning custom edge AI silicon.
This announcement did not come out of nowhere. Google's interest in specialized AI silicon traces back to a 2017 Google research paper on its first-generation Tensor Processing Unit, which reported performance-per-watt gains of 30 to 80 times over the Intel Haswell CPUs and Nvidia K80 GPUs it was benchmarked against in production neural-network inference workloads, per R&D World's account of the comparison. That data-center work eventually fed into Google's push toward edge-specific silicon, starting with early Coral development boards and continuing through a 2023 partnership with Qualcomm on a RISC-V wearable platform for Wear OS.
The Synaptics collaboration builds directly on that foundation. Google and Synaptics announced their partnership in January 2025, Synaptics launched the Coralboard development platform earlier in 2026, and the companies previewed the resulting Astra chip at Research@ this October, ahead of the formal Tech Day unveiling.

Competitive Landscape: Astra vs. Qualcomm, Ambiq, NXP, and STMicroelectronics
Synaptics is not entering an empty market. Qualcomm remains the volume leader in AI-capable edge silicon, having shipped more than 800 million AI-capable chips in 2025 according to industry data compiled by Axis Intelligence, with Snapdragon platforms using Hexagon NPUs rated at up to 45 TOPS for on-device inference. That positions Qualcomm at the high-performance end of the market, aimed at smartphones and premium wearables that can absorb more power draw than the ultra-constrained devices Synaptics is targeting.
Ambiq, NXP, and STMicroelectronics compete in a different tier, focused on ultra-low-power microcontrollers, embedded processing, and sensor-heavy industrial and automotive applications. Synaptics is trying to carve out space between these two groups, pitching Astra as a highly integrated, ultra-low-power platform that can still handle the multimodal sensing workloads usually reserved for higher-power chips like Qualcomm's.

For a chip generating this much coverage, the list of unconfirmed details is long. Neither company has disclosed the transistor count, the manufacturing process node, total on-chip memory capacity, a full-chip TOPS rating, the complete list of supported model architectures, pricing, expected production volume, or the first Google device that will actually ship with the chip inside it. The 40% power claim and the sub-6-milliwatt inference figure are the only hard performance numbers in circulation, and both come without a disclosed testing methodology.
That gap between marketing narrative and verifiable specification is normal for a pre-launch teaser, but it means most of the real analysis has to wait for October 20. Readers and investors treating this preview as a finished product announcement are getting ahead of what Google and Synaptics have actually confirmed.
Synaptics' Tech Day event in San Jose is where the preview either turns into a credible product or stalls as another unfulfilled chip tease. The company has described a noon press and analyst briefing followed by a demonstration showcase, which suggests working silicon will be on hand rather than simulations or reference designs alone. Analysts will be watching for a full TOPS rating covering the whole chip, confirmed pricing and sampling timelines for OEMs, and ideally a named lead customer or product category beyond the general "Google devices" language Patel used at Research@.
The compiler story also deserves scrutiny at the event. An open, MLIR-based toolchain is only as useful as its documentation and the breadth of model formats it actually supports on day one. If Synaptics ships the chip with a thin software stack and tells developers to wait for tooling updates, the open-compiler pitch will ring hollow regardless of how efficient the underlying silicon turns out to be.
Synaptics will likely disclose a full-chip TOPS rating and manufacturing process node at Tech Day on October 20, since withholding both details through a second public appearance would undercut the credibility of the power-efficiency claims.
