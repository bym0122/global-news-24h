President Donald Trump signed an executive order Friday that waives many of the sanctions in the Lindsey O. The waiver, coming a week after Trump announced a diesel deal with Russia, also underscores fraying relations between the U.S. and Ukraine.
The law, which was set to take effect Oct. 18, called for sanctions targeting Russian officials, financial institutions, and other entities, alongside tariffs of up to 100% on goods imported into the U.S. from major buyers of Russian crude oil or natural gas. Those measures were intended to restrict the financial resources supporting Russia’s war against Ukraine.
The U.K.’s Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office estimated in June 2025 that international sanctions had deprived the Russian state of at least $450 billion in war funds since February 2022, including through lost oil tax revenues and immobilized central-bank reserves.
While Trump’s waiver leaves existing sanctions in place, it blocks the additional pressure the new measures were intended to exert.
Trump has also pushed Ukraine to scale back its own campaign against Russia’s energy sector, repeatedly urging Kyiv to halt strikes on Russian fuel infrastructure and suggesting that those attacks are driving up U.S. fuel prices. But Ukrainian strikes are only one factor affecting fuel prices; disruptions linked to the war with Iran are largely responsible for constraining global supplies, while reduced Russian refinery activity has added pressure to diesel markets.
On Oct. 11, Trump claimed that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an “ energy ceasefire .” But Ukrainian officials reported Russian strikes on energy infrastructure hours after his announcement, and Zelensky said the purported agreement was news to him.
That same day, Trump called for Ukraine to replace President Volodymyr Zelensky . Withdrawing sanctions within a week of those statements, and thereby improving Russia’s financial position,  points to an increasingly uneven display of support from the U.S.
Jeff Colgan, a professor of political science at Brown University, tells TIME that the decision is basically “is like trading [Ukrainian] democracy for diesel.”
A diesel deal with Russia, announced Oct. 9, is one of the many recent actions Trump has taken in an effort to reduce fuel prices for Americans ahead of midterm elections on Nov. 3. The deal came with an easing of sanctions as the Treasury Department issued a temporary license authorizing transactions involving Russian diesel, including imports into the U.S., through April 7, 2027.

“There could be a modest short-term effect on wholesale prices, but only a small and delayed benefit, if any, would reach consumers at the pump,” says Tatiana Mitrova, a fellow at Columbia University&#x27;s Center on Global Energy Policy.

“The broader sanctions waiver may prevent additional market disruptions, but it does not itself create new supply,” Mitrova says. “The potential economic benefit to American consumers looks rather modest compared with the geopolitical cost of weakening sanctions pressure.”

Bolivia’s Energy Crisis Deepens as Natural Gas Reserves Run Dry
Bolivia's dwindling natural gas reserves and declining exports have undermined its economy and intensified fuel shortages.
Rising global energy prices and subsidy cuts have increased living costs and fueled widespread protests.
A $1.9 billion IMF rescue package and energy-sector reforms aim to stabilize the economy, but long-term recovery requires diversification.
Years of underinvestment in Bolivia’s gas industry have left the South American country facing a severe fuel shortage, as energy prices rise ever higher owing to the ongoing war in Iran. The energy shortages and economic crisis follow years of poor decision-making by the government.
In 2006, then-president Evo Morales took control of Bolivia’s fossil fuel industry, renegotiating contracts with international oil and gas companies and benefiting from the revenue. Much of the country’s newfound wealth was invested in public spending programmes, expensive fuel subsidies, and the maintenance of a fixed currency exchange rate pegged to the U.S. dollar. Fossil fuel revenues contributed heavily to Bolivia’s economy for several years, supporting GDP expansion and the development of social programmes that helped halve poverty rates .
For many years, Bolivian’s were led to believe that they had more extensive gas supplies than they actually did. However, in recent years, Bolivia’s fossil fuel sector has suffered as reserves have dwindled and no successful gas discoveries have occurred. As of April, Bolivia has an estimated 3.7 trillion cubic feet of proven reserves. The fall in gas production led exports to fall by almost 60 per cent over the last decade. Bolivia continues to rely heavily on natural gas , which accounts for around 70 per cent of its electricity mix.
Meanwhile, little progress has been made in developing the country’s green energy sector, leaving it facing severe energy shortages. The fall in gas revenues has left the economy suffering terribly, as inflation soars and the price of imported goods becomes ever more unaffordable. Luis Fernando Romero, the former head of an association of economists in southern Bolivia, stated, “We failed to take advantage of a historic opportunity that brought in immense revenue.”
This situation is not uncommon, with many other oil-rich states having had similar experiences. Several Middle Eastern states have long depended on oil and gas revenues for economic growth, and most have only recently announced plans to diversify their economies to reduce reliance on fossil fuels. Several countries across the MENA region announced new economic strategies following greater talk of a global green transition, which could leave them missing out on vital oil revenues if they don’t take moves to diversify and invest in value-added sectors.
The lack of investment in Bolivia’s other domestic sectors has left it struggling to stay afloat as it runs out of fuel and is forced to pay high fuel import costs. President Rodrigo Paz warned that “International prices are forcing us to make complex choices.”
In September, Bolivia’s Congress approved a $1.9 billion loan from the International Monetary Fund (IMF), even though the terms of the deal require the government to impose strict austerity measures that could fuel social unrest. The loan received approval from the IMF’s Executive Board on October 2, and the government will now be required to cut its fuel subsidies and significantly reduce public spending. It has already increased fuel prices and is expected to scrap further gasoline subsidies from the beginning of next year. The loan is expected to unlock up to $4 billion more in financing from the World Bank and other lenders.
However, there are major concerns that the new financial plan will significantly increase the cost of living for households already struggling. Last December, the government reduced fuel subsidies, leaving little support for the most vulnerable Bolivians. This led to widespread protests as people called for President Paz’s resignation . In February, there was further social unrest after the distribution of poor-quality gasoline ruined thousands of vehicles. Then, in June and July, several weeks of road blockades by protesters occurred.
Meanwhile, to tackle the ongoing fuel shortages, Bolivia’s government announced plans in September to triple the fund financing refined fuel purchases, following similar announcements by Peru and Ecuador. This is expected to help mitigate diesel shortages and rising prices. Both Peru and Ecuador have increased fuel subsidies in recent months and are focusing on increasing production at state oil refineries to alleviate the price pressure of expensive fuel imports.
In July, Bolivia's Energy Minister Marcelo Blanco announced that the government had authorised the private sector to import and sell refined fuels to mitigate severe shortages. Then, in September, the government said that the state hydrocarbons firm plans to focus on exploration, extraction, and refining to continue developing Bolivia’s gas resources. However, experts warn that Bolivia could become a net fossil gas importer by around 2030 .
As Bolivia continues to battle fuel shortages and seeks to support economic recovery by borrowing from international organisations, the government must focus on diversifying the economy by developing other value-added sectors to sustain long-term growth. In addition, the government must also develop Bolivia’s renewable energy sources to reduce dependence on expensive fuel imports and ensure the country’s energy security.

Berkshire rally to highest since Buffett announcement
(This is the Warren Buffett Watch newsletter, news and analysis on all things Warren Buffett and Berkshire Hathaway. You can sign up here to receive it every Friday evening in your inbox.)
Berkshire Hathaway shares closed Friday at their highest level since Warren Buffett told shareholders at the May 2025 annual meeting he planned to step down as CEO at the end of that year .
The day before that Saturday surprise, both the  Class A and Class B shares had closed at all-time highs.
Over the next three months, they lost more than 14% of their value as investors worried a " Buffett premium " was dissolving.
It&#x27;s been more than a year since their post-meeting low in August of 2025, and the recovery has not been a straight line, but BRK.A ended Friday at $798,000, just 1.4% below its all-time closing high of $809,350.
The B shares finished at $532.02, also 1.4% under their all-time closing high of $539.80.
A loss Thursday broke a 10-session winning streak that added 5% to Berkshire&#x27;s stock prices but they rebounded with gains of more than 1% Friday.
CNBC Pro thinks investors may be happy Berkshire is spending some of its big cash pile under CEO Greg Abel, with  $4.5 billion of buybacks in Q2 , investments in  Alphabet , Taylor Morrison Home , Lennar , (although there haven&#x27;t been any buys of that stock since October 2), and now, Bending Spoons . (More on that below).
It also notes Keefe, Bruyette & Woods analyst Meyer Shields believes higher short-term interest rates are helping to lift the shares.
Berkshire still has roughly $360 billion in cash, "a good symbol of continued patience under the new CEO," and those higher rates mean "they&#x27;ll be making more money" on that money.
In addition, its insurance operations have benefited from a quiet hurricane season so far.
Higher oil prices are good for its $19 billion stake in  Chevron , which closed Friday at an all-time high after gaining almost 36% since the start of July. Berkshire&#x27;s energy-related  subsidiaries are also getting a tailwind.
And recently, Berkshire may be getting a boost as investors  shift away from high-flying AI and tech stocks and turn to "old" economy names .
For example, Coca-Cola , a long-time Buffett favorite and now a $37.6 billion holding , also closed Friday at an all-time high, perhaps with some extra help from KFC&#x27;s switch from Pepsi to Coke . It&#x27;s up 34% year-to-date.
Berkshire&#x27;s position has gained $2.4 billion since last Friday, overtaking Alphabet to become the third largest stake in its portfolio. (See the full top 10 below.)
Berkshire&#x27;s B shares have cut their June 1 deficit of 17.5 percentage points versus the  S&P 500 by almost two-thirds.
They are now 6.0 percentage points behind the benchmark for the year.
Atypical bet on &#x27;buzzy&#x27; Italian tech company is big win ... so far
Berkshire Hathaway&#x27;s surprising bet of close to $1 billion on  Bending Spoons is paying off big, at least in the short term.
The Italian company, which began trading on Nasdaq in the U.S. in July, buys cast-off internet names like AOL, Evernote, and Vimeo, uses AI to cut costs, and deploys the savings to make more acquisitions.
In late July, CNBC Pro said , "Wall Street is buzzing," and "analysts ... see big gains ahead."
The tech stock is decidedly not a typical Berkshire  value play , trading at a large multiple to its earnings.
Matteo Danieli, co-founder of Bending Spoons, center, during the company&#x27;s initial public offering (IPO) at the Nasdaq MarketSite in New York, US, on Wednesday, July 1, 2026.
But before Wall Street&#x27;s open Wednesday morning, a Bending Spoons  news release revealed Berkshire is investing a "further" $330 million in the company by purchasing 10 million newly issued shares at $33 per share in a private placement .
That was already an almost 12% discount from Tuesday&#x27;s closing price of $37.86 and the news sent Bending Spoons shares even higher .
They hit an intraday high of $58.56 before closing up 41% at $53.46 on Wednesday.
Bending Spoons shares are up 24.3% over the three trading days since the revelation, after giving up a chunk of their earlier gains in  Friday&#x27;s tech sell-off to end at $47.07.
That&#x27;s a solid 42.6% higher than Berkshire&#x27;s $33 purchase price for a profit, on paper, of nearly $141 million.
But what&#x27;s that "further" in Bending Spoon&#x27;s statement?
The release also disclosed, apparently for the first time, that Berkshire invested $600 million at Bending Spoon&#x27;s IPO more than three months ago.
The release doesn&#x27;t specify how many shares Berkshire bought in July, and Bending Spoons wouldn&#x27;t confirm my math, but assuming Berkshire paid the $29 per share IPO price, that initial purchase would have been almost 20.7 million shares.
That puts Berkshire&#x27;s holding now at close to 30.7 million shares, which would be worth more than $1.4 billion at Friday&#x27;s close, a gain of 55.3% for its overall investment of $930 million.
Bending Spoons shares soar after Berkshire reveals $330M investment, taking a 4% stake
Bending Spoon&#x27;s release also says the "new investment brings Berkshire Hathaway&#x27;s ownership to 4%."
The company told me in an email it hasn&#x27;t "specifically disclosed" how many outstanding shares it has now, "but we had 635 million fully diluted weighted average shares outstanding in Q2, we issued 34 million shares as part of the IPO, and we will be issuing an additional 10 million as part of this transaction."
Using those numbers, Berkshire&#x27;s presumed 30.7 million shares are a 4.5% stake, and that&#x27;s what I&#x27;ve put in the  Berkshire Portfolio Tracker for now.

Bending Spoons CEO and Co-Founder Luca Ferrari shares Wall Street&#x27;s enthusiasm about Berkshire&#x27;s investments, saying in the news release the Omaha company "has been among the finest and most respected capital allocators in the world, and is a longstanding inspiration for Bending Spoons."

Intensifying drone attacks on ships in Black Sea bring war ever closer to Nato shores
Six attacks in 10 days by suspected Russian drones mark dramatic escalation in maritime conflict
T he first the crew of the Bulgarian ferry Dioscuria knew of an attack on a cargo ship in their country’s exclusive economic zone in the Black Sea in the early hours of 6 October was a distant explosion 70 miles off the coastal town of Byala.
The Turkish-owned Alfa Watan, heading for the Romanian port of Sulina, had been struck by suspected Russian air and naval drones. Arriving on the scene, Dioscuria found an overturned lifeboat and lifejackets in the water. There was no sign, however, of Alfa Watan’s crew of 10.
Map showing location of ships attacked in the Black Sea’s EU waters in October Six days later drones struck another ship off the coast of Bulgaria, HS Dignity. Eleven crew members died.
By Thursday, the attacks had encroached on Romania’s airspace and territorial water. Two chemical tankers, Wine Trader and Yara J, were attacked and left burning. Seven more sailors were missing.
Four of the rescued sailors from HS Dignity on a Bulgarian navy rescue tug, which transported them to the port of Varna on Tuesday. Photograph: moreto.net/AP View image in fullscreen
Crew members from the Able, which was damaged in a drone attack, disembark from a Bulgarian border police vessel in Varna after a rescue operation. Photograph: Petko Momchilov/Reuters The attacks on six ships in 10 days – the Able and Chelsea-3 were also hit – marked a dramatic escalation in a maritime conflict that is pressing on Nato’s borders.
While global attention has focused on the risks for sailors travelling through the strait of Hormuz, in recent months it is the Black Sea that has emerged as the more dangerous passage, with more sailors lost in a week and a half than US service members killed in the entire Iran war.
Alfa Watan, which sank after a drone attack, sails in Istanbul, Turkey, the day before the incident. Photograph: Yoruk Isik/Reuters The maritime insurer and risk analyst Ambrey has warned that there is no end in sight to the threats to Black Sea cargo shipping, including grain and oil, and that insurance prices for passage of the waters have rocketed.
Tomas Alexa, a senior analyst at Ambrey, has noted a sharp increase in the tempo of attacks beginning in July. Russian forces are estimated to be responsible for 80% of them.
The recent loss of life, he suggests, has been exacerbated by “double tap” attacks involving aerial and surface drones, the latter often carrying up to half a tonne of explosive payloads. Smaller vessels that primarily use the Danube canal system have sunk rapidly.

It doesn&#x27;t take any groundbreaking portfolio strategy to produce rapid recoveries in the event of a stock market crash. Investors can hold a simple 60/40 stock/bond portfolio with periodic rebalancing and achieve superior results in maintaining portfolio stability.
Since 1988, the 60/40 portfolio has recovered from drawdowns of 20% or more in just 11 months on average, according to data compiled by J.P. Morgan analysts. Meanwhile, the S&P 500 took an average of 24 months to recover, even with dividends reinvested.
It&#x27;s worth noting that the 60/40 portfolio only stands out against an all-stock portfolio in terms of recovery when the portfolio drops at least 20%. Maintaining a 60/40 ratio between stocks and bonds in your portfolio requires periodic rebalancing. You&#x27;ll have to sell some bonds and buy some stocks to return the portfolio to the 60/40 balance.
This act of rebalancing means you automatically buy stocks when they go down. The reverse is also true; you&#x27;ll sell stocks when they climb higher or if bonds decline. The chip can process voice, vision, motion, and contextual sensing data simultaneously, and it adds GPU and multimedia processing along with hardware-rooted security, according to the companies' public statements cited in a report from SBS .
The headline number from the preview is a claimed 40% cut in energy use for Google's Coral NPU M2, the successor to the Coral neural processing unit Google introduced the prior year. Reporting tied to the announcement, including coverage from ChosunBiz , describes M2 running on-device inference at an average power draw of less than 6 milliwatts. Neither Google nor Synaptics has specified the exact workloads used for that comparison, or which prior-generation processor served as the baseline, so the 40% figure should be read as a vendor claim rather than an independently benchmarked result.
That caveat matters because power-efficiency claims in the chip industry are notoriously workload-dependent. A processor that looks dramatically more efficient on a simple keyword-spotting task can lose much of its edge on continuous multimodal sensing, which is exactly the use case Google and Synaptics are targeting. Until Synaptics publishes a full datasheet, outside engineers will not be able to verify the 40% number against a defined test suite.

Synaptics did not simply license Google's silicon design and ship it unchanged. The Astra chip combines the Coral NPU M2 with Synaptics' own Torq NPU and standard Arm CPU cores, giving the company a path to differentiate on top of Google's open accelerator work. That architecture lets Synaptics target a wide span of constrained devices, from battery-powered wearables to fixed industrial cameras, with one underlying platform rather than a separate chip for each category.
A less flashy but arguably more consequential part of the announcement is software. Synaptics and Google Research spent roughly 18 months building an open-source compiler on top of MLIR, the Multi-Level Intermediate Representation framework, so the chip can run standard machine learning frameworks and model formats rather than locking developers into a proprietary toolchain. Patel framed this as a break from the walled-garden software that has defined microcontroller development for years, a point echoed in R&D World's account of his remarks. Developers can already prototype against the underlying Coral NPU using Coralboard, a development platform Synaptics launched earlier this year that pairs the NPU with Astra processors ahead of the full chip's release.
Coral's first milestone was a front end built on RISC-V, the open instruction set architecture that has gained ground across the chip industry as an alternative to proprietary cores. M2 marks the second milestone and adds what Google describes as the first Coral vector processor. Google Research has also said its next design, M3, is already complete and adds native 32-bit floating-point support so the chip can run Gemma 3 270M, a compact 270-million-parameter language model, directly on-device. Neither company has given a shipping date for M3-based silicon, which means the chip debuting this month is already one generation behind Google's internal roadmap.
Rahul Patel's Pitch: From Lab Bench to Google Hardware
Patel's most direct claim from the Research@ appearance was that the chip will not stay in the lab and will also be built into Google devices, a statement widely picked up across outlets covering the preview. He also highlighted the microcontroller's support for high-density DRAM, describing memory capacity as a source of scalability for future products built on the same platform. Synaptics plans to formally unveil the chip at its Tech Day event on October 20 at 1151 McKay Drive in San Jose, which the company has billed as including a noon press and analyst briefing followed by a demonstration showcase.
What Patel did not say is just as notable. He declined to identify which Google hardware lines would adopt the chip or offer any release window, leaving a gap between the marketing claim and a verifiable product commitment. That gap is where skepticism from hardware analysts tends to concentrate, since chipmakers routinely tout future design wins that take years, or never materialize, to reach shelves.
Why Google Keeps Investing in Edge Silicon Partnerships
Google's data center strategy has long rested on designing its own Tensor Processing Units alongside the models that run on them, and the company is now pushing the same co-design philosophy to the edge. The logic, as described in reporting on the Research@ event, is that existing edge chips consume too much energy and respond too slowly for always-on, ambient AI experiences that keep personal data on the device instead of sending it to the cloud. A fragmented market of proprietary microcontroller designs compounds the problem by making it harder for developers to build once and deploy across multiple vendors' hardware.
That framing explains why Google chose a partnership model rather than building its own edge chip from scratch. Synaptics brings decades of microcontroller and sensor-fusion experience along with existing customer relationships in wearables and industrial equipment, while Google supplies the Coral accelerator design and compiler work it has already validated internally.
Billy Rutledge Makes the Case for Co-Designed Hardware
When Google and Synaptics first announced their collaboration on edge AI for the Internet of Things, Billy Rutledge, Director of Systems Research at Google Research, laid out why the two companies paired up in the first place. "Synaptics' embrace of open software and tools and proven AI hardware makes the Astra portfolio a natural fit for our ML core as we ramp to meet the uniquely challenging power, performance, cost, and space requirements of Edge AI devices," Rutledge said in the companies' joint statement announcing the partnership.
Rutledge closed that announcement with a line that reads differently now that the chip has reached a public preview. "We look forward to working together to bring our capabilities to the broad market," he said. Nearly two years of engineering separate that statement from this month's Research@ demonstration, which is roughly in line with the 18-to-24-month development window Patel cited for the project.
Synaptics spokesperson Patrick Mannion gave the clearest public explanation of the division of labor between Google's open core and Synaptics' own hardware in remarks made around CES 2025. "What we're doing is taking their open ML core and adding our own hardware engine as an enhancement," Mannion said. He added that the goal was tuning that shared foundation for the specific operators Synaptics' engine supports: "We're making that open core run better for the set of operators that we support in the engine."
Mannion also confirmed the architectural lineage underpinning the whole platform in a brief, direct answer about the subsystem's design. "It's RISC-V-based," he said, a detail that lines up with Google's own description of Coral's first milestone as a RISC-V front end before M2 added the vector processor on top of it.
Target Devices: Wearables, Smart Glasses, and the Industrial Floor
The use cases Google and Synaptics are chasing span two very different markets. On the consumer side, smart glasses, watches, and connected cameras need to recognize speech, gestures, and context without draining a small battery in a few hours. On the industrial side, vision and voice systems on factory floors need similar always-on sensing but with tighter requirements around reliability and hardware-rooted security, since these devices often sit on networks that also touch safety-critical equipment.
Covering both markets with one chip platform is an ambitious bet. It means Synaptics has to hit aggressive power targets for battery-powered wearables while also meeting the durability and security expectations of industrial buyers, all on the same underlying silicon. If the company pulls it off, Astra could become a default reference design that OEMs reach for instead of commissioning custom edge AI silicon.
This announcement did not come out of nowhere. Google's interest in specialized AI silicon traces back to a 2017 Google research paper on its first-generation Tensor Processing Unit, which reported performance-per-watt gains of 30 to 80 times over the Intel Haswell CPUs and Nvidia K80 GPUs it was benchmarked against in production neural-network inference workloads, per R&D World's account of the comparison. That data-center work eventually fed into Google's push toward edge-specific silicon, starting with early Coral development boards and continuing through a 2023 partnership with Qualcomm on a RISC-V wearable platform for Wear OS.
The Synaptics collaboration builds directly on that foundation. “Houthi pressure on [Bab] al-Mandeb threatens the Suez Canal , which Egypt’s foreign minister says has lost about $11bn since the attacks began in 2023,” he said, referring to Houthi attacks on Israeli-linked ships passing through the narrow strait, carried out in solidarity with Gaza.
James suggested that the decision is also important to Cairo, because “millions of Egyptians live and work in Saudi Arabia, which is Egypt’s largest single source of remittances. An Egyptian resident was killed, and others were injured in the attacks on Riyadh’s airport.”
He also said that Egypt does not want to be seen as the regional ally that “stood aside while others defended the kingdom,” as Turkiye and Pakistan have made their own pledges after activating the Mecca defence pact signed in August amid the deteriorating security situation in the Middle East.
Hostilities in Yemen continue to escalate. Two air raids by the Saudi-led coalition hit Hodeidah in the southwest on Sunday.
Saudi forces also intercepted drones near Riyadh and Eastern provinces. The capital’s King Khalid international airport has come under attack at least 20 times since fighting flared between the internationally recognised government and the Houthis, also known as Ansar Allah – Arabic for Supporters of God.
The Saudi-backed forces are fighting the Houthis on several fronts, including Taiz and Marib, and some analysts argue the Houthis are stretched thin after their summer gains.
But James told Al Jazeera “that defeating them outright is a tall order”.
“Ground forces backed by air power can retake the coastline; stopping missiles launched from the mountains is much harder,” he said.
They will be convening on Sunday at 1pm local time (10am GMT).
Under the Egyptian law, the President cannot declare war or send the military on foreign missions without the approval from the parliament.
For the authorisation to be granted, at least two-thirds of the politicians in the House of Representatives must vote in favour of the proposal.
Does Egypt have security cooperation with Saudi Arabia?
Not exactly. Egypt and Saudi Arabia have not signed a formal bilateral or multilateral pact on defence cooperation. But Cairo has a long history of cooperation with Riyadh.
Egypt is a founding member of the Multinational Maritime Defense Coalition, which Saudi Arabia launched in July. It aims to protect shipping in the Red Sea and Bab al-Mandeb – territories on Yemen’s coastline. Houthis captured towns and cities near Bab al-Mandeb last month, but the internationally recognised government has since wrested control of some of the areas from the Iran-backed group.
Egypt also participated in joint military exercises with Saudi Arabia in 2024.
Cairo was also part of a 2015 coalition , cobled together after the Houthi group captured large areas of north and western Yemen, including the capital Sanaa in September 2024. Egypt contributed naval and air forces to help secure maritime corridors in the Red Sea against the Houthis.
But James, the Gulf studies expert, told Al Jazeera that what el-Sisi is proposing is different from the maritime defence coalition Egypt recently joined.
“He is hoping for authorisation to deploy Egyptian forces to help defend Saudi territory itself,” he said. “It would move Egypt from a multilateral maritime framework to a bilateral military commitment.”
What will be the nature of Egyptian assistance to Saudi Arabia?
El-Sisi explicitly said that he is only proposing “defence” and that he isn’t “speaking in order for us to fight anyone or attack anyone”.
Military Defence Analyst Alexandru Hudisteanu said that the use of counter-drones and anti-missile support is a high probability if the decision is authorised.
“It would be the most urgent need at this point to make sure that the defence of Saudi Arabia is backed up and reinforced,” he told Al Jazeera.
Simultaneously, he added that the request itself is also a strategic move of deterrence.
“We know Saudi Arabia has one of the most prolific militaries out there in terms of capacities and capabilities. It’s a matter of the territory which Saudi Arabia needs to cover at any given moment,” Hudisteanu said.
James said that any contribution by Egypt would focus on the protection of Saudi territory, using “air and missile defence around critical infrastructure, intercepting drones, naval escort and intelligence sharing”.
James said that it is unlikely Egypt will deploy troops to Yemen’s front lines.
In the 1960s, former Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser deployed tens of thousands of troops in the Yemen Civil War. Saudi Arabia backed the rival side. Egypt suffered heavy casualties and Saudi-Egyptian ties were strained.
“What Egypt will not want is to send troops into Yemen. Nasser’s intervention there in the 1960s sends a clear message: defend the kingdom, don’t take ownership of Yemen,” he told Al Jazeera.
