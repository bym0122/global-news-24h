BANGKOK – The International Monetary Fund warned Tuesday that government bond yields could rise in a disorderly fashion, as rising inflation, mounting debt and AI-related borrowings pressure sovereign bond markets.
Government bond yields in several advanced economies have climbed to multidecade highs, driven by higher energy costs due to the Middle East conflict and expectations for tighter monetary conditions as central banks look to curb resurgent inflation.
In its latest Global Financial Stability Report, the fund warned that sovereign bond risk could deteriorate, warning of "a sharp rise in sovereign bond yields." The report was released Tuesday as part of the IMF-World Bank annual meetings in Bangkok, Thailand, this week, where more than 18,000 delegates gathered to discuss the state of the world economy and the financial system.
High debt load and rollover risks make the bond market "susceptible to more frequent fiscal and inflation shocks," the report said. The IMF had warned last week that global public debt had reached near its highest level since World War II and was on track to soon exceed 100% of GDP.
"Impact could be severe given the foundational importance of sovereign bond markets," the IMF said Tuesday. A sharp rise in yields would mark down asset values through higher discount rates, including for AI-related assets, and erode the value of government bonds posted as collateral.
IMF also noted the exponentially rising borrowing by AI firms — that compete with sovereign bonds for investor demand — as an additional source of strain on bond markets. Hyperscalers&#x27; share of global investment-grade bond issuance has risen to 10% from less than 1% since 2024, the report said.
The global AI buildout has seen tech giants, such as Alphabet Inc&#x27;s Google, Meta Platforms , hyperscalers and AI labs raising hundreds of billions of dollars in debt this year.
"AI-linked issuance – much of it longer-dated – has grown large enough to compete with government bonds for investor capital, even if it does not appear to be the dominant driver of yields," according to Marc Seidner, CIO of non-traditional strategies at Pacific Investment Management.

Hedge funds&#x27; growing holdings of Treasuries leave the market "susceptible to a disorderly unwind," the IMF said. Leveraged basis trades have partly unwound without notable pressure in the short-term loan market but the leverage that remains is a concern.

Fed's Hammack warns inflation is persistent, favors more restrictive policy
Cleveland Federal Reserve president Beth Hammack said Monday that inflation is broad-based and will take time to come back down, even with a boost in interest rates.
Hammack said she is looking toward more frequent, smaller rate adjustments to ensure an inflationary mindset doesn't take hold.
"I see inflation pressures as relatively broad-based rather than concentrated in one component," Hammack said, pointing to rising energy prices , tariffs , and AI. "And it's not just the supply side. Demand-side pressures coming from the AI build-out are raising prices."
Hammack noted that while a one-time increase in tariffs could potentially be viewed as a one-time increase in price levels, multiple waves of tariff increases have resulted in a series of cost shocks.
She also noted how the AI build-out is raising prices on key computer, electronic, and high-tech components , which is raising costs for data centers, but also for projects that use similar components in aviation, electricity production, and construction.
She says when she talks with contacts across her district, she hears about additional cost pressures in health insurance, plastics, metals, grains, electricity, and other areas. Hammack noted price pressures are also showing up in core services inflation excluding housing, a category that shouldn't be especially exposed to tariffs or energy.
"Because of the breadth of inflationary pressures, my baseline forecast is that it will take some time for inflation to return to 2%, even with appropriate monetary policy," said Hammack.
While a decline in energy prices would help move inflation in the right direction, she said the demand-side pressures are not "self-correcting."
"They are likely to be more persistent and usually require a more restrictive policy to rein them in," said Hammack.
Headline inflation as measured by the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — the Personal Consumption Expenditures index — was 3.4% in August . Adjusting for volatile food and energy prices, it was 3%. Both are above year-earlier levels, when headline was 2.7% and core was 2.9%. Officials will get a fresh reading on inflation this week when the Consumer Price Index — a different inflation measure — is released.
Hammack warned that with each month inflation remains high, it raises the risk of an inflationary mindset becoming entrenched in the economy. She said she's beginning to see signs from business contacts that may be taking hold.
Hammack also laid out how she views interest rates should respond to changes in the economy, what is known in Fed parlance as her "reaction function."
"The reaction function is accountability through transparency," she said. "As a policymaker, I believe I have a responsibility to the public to explain why I make the decisions I make. Transparency has the benefit of allowing consumers, businesses, and financial markets to make better-informed decisions," she said.
She drew a sharp line between a reaction function and forward guidance.
"Treating the two as interchangeable can turn a well-meaning explanation of logic into the expectations of a promise," she said.
