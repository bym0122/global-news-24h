OPEC+ Oil Production Rises 900,000 bbl/d in September Despite Hormuz Disruptions &#8211; Monthly Report Says
The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) has reported that crude oil production by OPEC+ members increased by 900,000 barrels per day (bbl/d) month on month (MoM) to average 39.07 million barrels per day (mmbbl/d) in September, driven primarily by higher output from Saudi Arabia, according to its Monthly Oil Market Report (MOMR).
Released on October 13, the report highlighted that the increase in the output of the OPEC+ bloc came amid ongoing disruptions to oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz, as geopolitical tensions in the Middle East continued to affect regional crude flows and shipping activity.
According to secondary sources cited in the report, OPEC member countries increased their combined crude oil production by 920,000 bbl/d to 25.10 mmbbl/d in September. Production from non-OPEC countries participating in the Declaration of Cooperation (DoC) declined by 20,000 bbl/d to 13.97 mmbbl/d.
Saudi Arabia recorded the largest increase among OPEC members. Data based on direct communications showed that the kingdom's crude oil production reached 8.37 mmbbl/d in September, compared with 6.24 mmbbl/d in August. Its supply to the market increased by 866,000 bbl/d MoM to 7.99 mmbbl/d.
The production increase coincided with a sharp rise in oil prices. OPEC's Reference Basket climbed by $24.15 per barrel MoM to average $110.59 per barrel in September. Brent crude futures rose by $13.94 per barrel to $102.02, while West Texas Intermediate (WTI) futures increased by $13.24 to $95.69.
OPEC attributed the price gains to heightened geopolitical tensions, disruptions to regional crude flows, and strong demand for prompt deliveries.
The report also highlighted a surge in tanker freight rates as disrupted trade flows, longer voyages, and reduced vessel availability tightened shipping capacity.
Meanwhile, OPEC revised its global oil demand growth forecast for 2026 slightly downward to 200,000 bbl/d, bringing average demand to 105.7 mmbbl/d.
The organization expects demand to rebound in 2027, increasing by 2.6 mmbbl/d to 108.3 mmbbl/d, partly reflecting anticipated inventory replenishment.
On the supply side, liquids production from countries outside the DoC is forecast to increase by 600,000 bbl/d in both 2026 and 2027. Brazil, the United States, Canada, and Argentina are expected to lead growth in 2026.
OPEC+ is an alliance of OPEC and non-OPEC oil-producing countries, including Russia, that coordinate oil production policies to support oil market stability. Established in 2016 under the DoC, OPEC+ seeks to promote cooperation among participating countries on oil market management.

Saudi Arabia Plans Massive Expansion of Oil Pipeline to Bypass Hormuz
Saudi Arabia plans to raise the capacity of its East-West onshore oil pipeline that helps it bypass the Strait of Hormuz to 10 million barrels per day (bpd) within two to three years, Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman said at the WPC Energy Congress in Riyadh.
The pipeline, which returned to service after a two-week suspension following drone attacks in September, currently carries about 5.8 million bpd, the minister said last week.
The 740-mile-long pipeline, also known as Petroline, has a maximum capacity of 7 million bpd at present.
Despite the ongoing conflict and disruptions, Saudi Arabia plans to add 3 million bpd to the East-West pipeline, which became a lifeline for Saudi oil exports after the closure of the Strait of Hormuz.
Thanks to the pipeline, Saudi Arabia managed to divert most of its crude oil exports away from the choked Strait of Hormuz and ships increasingly larger volumes via the Yanbu port on the Red Sea.
The route to bypass Hormuz is not without risks, either. The Iran-aligned Houthis in Yemen vowed in July to halt Saudi shipments through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, which is the shortest route for Saudi oil supply from Yanbu to reach Asian markets.
Still, Prince Abdulaziz on Monday touted the energy resilience of the Kingdom, telling the WPC Energy Congress that energy resilience "must also be measured by the ability to maintain, repair, reroute, substitute and recover. Above all, it must be measured by the ability to continue delivering under pressure."
Saudi Aramco's chief executive officer, Amin Nasser, said at the same event that "In alignment with the Ministry of Energy, we are studying additional crude export routes, and working on expanding overseas storage."
Aramco's top executive added that the company is "also focused on mitigating risks and limiting damage, using frontier technologies to inspect facilities and accelerate restoration."

Iranian attacks target 6 ships near Strait of Hormuz in 1 day, highest since war with US began
More than 18 vessels targeted during first 12 days of October, according to UK maritime agency, international security sources
Ahmet Dursun, Mohammad Kara Maryam, and Lina Altawell
ISTANBUL Iranian attacks on vessels in and around the Strait of Hormuz have reached their highest pace since the war with the US began on Feb. 28, with six ships targeted in a single day, according to data from the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency (UKMTO) and international maritime security sources.
Data from UKMTO and maritime security sources showed that more than 18 commercial vessels were targeted by Iran during the first 12 days of October, including six on Monday.
Between Oct. 5 and 12 alone, at least 14 attacks, attempted attacks and interceptions involving vessels were recorded in and around the strait, according to the same sources.
UKMTO data and statements by Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) indicated a marked rise in attacks on commercial shipping since clashes between Tehran and Washington resumed in July.
At least 12 attacks on oil tankers and cargo ships were recorded in July, while at least 10 vessels were targeted in August, including oil and liquefied natural gas tankers off the UAE port of Fujairah and Oman’s Sohar.
The number rose to 18 vessels in September as the US and Gulf countries increasingly resorted to ship-to-ship transfers, using smaller vessels to transport oil before loading it onto larger tankers in safer areas.
The IRGC claimed that the operations were “illegal oil smuggling and an attempt to break the Hormuz blockade” and expanded its operations to include supply vessels, tugboats and small tankers in the Gulf.
The IRGC also claimed that some targeted vessels were damaged by explosions after striking naval mines while sailing “illegally” through routes it considered “unsafe.”
Tehran says the escalation in its operations against shipping is a response to a “US naval blockade,” while warning vessels against using routes it has not approved or crossing the area after switching off navigation and identification systems.
Since April, Iran has imposed restrictions on maritime traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, including designated transit routes and a requirement for prior coordination with the IRGC Navy.
On May 30, Tehran announced that vessels passing through the strait would be required to obtain prior authorization from the IRGC Navy, warning that transit without permission would constitute a “security threat.”
The developments come amid continued tensions between Iran and the US over navigation through the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world’s most important strategic routes for energy and commodity supplies and a key issue in negotiations between the two sides.

Strategic Vulnerabilities Translate into Price Shocks
Southeast Asia is structurally vulnerable to geopolitical energy shocks. Since 2010, fossil fuels have met nearly 80% of the region’s rapidly growing energy demand . Although some countries in the region produce oil and gas, they are not entirely self-sufficient (see Table 1). For others that rely heavily on imports, supply shocks hit harder. Unlike East Asian countries, most Southeast Asian countries have very limited strategic stockpiles as a buffer.
The oil and gas price hike is an immediate fiscal burden for Southeast Asian countries. The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) estimated that monthly import costs would increase by $3.36 billion . Because oil and gas account for 90% of the transportation sector and 31% of the electricity sector's needs in the region, price shocks have rippled through society, translating into higher estimated inflation across countries.
Governments Respond with Conservation and Subsidies
Countries soon rolled out extreme energy-conservation measures, but only for the public sector. They cut workdays, canceled travel, and limited air-conditioning in government buildings (See Table 2). These measures aimed to reduce demand among civil servants and encourage the public to change their behavior. However, governments didn’t impose large-scale rationing for industries or households for fear of political turmoil .
Many governments are doubling down on fossil fuel subsidies. Before the crisis, Indonesia, Malaysia, Viet Nam, Thailand, and the Philippines directly subsidized fossil fuels to lower prices, reaching $54.2 billion in total subsidies in 2024 and accounting for significant shares of each country’s GDP (see Table 3). After the crisis, Indonesia announced an additional $28.4 billion to fund fossil fuel subsidies and compensate state-owned energy and utility companies. Subsidies also jumped sevenfold in Malaysia and will reach $9.8 billion in 2026, which is 9.5% of the annual budget.
Crisis Mitigation Efforts Could Hamper Power Systems Long-Term
A crowding-out effect will undermine governments’ abilities to pursue other priorities. According to Indonesia’s Minister of Finance , the new fossil fuel subsidies will come from “spending cuts at ​some government agencies.” The response pattern follows what these governments did in 2022, when energy prices rose following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. In Indonesia, energy subsidies in 2022 accounted for 2.8% of the GDP, exceeding government expenditure on education, social protection, health, and economic affairs.
Governments must strengthen the power system while navigating energy shocks. Southeast Asia's grid has long faced challenges from a varied landscape, frequent natural disasters, densely concentrated urban demand, and growing intermittent renewables. Southeast Asia needs to more than double its grid capacity to meet projected demand by 2050. Doing so will require investing 14 times the amount invested since the 1970s, especially as countries focus on job-creating, energy-intensive sectors. The grid’s inability to keep up with industrial demand can create multiple problems. For example, the rapid expansion of nickel processing has driven up the construction of off-grid/captive coal generation in Indonesia.
1. How to supply electricity at manageable costs while keeping up with demand growth?
Industrial plans mean further surges in electricity demand. Indonesia, Malaysia, Viet Nam, Thailand, and the Philippines have industrial policies that require large amounts of electricity. They hope to expand data centers, mineral processing, semiconductor manufacturing, and electric vehicle manufacturing. According to the International Energy Agency, this means that the region’s electricity demand will grow by 4.4% annually to 2035 and more than double by 2050.
With ongoing uncertainty in the international energy market, these governments will have to figure out how to leverage supply- and demand-side policy tools to mitigate shocks while keeping up with a growing thirst for energy.
2. How to manage demand without further straining the power systems?
A surge in demand strains the grid, especially where capacity is limited. A month-long on-and-off blackout in Northern Viet Nam in 2024 cost the country $1.4 billion , equivalent to 0.3% of its GDP. Data centers, which demand intensive and stable electricity, can have sharp ramp-up and ramp-down profiles and pose new forecasting challenges and outage risks for system operators. Industries the region actively seeks to develop, including electric vehicles, semiconductors, and other types of advanced manufacturing, are also highly sensitive to power outages.
The region has to figure out how to bridge the persistent investment gap in new grid infrastructure and manage existing grid vulnerabilities in order to pursue economic growth driven by electricity-intensive industrial users.
Conclusion: Southeast Asia’s Future Growth Depends on Energy
The crisis response measures Southeast Asian countries rolled out are unsustainable. They drain governments’ ability to manage other priorities, including industrial growth. To meet their growth and development priorities, governments need a multi-pronged energy security strategy that plans for supply shocks while investing in energy infrastructure. Without this dual approach, both industries and consumers will continue to suffer from price hikes, unstable supply, and limited investment due to the fiscal pressures facing these governments.
