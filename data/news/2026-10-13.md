Oil Jumps 2% as Saudi Attacks and Tanker Strikes Revive Hormuz Fears
Oil prices climbed to their highest levels in nearly two weeks on Monday morning as fresh attacks on Saudi Arabia and oil tankers near the Strait of Hormuz put Middle East supply risks back at the center of the market, outweighing President Donald Trump's announcements of additional Russian diesel supplies and a Russia-Ukraine energy ceasefire.
West Texas Intermediate was trading at $93.78 per barrel, up $1.93, or 2.10%, on Monday morning, while Brent crude rose $2.09, or 2.00%, to $106.80 per barrel, according to Oilprice.com data.
The renewed buying followed a weekend of attacks on Saudi Arabia, including a Houthi strike on Riyadh's international airport on Saturday that killed 13 people and wounded more than 300. Further attacks were reported Sunday, while two tankers were struck near Oman and in the Strait of Hormuz.
The tanker attacks threaten to interrupt the recovery in Gulf oil exports , which reached 81% of prewar levels in September. Crude and condensate shipments had recovered to 91%, but refined-product exports remained at just 60%.
Goldman Sachs raised its December Brent forecast to $90 per barrel from $85, estimating that geopolitical risks are currently adding just over $20 per barrel to oil prices.
The price increase comes as OPEC has sharply reduced its expectations for global oil demand growth this year. OPEC Secretary General Haitham Al Ghais said Monday that the producer group now expects demand to grow by just 400,000 barrels per day in 2026, down from the 1.4 million bpd it forecast at the beginning of the year.
OPEC expects much of the demand deferred during this year's disruptions to return in 2027. The group had already cut its 2026 growth estimate to 380,000 bpd in September, meaning Monday's 400,000-bpd figure is actually a slight increase from last month's forecast even though it represents a 1-million-bpd reduction from OPEC's outlook at the beginning of the year.
The International Energy Agency expects an even deeper demand hit this year, forecasting a 2.5-million-bpd decline in global consumption in 2026 followed by a 2.6-million-bpd rebound in 2027.
Additional Russian barrels could put some downward pressure on fuel prices. Trump said Friday that Russia would immediately supply more than 300,000 metric tons , or roughly 2.25 million barrels, of diesel to U.S. and global markets, with additional supplies to follow. Moscow has also partially lifted restrictions on diesel exports, allowing another 500,000 tons, or about 3.7 million barrels, onto the international market.
Trump also announced Sunday that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to stop attacking each other's energy infrastructure. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky accused Russia of violating the agreement on Monday.
In the Gulf of Mexico, Shell , Chevron and BP were preparing to restore offshore production after Hurricane Isaias disrupted operations over the weekend.

Microsoft's Maia 300 AI Chip Aims To Make AI Cheaper And Faster
As Microsoft continues to build-out multi-gigawatt cloud and AI infrastructure deployments, its custom silicon remains a major focus. Today Microsoft reiterated that its Maia 200 is production-proven and deployed across regions in the United States, Europe, and Australia, and that the next-generation Maia 300, which reportedly boosts AI inference performance significantly over the previous-gen parts, is coming in 2027.
For those unfamiliar, the Azure Maia 200 is a dedicated AI processor that is already actively running limited Microsoft Copilot workloads, OpenAI models , internal Microsoft AI (MAI) models, and many open-source frameworks. Microsoft claims Maia 200, which is designed for real-time inference and model development, offers 1.7x higher token throughput per watt for core AI features in Microsoft Teams, Copilot, and Cowork use-cases compared to leading GPUs.
According to Microsoft, the next-generation Azure Maia 300 is a ground-up redesigned, inference-first architecture tailored specifically for memory-intensive, long-context reasoning, and latency-critical agentic tasks. The company is claiming Maia 300 will offer 40% more tokens per megawatt than competing GPU solutions, with a full 96-accelerator rack configuration pushing up to 7&times; the rack-scale token throughput of Maia 200. A full Maia 300 rack will feature 96 accelerators within a 167-kilowatt power envelope, with 27.6 TB of aggregate HBM capacity and 1.7 PB/s of aggregate HBM bandwidth per rack.
Maia 300 Features And Specifications - Image:Microsoft
Like a number of other architectures announced over the last few months, efficient, optimized data movement was a major design focus of the Maia 300. The architecture combines AI-assisted kernel optimization and on-die SRAM, which will act as a "software-managed scratchpad" to optimize access to memory across compute and the fabric layer, to minimize the need to access the HBM.
The Maia 300: Big Memory Bandwidth And Optimized Data Movement
The Maia 300 features 8 stacks of HBM4 memory to deliver an impressive 18.0 TB/s of peak memory bandwidth within a stout 950-watt Thermal Design Power (TDP). It also leverages a modular chiplet architecture separating compute, memory controllers, and I/O blocks. The Maia 300's compute chiplets are built on TSMC's advanced N2P process node, but information about the other chiplets wasn't mentioned in Microsoft's announcement.
The Maia 300 also features advanced low-precision support for various datatypes, including enhanced MXFP4, to compress memory footprints and lower bandwidth consumption. The chip features high-speed tensor-parallel interconnects as well, with a custom transport protocol over standard Ethernet-based infrastructure. According to Microsoft, this allows seamless scale-up networking from single-racks to multi-gigawatt many-rack datacenter deployments.
By co-designing and optimizing the silicon, software stacks, and cloud infrastructure, Microsoft's believes its Maia architecture will be a highly-efficiency foundation for next-generation frontier AI within Azure.

Fed's Hammack warns inflation is persistent, favors more restrictive policy
Cleveland Federal Reserve president Beth Hammack said Monday that inflation is broad-based and will take time to come back down, even with a boost in interest rates.
Hammack said she is looking toward more frequent, smaller rate adjustments to ensure an inflationary mindset doesn't take hold.
"I see inflation pressures as relatively broad-based rather than concentrated in one component," Hammack said, pointing to rising energy prices , tariffs , and AI. "And it's not just the supply side. Demand-side pressures coming from the AI build-out are raising prices."
Hammack noted that while a one-time increase in tariffs could potentially be viewed as a one-time increase in price levels, multiple waves of tariff increases have resulted in a series of cost shocks.
She also noted how the AI build-out is raising prices on key computer, electronic, and high-tech components , which is raising costs for data centers, but also for projects that use similar components in aviation, electricity production, and construction.
She says when she talks with contacts across her district, she hears about additional cost pressures in health insurance, plastics, metals, grains, electricity, and other areas. Hammack noted price pressures are also showing up in core services inflation excluding housing, a category that shouldn't be especially exposed to tariffs or energy.
"Because of the breadth of inflationary pressures, my baseline forecast is that it will take some time for inflation to return to 2%, even with appropriate monetary policy," said Hammack.
While a decline in energy prices would help move inflation in the right direction, she said the demand-side pressures are not "self-correcting."
"They are likely to be more persistent and usually require a more restrictive policy to rein them in," said Hammack.
Headline inflation as measured by the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — the Personal Consumption Expenditures index — was 3.4% in August . Adjusting for volatile food and energy prices, it was 3%. Both are above year-earlier levels, when headline was 2.7% and core was 2.9%. Officials will get a fresh reading on inflation this week when the Consumer Price Index — a different inflation measure — is released.
Hammack warned that with each month inflation remains high, it raises the risk of an inflationary mindset becoming entrenched in the economy. She said she's beginning to see signs from business contacts that may be taking hold.
Hammack also laid out how she views interest rates should respond to changes in the economy, what is known in Fed parlance as her "reaction function."
"The reaction function is accountability through transparency," she said. "As a policymaker, I believe I have a responsibility to the public to explain why I make the decisions I make. Transparency has the benefit of allowing consumers, businesses, and financial markets to make better-informed decisions," she said.
She drew a sharp line between a reaction function and forward guidance.
"Treating the two as interchangeable can turn a well-meaning explanation of logic into the expectations of a promise," she said.

Cleveland Fed&#x27;s Beth Hammack backs smaller, more frequent rate hikes
The Cleveland Fed president said she prefers incremental policy adjustments over waiting and risking a larger correction later
By Cris Tolomia · 2 min read · Updated October 12, 2026
Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland President Beth Hammack said Monday that she favors making smaller, more frequent adjustments to interest rates rather than delaying action and potentially requiring a larger move later.
“When feasible, I generally think it’s preferable to make smaller adjustments, and to do so relatively quickly, rather than to wait and risk needing a larger course correction,” Hammack said in a speech at the National Association for Business Economics annual meeting in Cleveland.
Hammack, who votes on policy decisions this year, said she views inflation pressures as relatively broad-based rather than concentrated in any single component of the economy. A restrictive monetary policy stance is appropriate to ensure inflation returns to the Fed&#x27;s 2% target in a timely fashion, she said.
Much of the speech centered on the value of transparency, with Hammack arguing that officials should articulate the logic behind their decisions — a so-called "reaction function" — while stopping short of offering "firm predictions" about where rates are headed. "I want to draw a sharp line here. A reaction function is not forward guidance," Hammack said, according to Bloomberg . "Treating the two as interchangeable can turn a well-meaning explanation of logic into the expectations of a promise."
Hammack also said that with each passing month, persistently elevated inflation raises the risk of an inflationary mindset becoming entrenched in the economy. She stopped short of backing a rate increase at the Fed’s Oct. 27-28 meeting, according to Bloomberg.
Hammack&#x27;s preference for steady, incremental tightening reflects a position she has held for months. She argued in August that more than one rate increase would likely be needed to bring inflation back under control and that interest rates at the time were not placing meaningful restraint on the economy. She dissented at the Fed&#x27;s July meeting, when the committee voted 9-3 to hold rates steady, preferring a quarter-point increase alongside Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari and Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan.
The Fed ultimately raised its benchmark rate by a quarter percentage point in September , a unanimous 12-0 decision that brought the federal funds rate to a target range of 3.75%–4%. The decision reflected a shared view among Fed officials that rising prices — fueled by the Iran war and surging demand connected to artificial intelligence buildout — required a policy response, according to Bloomberg. Sixteen of 18 meeting participants at that time projected at least one additional rate increase.
