Last month, investing legend Warren Buffett stepped down from his position as CEO of Berkshire Hathaway . This legendary holding company has significantly outperformed the S&P 500 ( ^GSPC -1.11% ) in the roughly six decades that he has been in charge, delivering an average annual return of over 19% compared to the market&#x27;s average of 10%.
But while Buffett is no longer in charge of Berkshire (shifting to a more advisory role as chairman emeritus), investors still look to his wisdom when deciding how to navigate difficult situations. Let&#x27;s discuss some reasons why the current stock market boom may be on shaky footing and see what lessons Buffett can give to help investors stay ahead.
After a brief dip during the COVID-19 pandemic, stocks have been on a tear, with the S&P 500 delivering an average annual return of 23% between 2022 and 2025. This is mainly due to optimism about a new technology called generative artificial intelligence (AI), which has led to a scramble for expensive data center equipment, resulting in booming growth for the tech giants that design and manufacture it.
The recent performance of AI infrastructure stocks has been nothing short of breathtaking, with top performers like Sandisk up over 1,000% in the last 12 months because of soaring demand for their products. AI-focused data center providers like the recently IPO&#x27;d Space Exploration Technologies (aka SpaceX) have also captivated Wall Street&#x27;s attention.

The situation seems similar to the dot-com bubble in the late 1990s, when the emergence of the internet led to a similar boom in tech valuations.
Right now, the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (P/E) multiple of the S&P 500 stands at 41.8. This number is well above its average of 17.4. And the only time it has been higher is in late 1999, when it peaked at 44 shortly before the dot-com bubble turned into a market crash.
In a May interview with CNBC, Buffett criticized the current mood in the stock market, stating, "It&#x27;s tough to find values when everybody is preferring gambling." Famously cautious, Buffett tends to prefer buying stable and low-priced businesses with easy-to-understand business models. A speculative industry like generative AI isn&#x27;t his usual cup of tea.
That said, Buffett has limited exposure to the megatrend through diversified, consumer-focused tech giants like Alphabet , Apple , and Amazon rather than pure-play chipmakers. Some might credit Buffett&#x27;s AI strategy to luck instead of skill (many of these investments were made long before the release of ChatGPT). But either way, these picks show how investors can gain exposure to a growth opportunity while minimizing downside risk.
The most interesting thing about Buffett&#x27;s investing strategy might not be the stocks he buys. Instead, it is what he is not buying. Under his leadership, Berkshire Hathaway has stuck with its stable, profitable technology leaders rather than chasing the biggest returns in less diversified AI stocks that have the furthest to fall if things go south.
Berkshire Hathaway is also sitting on a $365.5 billion cash pile, which will allow it to pursue deals in the event of a market downturn. It might pay to follow Buffett&#x27;s lead.

### 48. ★★★☆☆ US inflation rises more than expected - here's what is happening with prices

US inflation rose by more than expected in the year to September, according to official figures, in the final data release before the midterm elections next month.
The pace of price rises increased to 3.7%, up from 3.4% in the 12 months to August.
Inflation was above forecasts, driven by higher petrol prices, which have risen following disruption to Middle East energy supplies due to the US-Israel conflict with Iran.
The elections are being fought over cost-of-living pressures across the US.
Inflation is the rate at which the price of products or services increases over time.
For example, if a bottle of milk costs $1 but rises to $1.10 a year later, then annual inflation for bottled milk is 10%.
Officials from the Bureau of Labor Statistics measure inflation through the consumer price index (CPI).
It tracks the price changes of hundreds of day-to-day items including fresh food, gasoline and medical services to work out the overall level of inflation in the US economy.
The bureau updates this so-called basket of goods over time as peoples&#x27; spending and lifestyle habits change.
There are several factors that can cause inflation to rise.
If demand for a product outstrips its supply, that can lead to producers raising prices - the craze for Labubu dolls in recent years and the high resale prices are an example.
Higher incomes can also lead to higher demand, for example, which in turn pushes up prices.
Image caption, Supply and demand: Labubu dolls illustrate how inflation can rise
Supply can also affect inflation. If supply of a product is reduced, but demand remains constant, that can cause prices to rise. A good example of this is crude oil, which is used to make gasoline and diesel.
Before the US-Israel war with Iran began, it was trading at just over $70 a barrel, but when the conflict constricted crude supplies, the price quickly rose and is currently over $100.
Inflation has risen to 3.7% in the year to September, which is a higher-than-expected increase fuelled by petrol prices.
Vehicle fuel prices jumped by 34.2% over 12 months.
In September alone, they jumped by 9.1%, accounting for more than half of the increase in inflation, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
Overall, energy inflation was up by 20.4% over the year as prices for fuel oil, energy services and electricity climbed. Fuel oil, used in home heating, was up 74.2% over the year.
Experts said the US Federal Reserve - which sets the country&#x27;s interest rate - will be closely watching to see if energy costs begin to affect other areas of the economy, such as food prices, and drive inflation higher.
At the moment, food inflation remains subdued. It was flat over the month of September, and was 2.5% across the year.
Core inflation, which strips out food and energy prices, was 2.6%.
Will the inflation hike change Social Security payments?
Shortly after the latest inflation figure was released, the Social Security Administration announced that Social Security benefits including old age, survivors and disability insurance would rise by a similar amount - 3.6% - in 2027.
The administration said that the 3.6% rise will mean an average retirement benefit increase of $75 a month.
The cost-of-living adjustment will take effect in January for about 72 million people, while increased payments for the 7.2m receiving the supplemental security income will start on 31 December.
Since coming into office for the second time, President Donald Trump has introduced tariffs on most US trading partners around the world.
While most of the additional global levies were outlawed by the Supreme Court in February, the president immediately announced a new 10% global tariff to replace them.
Trump argued the tariffs would increase America&#x27;s tax earnings, boost manufacturing and encourage people to buy US-made goods, but the higher import taxes have also had an impact on inflation, according to research from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York.
Image caption, US President Donald Trump raised tariffs
Higher tariffs - which are levies that businesses pay on the goods they import - directly raise prices for US companies unless the overseas firms they are buying from lower prices to absorb some of the cost.
In response to the new tariffs, the New York Fed found exporters reduced prices very little, meaning US-based businesses ended up absorbing nearly 90% of the cost of the added tax.
Indirectly, higher tariffs also affected the prices of locally made goods, as many US producers rely on parts or materials from overseas.
Overall, the Fed found that every percentage point increase in average tariffs led to nearly a 0.25% price increase for consumer goods after one year.
While inflation is currently above where the US Federal Reserve would like - it has a target of 2% - it has been much higher in previous years.
During the Covid pandemic, issues such as supply chain disruption started pushing inflation up.
Additionally, in early 2022 when Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, oil prices surged over $100 a barrel.
Both of these factors helped to drive inflation to a peak of about 9%.
