How War Is Choking Global Oil Tanker Traffic in the Strait of Hormuz and Beyond
Gulf crude exports have recovered, but tanker traffic through Hormuz, the Red Sea, and the Black Sea remains far below normal, at a steep cost.
Recent reports that crude oil flows have been restored should be read with care. They include crude routed around the Strait of Hormuz , not only crude moving through it. The Energy Policy Research Foundation (EPRINC) examined this distinction in its March 30, 2026, Chart of the Week (2026-14), “ Strait of Hormuz Closure : The Arithmetic of the Crude Shortfall after Five Weeks of Policy.” Shipping data through Hormuz and other key choke points show how far traffic remains from normal.
Why Gulf Oil Exports Have Recovered but Hormuz Traffic Has Not
According to Kpler data, Gulf crude exports excluding Iran averaged roughly 16.5 million barrels per day in September, matching the prewar average. The routing has changed, though. About 40 percent of those barrels now bypass the strait, compared with 17 percent before the war, mostly via Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline to Yanbu on the Red Sea and the United Arab Emirates’ pipeline to Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman.
How War Has Disrupted Tanker Traffic at Global Choke Points
Over the past five years, regional wars have repeatedly disrupted the global economy, and the clearest evidence is at the world’s maritime choke points. Vessel transits and cargo volumes have fallen sharply at several of them, and tanker traffic has been hit hardest. That matters because tankers carry the crude oil, refined fuels, chemicals, and other liquids on which commerce and everyday life depend.
According to EPRINC research and using the International Monetary Fund’s PortWatch data , the Strait of Hormuz shows the disruption most starkly. The seven-day moving average of daily tanker volumes fell from more than 2 million metric tons (mt) in March 2025 to 264,000 mt in March 2026, a drop of roughly 87 percent. Daily tanker transits fell by a similar share, from 45 to 6.
The effects spilled over into Asia. Tanker volumes through the Strait of Malacca fell from 3.5 million mt to 2.8 million mt over the same period, a smaller but still significant decline of about 20 percent.
Hormuz is the latest, but not the first, choke point to be disrupted. After Houthi forces began attacking shipping in the southern Red Sea in late 2023, tanker volumes through the Bab el-Mandeb fell from 1.2 million mt in January 2023 to 806,000 mt in January 2024, a decline of about one-third. Tanker transits fell from 25 to 15.
This bears directly on the Hormuz workaround. Crude loaded at Yanbu must still leave the Red Sea through either the Suez Canal or the Bab el-Mandeb, and the East-West pipeline was itself shut from September 11 to September 22.
As Ukraine has stepped up its attacks on Russian energy infrastructure , tanker transits through the Bosphorus fell from 23 in August 2025 to 17 in August 2026. Farther north, tanker traffic through the Kerch Strait, already small at about 35,000 mt in late 2021 before Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, has since fallen to nil.
Rerouting has kept oil moving, but as  Standard Chartered puts it , “less efficiently and at considerably higher cost.” The  UK Maritime Trade Operations  center recorded nine attacks on vessels in a single week in early October, and  Brent crude remains above $100  a barrel, against roughly $72 before the war.

Gulf economies are expected to shrink sharply this year as long-term disruptions to energy production and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz outweigh the benefits of higher oil and gas prices, according to the International Monetary Fund’s latest forecasts.
The October World Economic Outlook points to particularly severe declines in economies most exposed to interruptions in hydrocarbon output and transport, with Kuwait, Qatar and Bahrain facing double-digit contractions.
The deterioration highlights a central feature of the regional shock: higher global energy prices offer limited protection when producers cannot maintain production or deliver exports to customers.
The damage also extends beyond hydrocarbons, as weaker trade, higher costs and uncertainty weigh on business activity and complicate efforts to diversify Gulf economies.
The IMF projects Kuwait's economy will contract 16.7% in 2026, while Qatar is expected to shrink 15.6% and Bahrain 11.3%, according to published accounts of the October report.
Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates are also forecast to contract, although by smaller margins, reflecting differences in economic structure and exposure to disrupted export routes.
Saudi Arabia's economy is projected to shrink 3.3% this year, a sharp reversal from the 1.7% growth forecast in July. The change represents a five-percentage-point downgrade in just three months.
The IMF expects Saudi growth to rebound to 7.9% in 2027, compared with its previous projection of 5.5%, indicating that part of the activity lost this year could return as conditions improve.
The UAE is forecast to contract 3.9% in 2026 before expanding 7.7% next year. Outside the six-member Gulf Cooperation Council, Iraq faces a projected 16% contraction, underlining the broader exposure of regional energy exporters to the disruption.
These figures are projections for annual real economic output, rather than confirmed full-year outcomes, and remain sensitive to the course of the conflict and the restoration of trade.

For the past decade, oil and gas investors have de-emphasised how long a company’s reserves would last, instead rewarding capital discipline, returns on existing assets, payout consistency, and balance sheet strength. But shareholders are again beginning to factor portfolio longevity into their valuation calculations, for the first time since the 1998–2008 supercycle.
This reflects the current reality of the energy transition. Demand for oil and gas has proven stickier than expected, while a decade of capital discipline has thinned and shortened the investment pipelines for future supply. Recent geopolitical events have heightened the urgency around energy security. High-quality resources are becoming more concentrated in limited-entry markets. In this demanding context, ensuring portfolio longevity is not about boosting overall production, but rather replacing existing output as it is depleted.
We’ve found that companies can achieve similar volume outcomes through pathways that have very different risk-return profiles. The companies that outperform will be those that select pathways to replenish oil and gas reserves proactively, based on where they have a genuine right to win. Three broad ways to augment portfolio longevity:
Exploration. Some companies can pursue exploration, but this won’t work for all and is unlikely to be the main source of new reserves through 2040. Most of what the industry needs is expected to come from existing production or from resources under development or already discovered. However, for players with access, technical capability, and financial structuring discipline, there are opportunities in the Atlantic margin, the Eastern Mediterranean, and selective markets across Africa.
Mergers and acquisitions is another option for companies seeking to replenish their oil and gas reserves. For companies with high valuations, it enables them to use their paper as a sought-after currency to achieve their renewal plans. In terms of existing resource bases, innovations in equipment have already transformed the economics of offshore recovery and continue to do so. AI-enabled subsurface modelling is creating further headroom. The most consequential frontier may be applying enhanced oil recovery technologies to shale. In markets where resources are controlled by host states, the basis for partnership rests on what a company can offer, and those with deep technical capabilities are more likely to gain access.
Companies do not enter the approaching cycle from the same starting position. The right strategic response to an increasingly challenging upstream environment depends on where a player sits across two dimensions: the depth of its existing portfolio, and the strategic flexibility its valuation provides (via equity-financed M&A, cheaper capital, and the ability to act decisively when others cannot).
Based on these dimensions, BCG’s analysis places leading oil and gas companies in one of four categories for pursuing portfolio longevity:
Deploy and extend. Companies with both portfolio depth and strategic flexibility should extend their resource positions selectively within the narrowing set of high-quality opportunities, based on genuine competitive advantage. Their risk is not scarcity of options but maintaining discipline in choosing from among them.
Act now. Companies with strong valuations but thinning portfolios can make accretive acquisitions that are impossible for lower-valued peers. They may be able to access resource positions that geopolitical volatility renders unavailable in the future. The risk is overpaying for assets or spreading capital thinly across too many positions.
Unlock the value. Players with strong portfolios but weaker valuations must first develop a credible narrative that connects portfolio quality to future cash generation. This also involves demonstrating oil and gas delivery, simplifying the business, and adding balance sheet discipline. Until their valuation gap closes, their strategic flexibility will remain constrained.
Define and focus. Players with weaker portfolio depth and more limited financial flexibility face the hardest decisions. Pursuing growth without advantage will destroy value. Instead, they should leverage technology deployment to increase recovery factors and utilise aggressive high-grading around a genuinely advantaged core of resource positions. Partnerships are particularly important with high-grading, as a way to create scale and share capital exposure.
Despite differences in portfolio depth and strategic flexibility, all major upstream players bring hard-won strengths, whether in subsurface, project execution, enhanced oil recovery within shale and tight reservoirs, trading, or geopolitical relationship management. Companies need to ask if the strengths they possess today align with the resource pathways that are open to them.
