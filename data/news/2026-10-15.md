History shows that the 10-year/three-month Treasury yield spread has been one of the most reliable recession indicators.
The general premise is that once this spread turns negative, which is to say that the three-month Treasury yield becomes higher than the 10-year yield, a recession is likely to come sometime in the subsequent six to 18 months.
Over the past 60-plus years, this inversion has occurred eight times. All eight times, a recession occurred shortly after this trigger fired.
The ninth time this happened was in late 2022. At its bottom, this spread went to nearly -200 basis points, the lowest reading it&#x27;s ever had. But there was no recession. For the first time in well over half a century, this spread going negative did not precede a recession as it has historically.

The yield curve can steepen for a few reasons. The primary ones are rising inflation expectations, Treasury bond supply, and stronger economic growth.
The current environment features the first two with some of the third. The U.S. inflation rate has been in the 3% to 4% range for most of 2026. That&#x27;s caused long-term yields to rise sharply while short-term yields, which are usually tied to what the Fed does, have moved comparatively little. This is what&#x27;s called a "bear steepener" and is often a bad environment for rate-sensitive sectors.
But current U.S. economic growth is softening the blow. Corporate earnings growth has been strong, and GDP growth has, for now, remained well in positive territory. When these dynamics are in place, stock prices often have room to rise even as long-term interest rates rise.
Outside of the obvious fact that it&#x27;s impossible to predict the future, there&#x27;s also no evidence that a recession or a market correction is imminent. I&#x27;d maintain long-term positions in the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF , the Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF , and similar funds.
