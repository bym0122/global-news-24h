Al Jazeera put a question to analysts: If fighting resumes in full, the Strait of Hormuz is shut off completely and Houthi attacks choke off Bab al-Mandeb, how long could the world’s reserves last?
Strategic oil reserves are stockpiles of crude oil and fuel held by governments or by companies on their behalf for use in a supply emergency.
The International Energy Agency (IEA), which was established in 1974 after the Arab oil embargo, coordinates most of them. Its importing members must hold at least 90 days of net imports. China and India are not members.
How much of the reserves has already been released this year?
On March 11, days after the start of the US-Israel war on Iran, the IEA’s 32 members agreed to make 400 million barrels available from emergency supplies, more than double the volume released after Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.
The US pledged 172.2 million barrels from its Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) and Japan 79.8 million, according to IEA figures.
The SPR has since fallen to 283 million barrels, its lowest level since October 1982, according to the US Department of Energy.
Much of that oil was lent rather than sold and must be returned with extra barrels, according to the US Energy Information Administration.
Far less than before the war began in February, and not all of it can be used.
Aaron Kildow, a senior crude oil analyst at Sparta Commodities, told Al Jazeera that global strategic stocks excluding China and Iran have fallen by nearly 327 million barrels since the end of December 2025 with the US, Europe and Japan accounting for about 95 percent of the draw.
He estimated that leaves only about 400 million barrels of usable reserves worldwide, excluding China’s stockpile, which is unlikely to be exported, and Gulf stocks, which could be cut off by a total closure of Hormuz.
Nearly half of these available stocks are in Japan.
Europe has about 76 million barrels uncommitted, but members such as Germany are holding some back for a local emergency, Kildow said.
“Europe is the real wildcard, both on timing and willingness,” Kildow said.
Meanwhile, China’s onshore crude stocks stand at 1.13 billion barrels, down from 1.23 billion in late February but still enough for about 80 days of refinery intake, Muyu Xu, a senior crude oil analyst at the analytics firm Kpler, told Al Jazeera.
India’s reserves cover just eight days of its imports, the Reuters news agency reported.
What would a full closure of Hormuz and Bab al-Mandeb mean?
Before the war, about one-fifth of the world’s oil and liquefied natural gas supplies, or roughly 20 million barrels of oil a day, passed through the Strait of Hormuz.
Saudi Arabia has increased the amount of crude it transports from its oilfields in the east to its Red Sea port of Yanbu in the west via the East-West Pipeline, thereby bypassing Hormuz.
But there have been strikes on that pipeline, and tankers heading south from Yanbu must pass through Bab a-Mandeb, where the Iran-backed Houthis declared a blockade on Saudi shipping in July.
Only a confirmed 373,000 barrels a day of Saudi crude have transited Bab al-Mandeb so far this month, the lowest level since February, Xu said, with most Yanbu cargoes now heading north via Egypt instead.
If Hormuz were shut and the Houthis blocked all Saudi tankers, Gulf producers could export only through a few pipelines and Yanbu’s northbound route, Xu said.
That would leave about 7.5 million barrels a day of Middle Eastern crude, against 16.8 million now, a shortfall of about 9.3 million barrels a day, she estimated.
“If such attacks occur, the supply disruption could be even more severe and prolonged,” Xu said, referring to strikes on oilfields, terminals and pipelines.

A second shock would send refiners scrambling for supplies from the US, Latin America, West Africa and Russia, Xu said.

Great Britain is expected to pay some of the highest energy costs in Europe this winter as its reliance on imports to ensure a steady supply is forecast to deepen.
Energy system operators have said the country will have enough gas and electricity to meet demand over the coldest months – in part because of paying higher market prices than neighbouring markets to secure imports.
The official winter energy forecasts were published days after Europe’s gas market benchmark climbed to its highest level since December 2022 when Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine sent prices rocketing.
National Gas, which runs Great Britain’s gas networks, set out its forecasts after striking an 11th-hour deal with the owner of a major Belgian pipeline to keep gas flowing into the country this winter by reversing a planned capacity cut.
The energy regulator, Ofgem, approved the deal – which will add around 10p a month to the average gas bill. Without the intervention there was a danger of gas running out in the event of a worst-case scenario where an important part of the country’s gas networks failed as temperatures plummeted.
In addition to gas imports by pipelines, Great Britain is expected to increase imports of seaborne liquified natural gas (LNG) cargoes by about 5% from last winter, according to National Gas forecasts, at a time when market prices are three times higher than they were before the Middle East crisis severely disrupted Gulf supplies.
Its winter outlook report suggested that prices could remain elevated if UK gas buyers need to compete more fiercely with countries in Europe for LNG cargoes amid low gas storage levels across the continent.
Europe ended August on course to enter the cooler months with gas stores at their lowest level in 13 years owing to the slowdown in gas exports from Qatar, one of Europe’s biggest suppliers, and the increase in gas consumption over the summer to power refrigeration and cooling during the record heatwaves caused by the global climate crisis.
The UK has some of the smallest gas stores in Europe after decades of relying on homegrown production from the North Sea. UK production was forecast to fall by 6% this year compared with last winter, but the government’s controversial decision to approve the new Jackdaw gasfield in the North Sea earlier this week, which was not fully accounted for in the winter forecasts, is expected to offer a modest boost to supplies when it starts up next month.
Great Britain is also forecast to be a net importer of electricity over the colder months, according to the National Energy System Operator (Neso) separate outlook report. That is also likely to come at a higher cost owing to tighter European power supplies compared with last winter.
The summer heatwaves reduced river levels across Europe, in a blow to French nuclear output and Norway’s hydroelectric reserves, which have remained below the 10-year minimum since late July.
The UK paid a record £4bn on power imports over the year ending in August, according to figures from the Office for National Statistics this week, of which half was spent on electricity imported from France. It has also made near-record payments over the past year to fire up gas plants while wind and solar farms have been paid to turn off due to bottlenecks in the grid.
Even though Great Britain will use gas power plants less overall this winter compared with previous winters thanks to rising renewable energy levels, the need for gas to play a role during power supply crunches could be 7% higher than previously assumed.
National Gas lifted its gas power demand forecasts for the most extreme electricity supply scenarios – when demand is unusually high and renewable output slumps – after noting over successive winters that gas plants have remained in high use when dark, still winter days lead to high electricity demand and lower renewable energy levels.
Glenn Bryn-Jacobsen, a director at National Gas, said: “While supply diversity and flexibility remain sources of resilience, taken together these trends in demand and supply have resulted in tighter supply margins during periods of high demand and system stress.”

Russian Offensive Campaign Assessment, October 22, 2026
The Kremlin's seeming interest in negotiations aims not to end the war in Ukraine but rather to secure economic concessions from the United States such that Russia can continue funding its war. Various Russian officials tried to present the October 21 meeting between US Special Representative to the Middle East Steve Witkoff and Russian President Vladimir Putin as evidence that Russia is willing to engage in meaningful negotiations to end the war while simultaneously attempting to extract economic concessions from the United States so that Russia can continue funding the war.[1] Russian Direct Investment Fund (RDIF) CEO Kirill Dmitriev, for example, claimed on October 21 that Witkoff and Putin used the October 21 meeting to agree to expand Russian-US economic cooperation and to “continue communicating.”[2] A Kremlin domestic policy official told Russian opposition source Verstka that the Kremlin does not expect negotiations to resolve the war in Ukraine but that Russian officials hope to extract economic concessions, which could boost Russia’s budget and give Russia a military advantage.[3]
Other Russian officials meanwhile demonstrated Russia’s unwillingness to make any meaningful concessions toward ending the war in Ukraine. Kremlin Spokesperson Dmitry Peskov stated on October 22 that Putin did not present any Russian peace proposal to Witkoff during their meeting and that Putin has not considered the composition of a potential Russian delegation for a future Russia-US-Ukraine trilateral meeting.[4] Russian State Duma deputies expressed skepticism on October 21 and 22 that negotiations could end the war and reiterated claims that freezing the current frontlines would be detrimental to Russia's own efforts to end the war.[5] A source close to the Russian Ministry of Foreign Affairs (MFA) told Verstka on October 22 that the Kremlin has been working for over 1.5 years to try to sway the Trump administration to Russia's side and that the Kremlin's current negotiation efforts are emphasizing Ukraine's alleged hopelessness. These are similar to the Kremlin's earlier negotiation tactics which tried to convince the United States and the West generally that Ukrainian defenses were going to collapse such that Ukraine should preemptively capitulate.[6]
The Kremlin is fundamentally interested only in buying time to resolve the war on the battlefield while Russia simultaneously reaps the benefits of economic cooperation with the United States. State Duma Defense Committee First Deputy Chairperson Alexei Zhuravlyov stated on October 21 that Russia must "press" Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky on the battlefield and force Ukraine to "capitulate."[7] Zhuravlyov added that Russia should not rely on diplomacy with the United States because Russian "successes on the ground” will strengthen Russia’s negotiating position.[8] Russian Presidential Aide Yuriy Ushakov similarly claimed that Russian forces maintain the "complete strategic initiative” and are conducting a "relentless offensive” in all key areas of the frontline.[9]
Russian forces are not winning on the battlefield, however, particularly as Ukrainian forces have stymied the planned Russian offensive to seize Donetsk Oblast. ISW has observed evidence that Russian forces suffered a net loss of 241 square kilometers of territory in Ukraine since September 1, 2026. The average rate of daily Russian advances has significantly declined since 2025, with an average of 0.25 square kilometers per day so far in 2026. Ukrainian forces have, comparatively, been contesting and seizing the operational initiative across several sectors of the frontline throughout 2026, including most recently in the Lyman direction with Ukraine's Operation Vivaldi which liberated over 270 square kilometers of territory and thwarted the Russian military command’s plan to encircle the Fortress Belt.[10] Russian forces are also facing mounting monthly casualties while the Russian recruitment rate continues to fall short of its loss rate.[11] Russian forces have suffered almost 37,000 casualties thus far in October 2026 — much higher than the 31,270 casualties for all of October 2025.[12] The Kremlin may face mounting challenges in the coming months, should Ukraine continue to contest the operational initiative and disrupt Russian offensive plans, potentially further pushing Russian commanders to urge Putin to conduct a mobilization in some form.[13]
The Kremlin is likely actively seeking economic cooperation with the United States as Russia faces significant financial strain. Sources familiar with the matter told Bloomberg on October 22 that even a few more months of continued war could exact lasting financial damage on Russian state-owned oil company Rosneft, as Ukrainian strikes against Rosneft's oil refineries have significantly reduced the company's profits and increased its debt.[14] Bloomberg added that repeated Ukrainian strikes have prevented Rosneft from completing repairs and restarting refinery operations and that strikes against complex secondary units that often include Western-sourced parts, like catalytic crackers, reformers, and hydrocrackers, are particularly difficult to repair. Russia remains highly susceptible to international sanctions that restrict its access to the technologically advanced components needed to repair the equipment damaged after Ukraine’s strikes.[15] The Kremlin is therefore pushing the United States for various forms of economic relief, so that Russia can continue funding its war against Ukraine.[16] The Kremlin has continuously sought to extract economic concessions during negotiations to alleviate the pressure on its oil industry — including restrictions on Ukrainian strikes against refineries and the relaxation of US sanctions against diesel exports — while expressing little real interest in ending the war.[17]
Russian forces conducted a massive missile and drone strike against Ukraine overnight on October 21 to 22 following the October 21 meeting between Witkoff and Putin — a continuation of Russia’s pattern of launching large strike packages in the days before and after major peace talks. The Ukrainian Air Force reported that Russian forces conducted a massive combined missile and drone strike primarily targeting Kyiv City and using an unspecified number of Iskander-M, S-400, Zircon, and Onyx missiles and 210 long-range drones, 110 of which were jet-powered.[18] Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky stated that Russian forces conducted three waves of ballistic missile strikes against Kyiv City using 21 missiles, damaging residential, energy, railway, and commercial infrastructure.[19] Zelensky stated that Russian strikes targeted 12 Ukrainian oblasts, killing at least two people and injuring at least 34 people. Russian forces regularly conduct massive strikes in the days right before and after bilateral and trilateral talks, such as the August 2025 US-Russia Alaska summit, the November 2025 US-Ukraine talks in Geneva, the November 2025 US-Russia talks in Moscow, the December 2025 US-Ukrainian-European and US-Russian talks, and the January-February 2026 US-Ukrainian-Russian talks in Abu Dhabi.[20]
Russian forces struck Kyiv City with cluster munitions to purposely harm the civilian population, likely as part of wider Russian efforts to distract from Ukraine’s recent battlefield successes and ultimately force Ukraine into capitulation by making daily life untenable. Zelensky stated that Russian forces struck Pechersk Raion, the historic center of Kyiv City, with three ballistic missiles with cluster warheads that have a dispersion diameter of 400 meters.[21] The strikes damaged monastery buildings and other civilian infrastructure near the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra and the Holy Presentation Monastery. Zelensky noted that there were no military targets in the area and that the objective of the strikes was to cause widespread destruction.[22] Kyiv City Mayor Vitaliy Klitschko stated that Russian forces also struck metro infrastructure on the Southern Bridge over the Dnipro River in Kyiv City and damaged six ambulances at an ambulance station in the city.[23] Ukrainian Presidential Advisor on Defense Technologies Serhiy “Flash” Beskrestnov stated that each Iskander-M ballistic missile’s cluster warhead releases 45 submunitions that detonate in the air and noted that the objective of the strikes was to destroy a portion of the historic center and injure as many people as possible.[24] Cluster munitions have a wide area of effect and are designed to kill enemy personnel or destroy light vehicles, underscoring Russia’s intent to maximize civilian harm and suffering. Cluster munitions historically have a high failure rate, and unexploded bomblets can cause lasting danger for civilians living in affected areas, further demonstrating Russia‘s aim to terrorize civilians.[25]
Russian strikes against Kyiv City are intended to make daily life untenable heading into Winter 2026-2027. Zelensky stated that Russian forces have struck more than 170 Ukrainian energy facilities since October 11 — the date when US President Donald Trump announced that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an “energy ceasefire.”[26] The increasing intensity and brutality of Russia's long-range strike campaign ahead of Winter 2026-2027 highlights the urgency of providing Ukraine with sufficient air defense munitions, reliable low-latency satellite access over Russia, and other Western assistance that allows Ukraine to defend its skies from Russian missiles and drones.
