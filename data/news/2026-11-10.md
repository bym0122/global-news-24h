LNG Traffic Through Hormuz Hits Highest Level Since Iran War Began
Exports of liquefied natural gas via the Strait of Hormuz last month reached the highest total since February, at 31 tankers, compared with 27 in September, according to data from Kpler, as cited by Reuters.
What is perhaps more impressive is that most of these cargoes were loaded from Qatar's Ras Laffan hub, despite it still being under force majeure. In two cases, per Kpler, LNG cargoes loaded at Ras Laffan were then transferred to other tankers once the original vessels had cleared the Strait of Hormuz.
Despite the increase in loadings, the total from Qatar is far from pre-war levels. In February, Qatar's total LNG exports hit close to 6.2 million tons, Kpler said, while the October total stood at 2.7 million tons.
Unlike Qatar, the UAE has managed to restore its exports of liquefied natural gas to pre-war levels, the data suggests. October loadings from Das Island hit seven, unchanged from September.
Iranian attacks on U.S. allies in the Gulf took out 17% of Qatar's export capacity and caused an estimated loss of $20 billion in annual revenue, QatarEnergy said in March. Since then, the company has been scrambling to find ways to service its contracts, despite the force majeure.
Recently, in an unusual twist , Qatar became the largest buyer of U.S. liquefied gas, after sealing two long-term deals with Cheniere Energy to buy up to 4 million tons per year of LNG from Cheniere's Sabine Pass and Corpus Christi export facilities, with deliveries expected to begin in 2031 and continue through 2054.
Despite the recovery in LNG flows out of the Persian Gulf, Rystad Energy earlier this week warned that this winter in the Northern Hemisphere will be extremely uncertain in terms of energy security, with a high likelihood of much higher gas prices because of the tight supply.

Oil Sinks to $94 as Iran’s $8 Billion Hormuz Deal Heads to Trump
Brent crude fell 3.6% to $94.00 per barrel on Monday as Qatar's prime minister headed to the White House with an Iranian proposal for an interim deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. WTI dropped 3% to $88.09, and U.S. natural gas rose 0.8% to $3.02/MMBtu as of 7:26 a.m. ET. The decline extends last week's 16% slide in Brent, the benchmark's steepest weekly fall since the start of the Covid-19 pandemic.
Iran has signed off on the Qatari-brokered terms, according to two diplomatic sources who spoke to Israeli media . Tehran would reopen Hormuz and halt attacks on Gulf tankers. Washington would lift its naval and economic blockade of Iran and release $8 billion in Iranian funds frozen in Qatar. Under the terms, Iran drops its demand to control the strait and charge transit fees but still refuses to put its nuclear program on the table. Neither government has confirmed the terms, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Secretary of War Pete Hegseth reportedly oppose a deal along these lines.
Trump said on Sunday that renewed U.S. strikes on Iran are "highly unlikely" if negotiations produce a strong agreement. Rubio, speaking in Florida the same day, called Iran's demands "outrageous and ridiculous" and said the two sides are not close to a breakthrough. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Iran's offer to reopen Hormuz within two days stands, on condition that the war ends on all fronts.
Shipping through Hormuz came under fire again over the weekend, with Washington and Tehran both reporting attacks on vessels in or near the strait, and the Iran-aligned Houthis claimed a strike on Saudi Aramco's offices in Riyadh on Sunday. Murban crude, the physical Gulf benchmark, climbed 3.4% to $99.17 in Asian trading. A similar U.S.-Iran memorandum reached in Switzerland in May collapsed after an Iranian attack on oil tankers in the strait.

Improving earnings estimates is one of the most reliable, time-tested short-term investment factors. Stocks with positive estimate revisions tend to meaningfully outperform those burdened with declining estimates in the short term.
The macro backdrop provides a sturdy foundation for this growth. We are in the midst of a broader nonresidential construction recovery, which has driven an environment of improving and resilient rental rates. Although admittedly a potential slowdown in data center construction could become a future headwind, broader infrastructure demand remains robust.
A balanced view requires acknowledging the risks. While URI looks cheap relative to the broader market, it is not exactly cheap relative to its own history. The stock is currently trading roughly 20% to 25% above its five-year average multiple. One could easily argue that the historical multiple was simply too cheap for a business of this quality, scale, and capital efficiency. Still, mean reversion back to historical levels of "cheapness" remains a downside risk.
This brings us to the options market. When you have a stock with solid momentum, fundamentals, and positive estimate revisions, but you&#x27;re hesitant to pay a premium to its historical valuation, outright unhedged equity accumulation isn&#x27;t always the optimal route. Instead, selling cash-covered puts offers a highly strategic entry mechanism.

(This approach to entering a stock, then writing covered calls if assigned, is sometimes referred to as "the wheel strategy").
So either we acquire an attractive stock at a price we dictate (the short strike, less the premium collected for selling it) or we simply collect premium for assuming that risk if the stock does not decline. For a company that provides the literal heavy lifting for infrastructure growth, that&#x27;s a structural advantage worth trading.

Slight fall in carbon emissions from fossil fuels as Iran war drives renewables push
However, authors of a comprehensive assessment to be presented at Cop31 warn talk of a global turnaround premature
Soaring oil and gas prices following the war in Iran have encouraged people to look beyond fossil fuels, leading to an easing of climate-damaging carbon emissions this year.
Carbon dioxide from oil, gas and coal is forecast to flatline for 2026, based on data from the year so far, according to a comprehensive assessment by scientists . Emissions from burning oil alone will drop by 2% as drivers economise on fuel use and flock to electric vehicles instead.
The findings, presented on Tuesday morning at the Cop31 climate talks hosted by Turkey in Antalya, show both the impact of the Iran war and the global expansion of cheap renewable energy , chiefly solar and wind power.
Talk of a global turnaround would be premature, however, the report’s authors and other analysts said. The reduction in emissions from fossil fuels for 2026 will be about 0.4% compared with last year.
graph Yet this year’s slight fall could be temporary, and was more than offset by increased emissions from deforestation and land use change, following major fires in south-east Asia and further conversions of wild land to agriculture .
Many factors could disrupt the fragile trend of slowing emissions from fossil fuels, including an intensified expansion of coal in India, an easing of oil prices and US president Donald Trump’s enthusiasm for fossil fuels . Much greater investment in renewable energy, and low-carbon technology such as electric vehicles, would still be required to make permanent reductions in annual emissions, the report’s authors said.
Glen Peters, research professor at the Cicero Centre for International Climate Research, said: “A drop in emissions in one year is not a peak, unless it is driven by structural change. Accelerated deployment of solar, wind, batteries and electric vehicles are pushing emissions down in dozens of countries, and slowing emissions growth in many more, but the changes are not sufficient to lock in a peak in global emissions.”
About 38.2bn tonnes of CO 2 will be emitted this year, according to the Global Carbon Budget, an annual assessment by scientists from scores of universities and science institutions around the world, published in the journal Earth System Science Data .
graph Energy demand continues to increase around the world, as more people gain access to electricity and are pulled out of poverty, but two-thirds of the increase is being met from rising generation from the sun and wind and the uptake of electric vehicles.
On the downside, emissions from deforestation and the conversion of land to agriculture have also ticked upward this year, leading to total carbon emissions rising slightly for the year, from 42.8bn tonnes last year to 43.3bn forecast for 2026.
The scientists warned that the world’s remaining “carbon budget” – the amount of carbon that can be poured into the atmosphere before global heating is likely to permanently exceed 1.5C above pre-industrial levels – was about 150bn tonnes, or less than four years at current rates.
Already, temperatures have exceeded the 1.5C threshold for more than two years , and El Niño conditions are heating the sea and air further. Global emissions must peak imminently and fall rapidly in subsequent years if the world is to have a chance of limiting the “overshoot” of temperatures above 1.5C.
graph China, the world’s biggest emitter of greenhouse gases, is likely to reduce its carbon output by close to 1% this year, mostly because of a 5% reduction in oil use, following the Iran war. Peters said: “China has had flat emissions for the last few years after a fairly steep increase in the last decade . There’s been a lot of discussion about whether China has peaked or not . Whether this is just a one-off and China rebounds next year, we’ll have to wait and see, it’s too early to say.”
India’s emissions have grown strongly, mostly from an increase in coal use, while the country’s oil use has declined slightly. Emissions from the US are likely to fall by about 1.3% this year, as less coal is used, despite Trump’s attempts to boost it.
Bill Hare, chief executive of Climate Analytics, who was not involved in the research, cautioned against taking too much from the report. “The picture from the Global Carbon Budget is a flatline, not a decline – a projected fall of just 0.4% in 2026, a figure that is unfortunately statistically indistinguishable from zero,” he said. “We are heading towards a sustained breach of 1.5C by around 2030. With imminent overshoot, we are entitled to expect governments everywhere to move decisively to cut fossil fuel CO 2 emissions.”
There seemed little sign of such a decisive move at Cop31 , where more than 90 heads of state and government, and high-level representatives from more than 190 countries are gathering for a fortnight of talks on the climate crisis.
Hare said: “We see governments in countries such as Canada, the US and New Zealand rolling back climate policies as fast as they can, and countries such as Australia – co-president of Cop31 – continuing to press forward with massive coal and gas developments that are holding fossil fuel CO 2 emissions at record levels. There is little sign yet that the Turkish and Australian leadership [of Cop31] intends to build on [the commitment to transition away from fossil fuels made three years ago] with stronger action.”
