Hezbollah is leveraging several political and informational lines of effort to deter the Lebanese government from prosecuting the group for a recent smuggling operation, which Hezbollah likely fears could establish a precedent for legal action against its illicit activities. The Lebanese government is investigating the group after Lebanese Internal Security Forces (ISF) personnel interdicted a Hezbollah truck carrying nearly 800 kilograms of high explosives from the Bekaa Valley to Beirut's southern suburbs on October 5.[1] The government currently seeks to refer the criminal case to the Judicial Council, which is Lebanon's highest criminal court, and prosecute the Hezbollah members involved in smuggling explosives.[2] Referring the case to the Judicial Council would break Hezbollah’s de facto legal immunity and expose the organization to potential legal consequences.[3] Hezbollah accordingly likely views the possible legal actions that the government is exploring as steps that could progress or even hasten existing government efforts to disarm the group entirely under the June 2026 Agreement, particularly by establishing a precedent for legal action against Hezbollah’s illicit activities.[4] Lebanese government success in holding Hezbollah accountable for smuggling weapons threatens Hezbollah’s ability to resupply itself with impunity and maintain its arms.[5] The successful prosecution of the case could also strengthen the government’s confidence and preparedness to further challenge Hezbollah’s military and criminal activities, potentially to the point of military disarmament.
Hezbollah is attempting to exploit the Lebanese government’s sensitivity toward civil conflict by threatening the government with unrest and portraying the government as sectarian and beholden to Israeli interests. Hezbollah’s parliamentary bloc warned on October 23 that Lebanon is liable to “fragmentation” and suggested that the government is pushing Lebanon toward civil conflict in pursuing legal action against Hezbollah.[6] Hezbollah officials further derided the government’s efforts as “settling internal sectarian scores.”[7] The Hezbollah officials also called any attempt to arrest Hezbollah officials an “act of Israeli aggression,” which is likely also a veiled threat to confront government police or military action.[8] Hezbollah has historically used these kinds of threats and rhetoric to render the Lebanese government and public unwilling to threaten the group’s military organization.[9] The government is particularly sensitive to such threats given Lebanon’s history of civil war and sectarian violence.[10]
Hezbollah also leveraged its political alliance with Parliament Speaker and Hezbollah ally Nabih Berri, who leads the Shia Amal Movement, to delay President Joseph Aoun and Prime Minister Nawaf Salam’s efforts to prosecute Hezbollah on October 22.[11] Lebanese media reported on October 23 that Berri urged Aoun and Salam to postpone a necessary cabinet vote that would refer the Hezbollah explosives case to the Judicial Council, which answers to the pro-government Justice Minister and issues binding rulings without appeal.[12] Berri reportedly promised Aoun and Salam that, in return for postponing the cabinet vote, Hezbollah would neither foment civil conflict nor trigger a government crisis by withdrawing Hezbollah and Amal from the cabinet.[13] The Lebanese ISF and Lebanese Armed Forces (LAF) will continue to investigate the incident during this postponement under the Lebanese Military Court’s jurisdiction.[14] The Military Court is led by Brigadier General Wassim Fayad, who is reportedly close to Hezbollah and has previously obstructed prosecutions under pressure from the group.[15]
Hezbollah’s lines of effort illustrate the challenges facing the Lebanese government as it attempts to confront the group, but these efforts do not guarantee that Hezbollah will succeed in breaking the government's will to disarm and prosecute the group in this instance. Hezbollah is relying on lines of effort that have succeeded during previous efforts to hold the group accountable over the last two decades.[16] Hezbollah is far weaker and less popular now than it was during some of these previous efforts, however, because it has exhausted much of its military strength and popular support since October 2023 by initiating destructive wars with Israel.[17] The Lebanese government is also demonstrating resolve to counter Hezbollah’s threats and thwart its illicit activities, including by authorizing ISF raids and LAF deployments in Beirut’s southern suburbs, which are a historical Hezbollah stronghold.[18] ISW-CTP has outlined Hezbollah’s lines of effort to avoid disarmament and vulnerabilities that the United States and Lebanon can exploit in an October 22 special report (read the full report here: How Hezbollah is Preventing its Disarmament, Again .)[19]
Saudi Arabia is expanding the scope of its air campaign in Yemen by interdicting Houthi smuggling cargo that could sustain Houthi military operations against the internationally recognized Yemeni government and Saudi Arabia.[20] Saudi coalition aircraft reportedly struck a likely Houthi smuggling vessel near Houthi-controlled Zuqar Island in the Red Sea on October 22.[21] Saudi coalition aircraft have increasingly targeted Houthi smuggling vessels in the Red Sea since October 16.[22] The Saudi Joint Forces Command also issued a statement warning Yemeni vessels against supporting Houthi smuggling efforts on October 19.[23] The Houthis have established an extensive international smuggling network, with support from the Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps (IRGC), to acquire small arms, drone and missile components, and other military equipment in recent years.[24] Saudi interdiction efforts likely aim to prevent arms and equipment that the Houthis could use to resupply frontline units from reaching the group by interdicting vessels carrying illicit cargo. The Houthis could also theoretically use drone and missile equipment acquired through smuggling to supply Houthi missile and drone units that are conducting daily attacks on Saudi critical infrastructure (see below).
The increase in Saudi airstrikes targeting Houthi smuggling assets comes amid challenges to the Yemeni government’s ability to conduct maritime counter-smuggling operations. The Yemeni-government-aligned, Saudi-backed National Resistance Forces historically relied upon its positions on the Red Sea coast, and at Mokha port in particular, as a base for the Yemeni government's maritime counter-smuggling operations in the Red Sea. The Houthis seized this coastline, including Mokha, on September 10.[25] The loss of Yemeni-controlled territory and port infrastructure along the Red Sea coast has almost certainly limited the Yemeni government's ability to conduct anti-smuggling operations against the Houthis.[26] It is also unclear if government forces have had the bandwidth to conduct such counter-smuggling operations while forces are currently engaged in a full mobilization against the Houthis.

Russian Offensive Campaign Assessment, October 23, 2026
Ukrainian officials are widely reporting continued in-motion speed restrictions on SpaceX’s low-earth orbit satellite communication technology (Starlink and Starshield) on October 23, but the situation remains unclear. Ukrainian sources, including the Ukrainian General Staff and sources within the Ukrainian Ministry of Defense (MoD), reported on October 23 that Ukrainian forces have observed “cases of connection loss” in Starlink/Starshield terminal operations at speeds of 161 kilometers per hour (around 100 miles per hour).[1] Sources within the Ukrainian MoD stated that there has been “no confirmation” about the removal of in-motion speed restrictions.[2] Ukrainian defense minister Serhii Sternenko stated that the situation with Starlink/Starshield speed restrictions “remains ambiguous” since some Ukrainian units report the resumption of operations at speeds exceeding the previously established limits, but the resumption does not apply to all missions, and he noted that some disruptions continue.[3] The situation regarding in-motion Starlink/Starshield speed restrictions remains unclear, but Ukrainian sources are emphasizing that Ukrainian forces continue experiencing problems with accessing Starlink/Starshield terminals as of October 23.
Russia and Ukraine have reportedly agreed to a limited ceasefire in Russian-occupied Oleshky, Kherson Oblast. The White House reported on October 23 that US First Lady Melania Trump secured a localized ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine in Russia-occupied Oleshky, Kherson Oblast beginning on October 29 to facilitate humanitarian aid deliveries and a civilian evacuation from the city amid a dire humanitarian crisis.[4] Ukraine’s Human Rights Commissioner Dmytro Lubinets confirmed on October 23 the White House’s press release and noted that Ukraine and Russia agreed to conduct a humanitarian mission in occupied Oleshky, including securing a limited ceasefire, delivering humanitarian cargo to the city, and ensuring the free movement of civilians from the settlement.[5] Russian Chief of the General Staff Army General Valery Gerasimov confirmed on October 23 that Russia agreed to the limited ceasefire in the Kherson direction beginning at 0400 local Moscow time on October 27 — a notably different start date from the one given in the White House press release.[6]
Russian occupation authorities are fully responsible for the humanitarian crisis in Oleshky and have repeatedly and deliberately blocked evacuation and humanitarian routes into the settlement since the beginning of Russia's occupation.[7] Russian occupation authorities stopped the delivery of food and other critical supplies into Oleshky in late December 2025 and have failed to deliver any food or medicine to Oleshky since May 2026. The Kremlin rejected three local humanitarian ceasefire proposals from Ukrainian Ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets in May, July, and September 2026, after which Ukrainian forces conducted an operation to provide food to Oleshky residents via drone drops in September 2026.[8] Russian authorities have long refused to address the humanitarian crisis in Oleshky, and Gerasimov’s October 23 statement is the first instance of a Russian attempt to facilitate humanitarian aid deliveries.
Russian President Vladimir Putin articulated his long-standing commitment to wider territorial aims in Ukraine far beyond Donetsk Oblast by invoking mythologized and false Russian “historical claims”. Putin claimed on October 22 that occupied Ukraine is “the territory of historical Russia” while rejecting Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky’s October 21 proposal to freeze the current frontline as part of a ceasefire agreement.[9] Putin also claimed that the “latest genetic research” identifies Russians and Ukrainians as “one people.” Putin’s October 22 reference to “historical Russia” likely refers to "Novorossiya" — an amorphous invented region in southern and eastern Ukraine that the Kremlin has claimed is “integral” to Russia.[10] Novorossiya notably includes not only Crimea and the four oblasts that Russia has illegally annexed (Luhansk, Donetsk, Zaporizhia, and Kherson oblasts), but also Kharkiv, Dnipropetrovsk, Mykolaiv, and Odesa oblasts.[11] Putin’s invocation of Russia’s historical claims demands that Ukraine surrender the Ukrainian-controlled 20.5 percent of Donetsk Oblast, 24.5 percent of Zaporizhia Oblast, including Zaporizhzhia City with a pre-war population of over 700,000 people, and 30 percent of Kherson Oblast, including Kherson City with a pre-war population of nearly 300,000 people. Putin’s October 23 statement explicitly demonstrates that Russian demands extend far beyond Ukraine’s Fortress Belt and Donetsk Oblast and may include the entire territory of Ukraine . Putin also emphasized his commitment to achieving all Russian war goals, which include the Russian demand for Ukraine’s full capitulation.[12] Putin’s statement demonstrates that the Kremlin is willing to participate in “negotiations” with Ukraine solely for the purpose of accepting Kyiv's surrender — not for the purpose of coming to some compromise peace agreement.
Putin also agreed to discuss the “European security architecture as a whole” after ensuring Ukraine’s and Russia’s future security.[13] Putin’s comment on “revising” the European security architecture refers to another Russian long-standing demand first articulated in the December 2021 ultimatum preceding the full-scale invasion that includes halting the deployment of military forces and weapons systems to NATO states that joined the alliance after 1997 and granting Russia a veto over certain NATO exercises.[14] Putin’s December 2021 demands are incompatible with US President Donald Trump’s commitment to maintaining a US military presence in Poland and the current US troop deployments in the Baltic States.
The Kremlin appears to be increasingly adopting a more cautious monetary policy amid continued inflationary pressures, which may pose risks to wage growth and Russia’s defense industrial base (DIB). The Russian Central Bank announced on October 23 that it would keep the key interest rate at 14 percent for the second consecutive month, after lowering the rate 10 times over the past 18 months, citing elevated price pressure and high inflation expectations.[15] The Central Bank’s baseline scenario assumes an average key interest rate of 12.5 to 13.5 percent in 2027 — only 0.5 to 1.5 percentage points below the current rate. The Central Bank noted that underlying price growth is “not slowing” and identified pro-inflationary risks (or risk factors that increase inflation over the medium term), including price growth between five and six percent and estimated annual inflation of 6.8 percent. The Central Bank estimated that annual inflation will range between 7.1 and 7.6 percent by the end of 2026, a slight increase from the Central Bank’s September 11 announcement, and noted that volatile items like motor fuel (an oblique reference to the effects of Ukrainian strikes on Russian energy infrastructure) will continue impacting price dynamics. Russian Central Bank Chairwoman Elvira Nabiullina stated on October 23 that supply and demand continue facing inflationary risks, elevated inflation expectations, labor market tensions, and a larger budget deficit.[16] Nabiullina stated that Russia has experienced a decrease in consumer activity and that Russia’s GDP growth estimates for 2026 are between 0.5 and one percent. Nabiullina stated that the Central Bank is aiming to return the inflation rate to four percent in 2027.
The Russian Central Bank’s continued reduction of the key interest rate over an 18-month period prior to September 2026 likely contributed to domestic inflationary pressures.[17] Nabiullina has consistently expressed the need for monetary restraint — often trying to resist the Kremlin’s demands to expand capital availability for the Russian DIB and devalue the ruble to increase export revenues, despite ongoing fiscal inflationary pressures.[18] The Central Bank’s apparent success in avoiding further interest rate reductions at this time indicates that Putin has likely become more tolerant of contractionary monetary policy. Putin has recently prioritized reducing inflation to secure support amongst the Russian population by attempting to reduce the economic effects of Russian war spending, a prioritization that may have led him to accept Nabiullina’s fiscal policy for now.[19] Keeping the key rate at the current level will likely suppress wage growth and limit the amount of capital available to the Russian DIB, which may prevent Putin from achieving his goals of raising Russians’ effective purchasing power and maintaining the DIB’s production capacity. It remains difficult to assess, however, what effect fiscal policy will have on wage inflation and Russians’ quality of life, since Russia’s persistent labor shortages drive wage inflation for employed individuals while households face pressures that nominal wage figures do not capture.[20]
A likely Russian drone struck a cargo ship off the coast of Romania and drone debris fell in Moldova on October 23. Romanian President Nicusor Dan stated on October 23 that an unspecified drone struck a Comoros-flagged Khoryv cargo ship “near Romanian territorial waters” and added that the strike is a consequence of “Russia’s aggression against Ukraine.”[21] The Romanian Naval Authority reported that the Khoryv vessel sent a distress signal about 13 nautical miles (around 24 kilometers and just outside of the Romanian territorial waters) off the coast of Constanta, Romania.[22] European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen called the October 23 drone strike yet another “Russian escalation” in the Black Sea.[23] Territorial waters extend up to 12 nautical miles (about 22 kilometers) from the country's shoreline, and states have sovereignty and jurisdiction over their territorial waters.[24] The October 23 drone strike comes against the backdrop of recent escalating Russian strikes against vessels in the Black Sea, including likely Russian drone strikes against two commercial tankers within Romanian territorial waters on October 14 and 15 and strikes against civilian vessels in the Bulgarian Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) overnight on October 12 to 13.[25] The Moldovan police separately reported on October 23 that drone debris fell up to 100 meters from a residential building near Chistelnica, Telenesti Raion, Moldova.[26] These continuous, likely Russian violations of Moldovan and Romanian sovereign territory demonstrate Russia’s increasing willingness to pursue strike tactics that may directly impact the territory of NATO or NATO-aligned states.[27]

Beyond the processors found in computers and phones, a different class of semiconductor controls how electricity moves through everything from electric grids to high-performance computing equipment.
Used to regulate electrical current and enable power converters to raise or lower voltage, power semiconductors help manage the large electrical loads required by modern infrastructure.
Because these processes inevitably result in some energy being lost as heat, improvements in semiconductor efficiency can reduce electricity consumption and heat generation, particularly in power-intensive applications where even small reductions in energy losses can become significant.
To advance those and other semiconductor technologies domestically, the CHIPS Research and Development Office, established under the 2022 CHIPS and Science Act, supports research and manufacturing capabilities aimed at reducing dependence on overseas suppliers.
"The Trump Administration is accelerating American semiconductor innovation to enhance our domestic energy and power resources," said Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick. "These investments expand domestic research, secure critical supply chains, and create high-paying American jobs."
Among the technologies receiving attention through the latest agreements are advanced power semiconductors intended for nuclear fusion and data centers, along with specialized materials used to manufacture semiconductor wafers.
Still experimental, nuclear fusion seeks to harness the same basic process that powers the sun as a source of electricity.
Working toward commercial fusion generation, developers rely on advanced electrical systems to create, heat, and control plasma, with some designs requiring rapid magnetic pulses and others using powerful magnets to contain the superheated fuel.
The challenge of managing large electrical loads also extends to AI data centers, where growing power requirements place additional demands on the systems responsible for converting and distributing electricity to computing equipment.
Alongside the power semiconductor research aimed at fusion and advanced computing, the proposed awards also extend to materials critical to chip manufacturing, including ultra-high-purity quartz used to produce heat-resistant crucibles for growing the single-crystal silicon needed to manufacture semiconductor wafers.
Under the four letters of intent, the proposed recipients and their projects are:

With the bull market now nearly four years in, investors are bracing for some kind of reset.
The worst-case scenario would likely be a repeat of the dot-com bubble, in which the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite lost roughly 80% of its value between 2000 and 2002.
Many investors have cited similarities between what&#x27;s going on now with artificial intelligence and the rise of the internet in the late 1990s.
In recent years, the market has gone through several major sell-offs, including the tariff storm in 2025, the market&#x27;s reaction to rising interest rates in 2022, and the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.
There&#x27;s no sugarcoating it: Those were not fun experiences and definitely felt stressful at the time. But if I could tell investors one thing about market crashes, it would be this.
Any long-term investors who think the past three or four years are the norm, where the market gains at least 15%, and often over 20%, would be sorely mistaken.
If you are a long-term investor with a five- to 10-year time horizon, expect a pullback. It may not necessarily be an event like the dot-com bubble. It could be a correction, in which the market falls by at least 10% from a recent peak, or a bear market, in which the market declines by at least 20%.
According to a Charles Schwab study published in 2025, there were 27 market corrections between November 1974 and February 2025, meaning there was a market correction roughly every other year during this period.
On six of those occasions -- 1980, 1987, 2000, 2007, 2020, and 2022 -- the broader benchmark S&P 500 ( ^GSPC -0.52% ) tipped into a bear market.
While it would certainly be nice for the market to grow like a weed every year, corrections and resets are actually what make a healthy market. Often, stocks or sectors soar unjustifiably due to investor enthusiasm and greed, ultimately becoming overvalued.
Those stocks need to reset to maintain market fundamentals and root out bad actors (not that poor performance necessarily means bad actors are involved).
Dips are also good for long-term investors because they allow them to buy stocks of very strong companies at a much better entry point, helping them accumulate more wealth over time. It&#x27;s tougher to make money in a market where most companies always trade at premium valuations .
As I mentioned, a correction or bear market is never fun, especially if your portfolio is struggling. But the important thing to understand is that these crashes don&#x27;t often last long.
For instance, according to Schwab, past bear markets tend to be much shorter than past bull markets. Dating back to 1961, past bear markets have averaged a 36% loss and lasted 409 days, or about 13 months.
Meanwhile, past bull markets have, on average, lasted 1,894 days, or slightly over five years, giving investors an average return of 182.5%.
What does this tell us? Those who grind through a bear market often eventually enter a bull market that more than makes up for losses incurred in the bear market.
If you don&#x27;t believe me, just look back at the period since the 2020 pandemic. There have been two bear markets, and the S&P 500 has still generated fantastic gains.
As things stand, four years into a bull market, are we due for some kind of pullback? History suggests the answer is yes. But timing the market is next to impossible, so never try to.
The best thing you can do as an investor is to stay invested in the broader market and battle-test all your individual holdings. Make sure, to the best of your ability, that your investment thesis in a stock looks at what might happen to the company under tougher market or economic conditions.
It may not be a bad idea to have some cash ready to buy stocks if the market crashes.

Federal Reserve officials are widely expected to leave the central bank’s key interest rate unchanged when they meet on Wednesday, signaling patience in their fight against inflation.
Financial markets were pricing in a one-in-ten chance that policymakers on the Federal Open Market Committee will vote to raise the fed funds rate by a quarter-point when they meet Wednesday, according to the CME Group’s FedWatch tool, which forecasts rate movements based on fed funds futures trading data.
In recent weeks, Fed officials have publicly discussed whether to follow up on September’s quarter-point rate hike to counter higher-than-expected inflation. Although some Fed officials have voiced concerns, others have advocated a wait-and-see approach.
A higher fed funds rate means higher borrowing costs on all kinds of loans, which could discourage spending and subdue inflation. But it could also drag on the job market and the economy overall.
The Fed is balancing its dual mandate from Congress to keep inflation low and employment high. With inflation running above the Fed’s inflation goal for a fifth year and unemployment hovering near historic lows, most experts expect the Fed to raise interest rates again in December even if they forego an October hike.
“All signs are that the Federal Reserve will need to continue lifting interest rates in the near term, yet comments from a few key officials suggest they’re unlikely to do so at the October policy meeting,” Michael Pearce, chief U.S. economist at Oxford Economics, wrote in a commentary.
If the Fed pauses its rate hike campaign in October, it would allow officials more time to assess how much the Iran war’s fuel price hikes are pushing up inflation before taking action again.
Recent comments from two key FOMC members—Fed Governor Michelle Bowman and John C. Williams , president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York—suggested they didn’t favor rate hikes at back-to-back meetings.
The recent Fed remarks indicated that most officials will support hiking rates by a quarter point at every other meeting, which would mean the next two rate hikes will take place in December and March, David Mericle, chief U.S. economist at Goldman Sachs, wrote in a commentary.

Still, Warsh’s words, however cryptic, could offer some clues.
“Chair Warsh is unlikely to offer any direct forward guidance in his press conference, but we expect he will implicitly endorse a December rate hike,” Ruchir Sharma, an economist at Nomura, wrote in a commentary. “His characterization of recent inflation data and market financial conditions could provide some clues around the threshold for additional tightening in 2027.”
