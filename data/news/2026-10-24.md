The S&P 500 ( ^GSPC -0.52% ) and Nasdaq Composite ( ^IXIC -0.21% ) have recently been experiencing a bout of volatility after repeatedly notching record highs throughout 2026. With stocks coming under pressure, some investors are wondering whether a larger market correction is approaching.
Part of the concern stems from elevated bond yields and stubborn inflation. Meanwhile, artificial intelligence (AI) hyperscalers like Amazon , Microsoft , Alphabet , and Meta Platforms are among the companies supporting the broader economy as they accelerate spending on data centers .
Whether this volatility is just a temporary pullback or the beginning of something worse is impossible to know. But legendary investor Warren Buffett spent decades explaining how to navigate markets dominated by exciting new technologies, sky-high valuations, and uncertainty.
Former Berkshire Hathaway CEO Warren Buffett. Image source: Getty Images.
Revolutionary technology does not equal a good investment
Buffett built Berkshire Hathaway  into one of history&#x27;s most successful investment firms simply because he had a relentless focus on businesses he understands. He generally avoided technology stocks, instead favoring industries such as insurance, banking, consumer products, utilities, and energy. These businesses usually have relatively predictable economics, which allowed Buffett to better assess their long-term earnings potential.
Buffett understood that an industry can revolutionize society without producing attractive returns overall. Consider the automobile and aviation industries as examples. Cars and airplanes have unquestionably transformed the global economy, but countless early manufacturers and airlines have gone bankrupt as competition intensified.
The theme here is that identifying which industries are game changers is much easier than choosing the specific companies that will capture profits in the long run. This distinction matters in today&#x27;s AI environment.
While AI is shaping up to be one of the most transformative technologies in history, that doesn&#x27;t mean every semiconductor company, software developer, or infrastructure provider is going to generate market-beating returns.
Is the AI revolution destined to end up like the dot-com bubble?
During the late 1990s, investors were pouring money into virtually anything that resembled the internet. Speculative technology stocks became multibaggers , even when the underlying companies had little revenue, no profits, or incomplete business models.
Reality inevitably caught up with expectations. After peaking in March 2000, the Nasdaq plummeted by 78% by October 2002. Suddenly, once-promising internet businesses left investors holding the bag.
Today, some investors are worried the AI boom could become a modern retelling of the dot-com bubble. Sure, AI leaders like Nvidia and the cloud hyperscalers already generate substantial profits and cash flow. The reason why is that their AI infrastructure is supported by numerous established businesses rather than simply promises around future growth. Still, the industry faces uncertainty because not every model developer is going to succeed in the long run.
This brings us to Buffett&#x27;s most important investing principle. The single best thing an investor can do for their portfolio right now is load up on companies with durable competitive advantages rather than chasing the stocks attracting the most attention. Buffett describes these advantages as economic moats that come in the form of powerful brands, high switching costs, network effects , and economies of scale.
There&#x27;s a more subtle lesson investors can learn from the dot-com crash: Time in the market is more valuable than trying to time the market. Suppose you invested in the S&P 500 near its peak during the dot-com era. If you managed to stay invested, you would have witnessed the index appreciate roughly 450%.
A hypothetical $10,000 investment would have grown to nearly $55,000, even though it was made right before a major market collapse. What&#x27;s even more inspiring is that over the last 25 years, the S&P 500 has endured three recessions -- pictured above as the grey-shaded columns -- and managed to come out higher after each one. This underscores the resiliency of the S&P 500 as one of the greatest wealth-creation vehicles.
This performance is why Buffett&#x27;s emphasis on diversification is both valuable and relevant in today&#x27;s market. Rather than panic-selling whenever stocks become volatile or valuation is frothy, investors should rebalance their portfolios if their positions expose them to unnecessary risk.
Ultimately, no one knows whether the recent sell-off will permeate into a full-blown crash. But Buffett&#x27;s philosophy offers a practical response: The best opportunity is to own quality businesses, avoid paying unreasonable prices, and remain patient enough to let compounding do the heavy lifting.

The Saudi government owns about 81.5% of Aramco. The kingdom’s sovereign wealth fund, the Public Investment Fund, holds 16%, and roughly 2.5% trades publicly . “The irony is that the balance of regional power has tilted from Saudi Arabia towards Iran as a result of the attacks on Iran,” Metcalf says. “This would have been unthinkable just a year ago.”

Trump Invokes Cold War-Era Law to Boost Oil Refining as Gas Prices Soar
President Donald Trump has invoked a Cold War-era defense law to direct up to $150 million toward U.S. oil refineries as gasoline prices climb above $4 a gallon and diesel exceeds $6. The October 22 action allows the federal government to support refinery expansion and petroleum infrastructure projects under powers intended to protect national defense.
The administration&apos;s plan draws on a broader $1 billion congressional allocation for energy projects considered critical to national security. The Department of Energy expects the investments to add millions of gallons of gasoline, diesel and jet fuel to daily domestic production, although funding recipients and project timelines have yet to be announced.
Trump signed a presidential memorandum directing Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to use Section 303 of the Defense Production Act of 1950 to strengthen domestic petroleum production, refining, pipelines and fuel storage facilities. The order identifies those operations as essential to national defense and authorizes federal purchases, financial commitments and agreements to expand industrial capacity.
The directive comes as global energy disruptions linked to the conflict involving Iran and attacks on Russian energy infrastructure continue to pressure fuel markets. As of October 23, the national average price of gasoline stood at approximately $4.29 per gallon, while diesel averaged $6.32, increasing transportation and operating costs for consumers and businesses.
Trump determined that financing constraints, construction timelines, permitting delays and supply chain limitations have prevented private industry from expanding petroleum infrastructure quickly enough to meet national defense needs. Trump cited the risk of a petroleum shortfall that could severely impair national defense capabilities.
The president also waived six statutory requirements governing federal support for industrial expansion, citing the national energy emergency he declared in January 2025. Section 303 of the Defense Production Act allows the government to support industries essential to national defense through purchases, financial commitments and investments in production capacity. Enacted during the Korean War, the statute permits specified requirements to be waived during a declared national emergency or when an industrial shortage threatens defense capabilities.
Trump previously invoked the same provision on April 20, designating domestic petroleum production, refining and distribution as essential to national defense. That determination assigned implementation to the energy secretary, while the October 22 memorandum directs Hegseth to carry out the authority.
The Energy Department&apos;s latest funding initiative would support efforts to restore and expand American refineries. Energy Secretary Chris Wright said the funding would help restart and expand U.S. refining operations.
Federal funding could accelerate expansions at existing refineries, although industry specialists expect substantial increases in fuel production to take months. Equipment purchases, construction and regulatory approvals remain among the obstacles facing operators.

Beyond the processors found in computers and phones, a different class of semiconductor controls how electricity moves through everything from electric grids to high-performance computing equipment.
Used to regulate electrical current and enable power converters to raise or lower voltage, power semiconductors help manage the large electrical loads required by modern infrastructure.
Because these processes inevitably result in some energy being lost as heat, improvements in semiconductor efficiency can reduce electricity consumption and heat generation, particularly in power-intensive applications where even small reductions in energy losses can become significant.
To advance those and other semiconductor technologies domestically, the CHIPS Research and Development Office, established under the 2022 CHIPS and Science Act, supports research and manufacturing capabilities aimed at reducing dependence on overseas suppliers.
"The Trump Administration is accelerating American semiconductor innovation to enhance our domestic energy and power resources," said Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick. "These investments expand domestic research, secure critical supply chains, and create high-paying American jobs."
Among the technologies receiving attention through the latest agreements are advanced power semiconductors intended for nuclear fusion and data centers, along with specialized materials used to manufacture semiconductor wafers.
Still experimental, nuclear fusion seeks to harness the same basic process that powers the sun as a source of electricity.
Working toward commercial fusion generation, developers rely on advanced electrical systems to create, heat, and control plasma, with some designs requiring rapid magnetic pulses and others using powerful magnets to contain the superheated fuel.
The challenge of managing large electrical loads also extends to AI data centers, where growing power requirements place additional demands on the systems responsible for converting and distributing electricity to computing equipment.
Alongside the power semiconductor research aimed at fusion and advanced computing, the proposed awards also extend to materials critical to chip manufacturing, including ultra-high-purity quartz used to produce heat-resistant crucibles for growing the single-crystal silicon needed to manufacture semiconductor wafers.
