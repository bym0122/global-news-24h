Investors have had plenty to celebrate over the past four years. The S&P 500 ( ^GSPC -0.30% ) has gained nearly 115% since the fall of 2022, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average ( ^DJI -0.25% ) has gained nearly 80%, and the Nasdaq Composite ( ^IXIC -1.38% ) has jumped more than 150%.
And while it&#x27;s fallen somewhat in the past two weeks, it was just earlier this month that the tech-heavy Nasdaq-100 notched another all-time high.
But as welcome as those gains are, they can also make investors uneasy. It&#x27;s natural. Many investors are wondering if a crash is incoming. So, now is as good a time as ever to consider sage advice from Warren Buffett, one of the most successful investors of all time.
First, while it might feel otherwise, the stock market recording all-time highs isn&#x27;t, by itself, a reason to expect trouble. It&#x27;s all too common for success to make people uneasy.
But consider this: The investment firm Dimensional Fund Advisors looked at S&P 500 data from 1926 through 2022 and found that a year after setting a record, the index was higher 81% of the time and gained an average of nearly 14%.
Of course, crashes still happen. They are a fact of life in the stock market. So, what can we learn from the Oracle of Omaha when the next one happens?
One of Buffett&#x27;s most famous quotes dealt with watching for herd behavior in the market and running in the opposite direction. Since we&#x27;re talking about benefiting from crashing prices, we&#x27;ll stick to the second half of the quote: "Be greedy when others are fearful."
These six words can help you avoid the worst thing you can do during a crash: panic sell. Instead, his advice can help you see a crash as an opportunity: a chance to buy shares of great businesses at steep discounts. For those who can keep their heads, a stock market crash can be lucrative.
A perfect example of this advice in action: the great financial crisis. While most investors were running scared, Buffett poured billions into several companies when confidence was in short supply. This wasn&#x27;t charity: He helped the companies raise cash when they needed it most while ensuring Berkshire Hathaway shareholders made money -- a lot of it.
Here&#x27;s a rundown of his work in 2008 and the years that followed:
Goldman Sachs: Berkshire invested $5 billion in September 2008, receiving preferred shares paying a 10% dividend and warrants -- the right to buy shares at a set price. The deal generated $3.7 billion in profit.
Bank of America: Berkshire invested $5 billion in 2011 as the bank continued to struggle after the crisis. Again, Buffett secured preferred shares and warrants. When he exercised those warrants in 2017, Berkshire had an on-paper gain of $12 billion.
General Electric: Buffett invested $3 billion in the struggling industrial giant in 2008, securing preferred shares and warrants. The investment produced $1.5 billion in profit over nine years.
Of course, you have to be careful. A cheap stock isn&#x27;t automatically a bargain. There are plenty of stocks that fall in a crash and never recover because the underlying business was bad .

WASHINGTON, DC - OCTOBER 28: Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh delivers remarks during a news conference following the Federal Open Market Committee meetings at Federal Reserve Headquarters on October 28, 2026 in Washington, DC. The Federal Reserve will leave interest rates unchanged even as inflation remains elevated. (Photo by Alex Wong/Getty Images)
WASHINGTON (TNND) — The Federal Reserve is planning to cut down the number of its meetings devoted to moving its benchmark interest rate over the next two years in one of the most significant shifts yet under its new chairman.
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh announced the change after Wednesday’s meeting, where the central bank opted to keep rates steady despite mounting pressures on inflation. Beginning next fall, the Fed will dedicate one of its eight policy meetings to weigh broad-based questions about the economy instead of voting on interest rates. In 2028, there will be two meetings without a rate decision.
Warsh argued the longer gap between meetings would give officials more time to gather data to see how the economy is responding to previous decisions and allow them to make more informed judgements on how to move forward.
“Getting policy right involves assessing near-term developments at timely, regular and information-rich intervals,” Warsh said. “But it’s equally important we reserve time and space as an intellectually diverse group for strategic deliberations, for thinking anew about big questions, looking out over longer time horizons.”
Cutting down the number of rate-setting meetings is a significant shift for the Fed, which has run on its eight-meeting schedule since 1981. While Fed officials will still meet at the same frequency, they will have fewer planned opportunities to shift monetary policy.
“It serves a useful purpose to meet eight times a year. It&#x27;s arbitrary whether it&#x27;s six or eight, but it gives you an opportunity to actually have to make decisions, and that is do more than talk about something where you really have to figure out where things are and whether you want to stay the same,” said Thomas Hoenig, former president and CEO of the Kansas City Fed and distinguished senior fellow at the Mercatus Center. “By eliminating the meeting, you may decrease the likelihood that you have a discussion about where the economy is and whether you should actually take an action. I think that&#x27;s a loss, frankly.”
Depending on which meetings are changed on the calendar, the Fed could go nearly three months without a regularly scheduled opportunity to adjust interest rates, compared to the current roughly six-week interval.
It’s not yet clear what, if anything, will come out of the meetings that do not include a rate decision. Warsh also did not indicate which meeting will be the first to go.
“I do not anticipate making any policy decision, putting out a policy statement. But what might we come out with? It&#x27;s not obvious yet,” Warsh said.
The Fed’s rate-setting meetings are always closely watched by investors and economists but have been under particular scrutiny in recent years after inflation spiked to a high of 9.1% in 2022. While the pace of price increases has slowed significantly since then, inflation still remains well above the Fed’s 2% target.
It is not the first time the Fed has considered scaling back the number of rate-setting meetings , but it has never made the switch over concerns it could become less responsive to sudden changes in the economy. The Fed is required to meet at least four times a year, which Warsh said during his confirmation hearings was not enough.
Some economists have argued fewer rate decisions would give Fed officials more time to see meaningful changes to the economy and allow them to make more informed decisions. The Fed can also hold emergency meetings during times of sudden turmoil, which it has done in the past, most recently in 2020 during the onset of the coronavirus pandemic.
But reducing the number of scheduled rate decisions will also change the balance of influence within the central bank and make choices to call additional meetings more consequential. While the chair does not make interest rate decisions alone, the decisions on whether to meet or adjust rates could take on more importance.
“It now places more discretion in the hands of the chairman as to whether they hold an extra meeting for a vote, and otherwise there will be no meeting, so there&#x27;ll be two less opportunities for presidents and for governors to vote on conditions,” Hoenig said. “In that sense, it does put more authority in the hands of the chairman and the FOMC broadly.”
It is the latest push by Warsh to get financial markets, which closely monitor Fed meetings and speeches from its officials, to become less reliant on communication from the central bank and focus more on incoming economic data. Warsh has significantly scaled back the level of communication coming from the central bank, dialing down the length of post-meeting policy statements, avoiding giving forward guidance and sitting out of making quarterly projections for rates and other economic conditions.
Warsh’s perspective on forward guidance is a departure from his recent predecessors, who generally embraced greater transparency in an attempt to reduce market volatility and help investors better understand the Fed’s objectives.
He has also tried to remake the Fed in other ways, creating task forces to examine whether the Fed should make more changes. The task forces are looking into how the central bank communicates, manages its massive balance sheet, how it gathers and interprets economic data, the impact artificial intelligence is having on the economy and how it analyzes inflation.

Drones have transformed the way wars are fought. Azhnyuk says artificial intelligence is making drones faster and more capable.
"When the other side has orders of magnitudes higher capabilities, it gives you an asymmetric advantage," he says. "It can make you 10,000 times more effective."
Azhnyuk runs the Ukraine-based defense firms The Fourth Law and Odd Systems. They are among the top companies producing AI-powered drones in Ukraine. Dozens of Ukrainian military units already use their autonomous drone, called the Lupynis-10-TFL-1.
Russia is also deploying AI-enabled drones against Ukraine. AI warfare is part of a high-tech arms race that has many worried, including Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He has warned that AI could lead decisions on the battlefield as soon as next year.
Azhnyuk told NPR that he acknowledges the concern over destructive weapons but says it's not specific to AI.
"It has been with humanity since the invention of fire or steel," he says. "Any kind of tool can be used for good or for bad. And in the end, it's the human that decides how to use that tool."
Azhnyuk is 37, a gregarious computer scientist who hails from a family of scholars and scientists. NPR meets him in a conference room in Kyiv; for security reasons he asked us not to reveal much else about the location.
