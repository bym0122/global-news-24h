Tankers defy Iran attacks to keep crude oil flowing through Hormuz
More commercial ships, mostly oil tankers, have come under attack in October than at any other point in the Iran war.
But tankers continue to ship substantial volumes of crude oil through the Strait of Hormuz, in stark contrast to March when exports cratered due to Iranian attacks.
Shippers have adapted to wartime in the Persian Gulf but at a very high cost.
Rapidan Energy&#x27;s McNally: Ceasefire optimism is fading and crude is repricing
Within 24 hours of the U.S. and Israel going to war with Iran, Tehran responded with a wave of attacks on commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz to cut off one of the world&#x27;s main sources of crude oil.
Iran&#x27;s campaign was initially successful, with crude exports plunging nearly 90% to 1.8 million barrels a day in March compared with 16 million bpd in February, according to data provided by Kpler.
But crude flows have rebounded over the past eight months as the U.S. military provides protection to tankers transiting Hormuz. Tehran has responded by escalating its attacks in recent weeks .
More commercial ships have come under attack in October than at any other point in the war. Yet in contrast to March when exports cratered, tankers have continued to ferry substantial volumes of crude oil through Hormuz in a sign that the shipping industry has adapted to wartime in the Persian Gulf.
As of Oct. 27, at least 30 vessels, mostly oil tankers, were hit this month leaving at minimum 16 seafarers injured, according to data from the International Maritime Organization , a United Nations agency. Ship attacks doubled in October compared with 15 confirmed incidents in September.
"It&#x27;s a difficult situation. Ships are attacked really on a daily basis," said Jakob Larsen, chief safety and security officer at Denmark-headquartered BIMCO, one of the big international shipping trade associations.
Ship attacks in the Strait of Hormuz surged in October
Crude prices have surged more than 13% this month as security conditions have deteriorated in the Persian Gulf. The international Brent contract traded above $117 a barrel on Friday. That&#x27;s just shy of its wartime closing high of $118.35 during Iran&#x27;s big wave of ship attacks in March.
Yet actual crude exports through Hormuz stood at nearly 11.1 million bpd on average in October, only about 2% lower from September despite the sharp escalation of violence, Kpler&#x27;s data shows.
"On balance in terms of oil coming out, there hasn&#x27;t been a massive impact," said Mary Melton, senior tanker analyst at Braemar, a ship broker headquartered in London.
Crude flows through Hormuz are highly volatile on a daily basis with exports near prewar levels one day while falling well below such levels another day. The final leg of October has seen flows decline to 7.3 million bpd for the week ended Wednesday, more than 40% below the prewar baseline, according to Kpler.
Oil flows are more resilient now because the shipping industry has adjusted its operations to the threats, Larsen said. Freight rates have soared, making it "more attractive for the risk takers in the industry to actually carry out these voyages," he said.
"And when the cost of oil goes up that also means that there&#x27;s more commercial incentives to get the oil out and sell it in the global market," Larsen said.
Shippers have adapted by setting up a shuttle system to reduce the risk of attack. Tankers load crude at Persian Gulf ports, sail through Hormuz with their transponders turned off and transfer the cargo to tankers waiting in the Gulf of Oman that then deliver it to refiners in Asia. This reduces insurance costs for the ships waiting outside the strait, Melton said.
The shuttle system has been effective at getting oil out with flows through Hormuz surging more than 70% since July, according to Kpler. But this system is also inefficient, logistically complicated and expensive, Melton said.
It requires more equipment, ships and experienced crews, the analyst said. About 20% of the world&#x27;s supertanker fleet is tied up helping to shuttle oil out of the Gulf, she said.
Tanker rates have exploded as a consequence. It cost $249,000 per day to hire a supertanker to make the journey from the Gulf of Oman to Asia in early September, Melton said. By the first week of October, the price had surged to more than $900,000 per day. This translates to about $21 in transportation costs per barrel of crude oil compared with $6.50 a barrel in September, the analyst said.
"If you&#x27;re an importer in Asia, you&#x27;re used to paying almost nothing for freight," Melton said. "Then you&#x27;re facing 20% to 30% of the actual delivered cost of crude is going to freight.
"That really starts impacting your margins as a refiner," the analyst said.
More Gulf oil producers are now covering the cost and risk of shipping through Hormuz in a bid to claw back market share in Asia from the U.S. and other Atlantic producers, she said. And they are increasingly using their own tankers to run the gauntlet through the strait, she said.
It is unclear how long the complex and expensive shuttle system can sustain current export levels. The tankers transiting Hormuz are totally dependent on air support from the U.S. military for their security, Larsen said.
"I don&#x27;t think there would be many shipowners willing to take the risk if they didn&#x27;t have that air cover from U.S. fighter jets and helicopters and drones," he said.
It is also uncertain how long the U.S. military will remain engaged, particularly if Democrats regain control of Congress after Tuesday&#x27;s elections and demand that the president end the war.
The U.S. has told several Gulf Arab nations to be ready for a short but intense military campaign following the Nov. 3 midterm elections, people briefed on the conversations told The Wall Street Journal . The U.S. Navy, which is enforcing a blockade against Iran, has warned ships that don&#x27;t need to transit the Persian Gulf, Hormuz and Gulf of Oman to steer clear of the region.
"Due to the current geopolitical situation, vessels which do not need to transit the area are advised to remain well clear to avoid unnecessary delays to their voyage," the U.S. military told vessels Wednesday through a notice issued by the Joint Maritime Information Center .

Congress must act to ensure that families are able to heat their homes before the start of the season
H eating oil may be the clearest warning yet of how the war in Iran is deepening the US’s energy affordability crisis. Families who heat their homes with oil are facing bills that could be 50% higher this winter, adding nearly $900 to the cost of keeping their homes warm. These families were already struggling before the war began.
As the Guardian recently reported , research by my organization found that electricity shutoffs in 10 states had increased 28% over the past two years. Millions of Americans were already falling behind on their utility bills, forced to choose between paying for energy, food, medicine and rent. Now they face another round of increases in gasoline, electricity and home heating costs.
The war didn’t create America’s energy affordability crisis. But it is making a bad situation much worse.
For families living on social security, minimum-wage workers and others with little room in their budgets, higher energy prices are taking an especially heavy toll. Even middle-class families are struggling to absorb another $100 or $150 a month for gasoline, groceries and home heating.
Heating oil is the canary in the coalmine for the broader affordability crisis.
Trump is in a corner and likely to lash out at Iran again. Here’s how to avert that disaster | Simon Tisdall
Heating oil and diesel are essentially the same petroleum product, and both have been hit hard by the disruption to global energy markets. Higher diesel prices increase the cost of moving food, delivering goods and operating farms and construction equipment. Those costs eventually find their way into the prices families pay.
But for the millions of households that rely on heating oil, the impact is much more immediate. Unlike gasoline, which families can sometimes conserve by driving less, heating oil is essential for keeping homes warm. Families cannot simply stop filling their tanks when prices rise.
Trump described higher oil prices resulting from the war as “ a very small price to pay for USA, and world, safety and peace”. But there is nothing small about an additional $878 in heating costs for a family living on social security or working for minimum wage. The president has also said that fuel prices will fall “ like a rock ” when the war ends. I hope he is right.
Even if the war ended tomorrow, energy prices would not necessarily return to their previous levels. Damaged refineries need repairs, shipping routes must reopen, and depleted petroleum inventories need to be replenished. That could take months. Families cannot postpone paying their heating bills while the oil market recovers.
Congress needs to act when it returns after the midterm elections and before winter arrives. We are calling on lawmakers to add $3bn to the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (Liheap), which helps low-income families pay their heating bills.
This would allow states to provide families with supplemental assistance to cover rising heating oil and electricity prices before the start of the winter heating season.
The problem extends beyond the poorest families. Many working families earn too much to qualify for assistance but still struggle to pay their energy bills. Congress should consider temporarily expanding eligibility for Liheap to struggling middle-class families, with additional funding to ensure that assistance to the lowest-income households is not reduced.
A recent survey conducted by Consumer Reports found that more than a quarter of all families reported reduced the heating and cooling in their homes to uncomfortable levels to save money and 19% cutback on groceries or switched to less expensive food options.
Without additional energy assistance, families will fall further behind, and some will face the possibility of losing heat during the coldest months of the year.
But Congress also needs to address the larger problem. For too long, we’ve measured America’s energy security by how much oil and natural gas we produce. Yet producing more energy at home hasn’t protected the US families from price shocks caused by wars overseas.
We need to think about energy security differently. That means expanding energy sources, including wind and solar, whose fuel costs cannot be driven up by a war. There is no strait of Hormuz for solar power.
That is the longer-term challenge. Right now, millions of families are heading into winter facing energy bills they cannot afford.
At some point, the war in Iran will end, but the affordability crisis will not end with it. Washington needs to help families get through this winter and make sure the next war thousands of miles away doesn’t once again determine whether they can afford to heat their homes.
