WASHINGTON (TNND) — The Federal Reserve is planning to cut down the number of its meetings devoted to moving its benchmark interest rate over the next two years in one of the most significant shifts yet under its new chairman.
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh announced the change after Wednesday’s meeting, where the central bank opted to keep rates steady despite mounting pressures on inflation. Beginning next fall, the Fed will dedicate one of its eight policy meetings to weigh broad-based questions about the economy instead of voting on interest rates. In 2028, there will be two meetings without a rate decision.
Warsh argued the longer gap between meetings would give officials more time to gather data to see how the economy is responding to previous decisions and allow them to make more informed judgements on how to move forward.
“Getting policy right involves assessing near-term developments at timely, regular and information-rich intervals,” Warsh said. “But it’s equally important we reserve time and space as an intellectually diverse group for strategic deliberations, for thinking anew about big questions, looking out over longer time horizons.”
Cutting down the number of rate-setting meetings is a significant shift for the Fed, which has run on its eight-meeting schedule since 1981. While Fed officials will still meet at the same frequency, they will have fewer planned opportunities to shift monetary policy.
“It serves a useful purpose to meet eight times a year. It&#x27;s arbitrary whether it&#x27;s six or eight, but it gives you an opportunity to actually have to make decisions, and that is do more than talk about something where you really have to figure out where things are and whether you want to stay the same,” said Thomas Hoenig, former president and CEO of the Kansas City Fed and distinguished senior fellow at the Mercatus Center. “By eliminating the meeting, you may decrease the likelihood that you have a discussion about where the economy is and whether you should actually take an action. I think that&#x27;s a loss, frankly.”
Depending on which meetings are changed on the calendar, the Fed could go nearly three months without a regularly scheduled opportunity to adjust interest rates, compared to the current roughly six-week interval.
It’s not yet clear what, if anything, will come out of the meetings that do not include a rate decision. Warsh also did not indicate which meeting will be the first to go.
“I do not anticipate making any policy decision, putting out a policy statement. But what might we come out with? It&#x27;s not obvious yet,” Warsh said.
The Fed’s rate-setting meetings are always closely watched by investors and economists but have been under particular scrutiny in recent years after inflation spiked to a high of 9.1% in 2022. While the pace of price increases has slowed significantly since then, inflation still remains well above the Fed’s 2% target.
It is not the first time the Fed has considered scaling back the number of rate-setting meetings , but it has never made the switch over concerns it could become less responsive to sudden changes in the economy. The Fed is required to meet at least four times a year, which Warsh said during his confirmation hearings was not enough.
Some economists have argued fewer rate decisions would give Fed officials more time to see meaningful changes to the economy and allow them to make more informed decisions. The Fed can also hold emergency meetings during times of sudden turmoil, which it has done in the past, most recently in 2020 during the onset of the coronavirus pandemic.

A drone operator with the military call sign Professor operates a Lupynis-10-TFL-1 from The Fourth Law at a test site in central Ukraine.
KYIV, Ukraine — Like many tech entrepreneurs in Ukraine, Yaroslav Azhnyuk turned to developing drone warfare after Russia's 2022 invasion.
Drones have transformed the way wars are fought. Azhnyuk says artificial intelligence is making drones faster and more capable.
"When the other side has orders of magnitudes higher capabilities, it gives you an asymmetric advantage," he says. "It can make you 10,000 times more effective."
Azhnyuk runs the Ukraine-based defense firms The Fourth Law and Odd Systems. They are among the top companies producing AI-powered drones in Ukraine. Dozens of Ukrainian military units already use their autonomous drone, called the Lupynis-10-TFL-1.
Russia is also deploying AI-enabled drones against Ukraine. AI warfare is part of a high-tech arms race that has many worried, including Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He has warned that AI could lead decisions on the battlefield as soon as next year.
Azhnyuk told NPR that he acknowledges the concern over destructive weapons but says it's not specific to AI.
"It has been with humanity since the invention of fire or steel," he says. "Any kind of tool can be used for good or for bad. And in the end, it's the human that decides how to use that tool."
Azhnyuk is 37, a gregarious computer scientist who hails from a family of scholars and scientists. NPR meets him in a conference room in Kyiv; for security reasons he asked us not to reveal much else about the location.
He worked for years in Silicon Valley and started several companies, including Petcube , which makes smartphone-powered cameras that use artificial intelligence to watch and even entertain pets while they're alone.
"These cameras allow pet parents to watch pets when they're not at home," he says, "and fling treats to them and shine a laser toy for them. We sold a million of these devices that are quite popular in the U.S."
After Russia launched its full-scale war, Azhnyuk, by then living in Ukraine, pivoted to the defense space.
"I was like, OK, I've got to move on from Petcube and do something to protect my country," he says. "And so now we're making not cameras that fling treats but cameras that fling explosives at people who came to occupy our land."
The cameras he's talking about are part of an image recognition system incorporated into first-person-view or FPV drones.
"We are leaders in autonomous drones and thermal cameras," he says.
A Lupynis-10-TFL-1 drone is seen in the air at a test site in central Ukraine.
The autonomous Lupynis TFL-1 drone combines a small, affordable FPV drone with an autonomous terminal guidance unit. That means the drone has an onboard computer and AI technology that takes control during the last 500 meters (or 0.3 miles) of the flight.
"But it's very different from, say, the kind of AI that sits on those data centers that power Claude," he says. "Or even the kind of AI that does self-driving for your Tesla."
Azhnyuk says this AI technology uses simple tools to recognize images and make smart software "which then allows you either to navigate autonomously without a human or to choose a target autonomously or to fly toward the target autonomously."
The Fourth Law's autonomous drone is named after the 20th century Ukrainian poet and political activist Anatoliy Lupynis, who endured decades of Soviet imprisonment and psychiatric torture. The company named all its weapons after Ukrainian intellectuals and artists killed or persecuted by Russians in the last century.
"It is important for us to help to resurrect those people," Azhnyuk says. "Their ideas are still with us. And now they're fighting back."
The Fourth Law teams test the Lupynis TFL-1 drones every day. We meet one team in an overgrown field outside Kyiv. They've set up a fold-out table near a copse of trees and a hornets' nest.
A slight, sandy-haired man stands up from the fold-out table and says he's the head of the test and integration team. He uses the military call sign Stab. The Fourth Law asked NPR not to reveal this location or the identities of those who work here for security reasons.
"So you see here is our camera, plus the microcomputer inside this 3-D model," Stab says. "All of the mind is here," he adds with a laugh.
The microcomputer inside the drone runs on a deep-learning neural network that uses hours of combat footage to recognize targets.
"Like vehicles, light-armored vehicles, tanks, and flying objects," he says.
The drone begins its flight guided by a pilot, who also asked us to use his call sign, Professor. He navigates the drone with a control stick. He selects a target and checks a video monitor. Then he sees a red flash.
The drone has locked onto its target: a dilapidated car an employee is driving along a dirt road. Professor lets go of the control stick. The drone flies itself to the car. The microcomputer inside constantly runs algorithms to keep it on course.
"See, Professor is doing nothing, and the drone is doing everything itself," Stab says.
On the battlefield, the drone would carry an explosive to hit the car. The test drone doesn't have one.
Stab says AI drones are useful because they're hard to jam. He also insists safeguards are in place so The Fourth Law drones won't go rogue. Humans select the target, he says.
Yaroslav Azhnyuk, the founder of the AI drone company The Fourth Law, previously worked in Silicon Valley and started several companies, including one that makes smartphone-powered cameras that use artificial intelligence to watch and entertain pets while they're alone.
Back in the conference room, Azhnyuk says his company's AI-powered drones are not killer robots.
"They're very far from a Terminator kind of scenario, right?" he says. "These are just sort of calculators. Like they're no more likely for a Terminator uprising than a warehouse with artillery shells."
He does acknowledge, though, that AI is developing quickly. "It's not unthinkable," he says, "to imagine that some years in the future there will be drones that will be able to make their own decisions on designating targets or not."
That's the future. Right now he is focused on what Ukraine needs today.
"Our adversaries are using the very same technologies, and if they use AI for decision-making in an offensive scenario, and if we refuse to use AI in decision-making in a defensive scenario, we've lost, right?" he says. "Because their systems are capable of making decisions in microseconds, and human control takes minutes, hours and sometimes days."
If Ukraine loses, he says, "there's no way for us to argue about the ethics of AI."

After the initial kinetic period, the hostilities will likely continue whether or not there is active shooting, forcing every affected actor to prepare as if violence could return at any moment. Talk of victory or defeat—whether for U.S. domestic political consumption or, worse, for the Pentagon's actual war planning—has little meaning in a conflict that Iranians rightly expect to continue for many years to come. America’s regional allies that have to live in this environment can see this clearly and want none of it. That constant risk of a sudden eruption would deny the Gulf states the kind of stability and security they desperately need to get their economic projects back on track. And it would leave the door open to flare-ups elsewhere, such as the Houthi sweep down the Red Sea coast and seizure of the Bab el-Mandeb chokepoint in September.
The suggestion to launch a renewed, intense military campaign represents a tacit recognition of U.S. failure. No amount of happy talk about the increased volume of oil flowing through the Strait of Hormuz and the mounting economic costs of the blockade on Iran can mask the new strategic reality. Iran has proved that it retains the capacity to close the strait when it wishes, and the Houthi advances in the Red Sea and effective targeting of Saudi Arabia have expanded Iran’s range of options. A diplomatic solution has been rendered largely impossible by the Trump administration’s constantly changing positions and incoherent messaging, which give Iran no reason to even consider a deal. From Tehran’s vantage point, any agreement would likely be broken before the ink was dry. Trump’s signature, as Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney has quipped , is written in pencil.
But if Iran can live with the state of potential war and episodic eruptions of violence, the Arab Gulf states cannot. Bahrain, Kuwait, and Qatar have no alternative shipping routes other than the Strait of Hormuz. Saudi Arabia, which for a time thought it could adapt by shifting its oil exports to the Red Sea, learned the hard way that it could not count on that option in the rapidly mutating conflict. The Houthi seizure of Yemen’s Red Sea coast and control over the Bab el-Mandeb and an Iraqi militia drone strike temporarily disabling the East-West pipeline put an end to that fantasy.
The escalating Houthi attacks on Saudi airports, oil facilities, and other targets, along with the Saudi inability to mount an effective military challenge to the new Houthi positions, have caught Riyadh in an untenable bind. The United States rather shockingly declined to come Riyadh’s assistance against the Houthis. That may be for the best. The large-scale international intervention against the Houthis proposed by the Saudi ambassador to the United States this week would simply pull the world deeper into an unwinnable war scape. Nor is the Mecca Pact between Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan going to ride to a military rescue in Yemen. The three countries may have ratified the defense pact and floated intervention possibilities, but neither country is likely to provide the ground troops that would be needed.

But there could be a silver lining in this parade of failure. With no plausible military path to victory in Yemen or over Iran, Saudi Arabia has little stomach for renewed war and strong incentives to find a diplomatic way out of its predicament. That’s probably why a high-level Mecca Pact delegation visited Tehran this week and why other Gulf states have been urgently looking for a diplomatic path away from war. The damage inflicted on Iran’s economy and infrastructure is real and will require massive rebuilding. There’s precedent. Following the conclusion of the horrifically destructive eight-year Iran-Iraq War and the death of Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini in the late 1980s, the new leadership of Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani and Ali Khamenei chose to ratchet down revolutionary rhetoric and militant adventurism and create a period of calm to allow desperately needed economic reconstruction. Perhaps the new Revolutionary Guard-led Iran would do the same.
A de-escalatory deal between Iran and the Saudi-led bloc would pose a challenge to Israel and its allies in the region, primarily the United Arab Emirates, as well as to U.S. conceits about determining the terms of regional order. The regime has strong grounds to believe that it (if not the Iranian people) will survive and prosper even under another round of intense military attack. The possible strike would simply accelerate the escalatory dynamics and reinforce emergent Arab convictions that the United States has become a destabilizing force and an unreliable partner that cannot be counted on to provide security or order. When the smoke once again clears, in Iran and in the countries that bore the costs of its retaliation, the renewed campaign may well prove to have been the final blow not to the Iranian regime but to U.S. leadership in the region.

“What concerns me most is the possibility of an open-ended commitment: continuing to spend heavily without a clear political end state or a settlement that delivers lasting security,” he said.
Currently, the US must weigh three costs, Phillips said: the money spent, the military capability consumed and the economic disruption generated.
