The small-cap Santa Claus rally is on — but a December Fed rate hike has spoiled it before: Chart of the Day
Small-cap stocks sat down to their favorite holiday trade on Tuesday. History says a Fed hike can leave them with a much smaller helping than usual.
The trade is Jeff Hirsch's "Small Cap Feast." Buy the Russell 2000 at the close on the Tuesday before Thanksgiving, then sell on the second trading day of the new year — Jan. 5 this time.
It rides through the Santa Claus rally , which his father, Yale Hirsch, discovered. It has worked in 36 of the past 47 years, according to the Stock Trader's Almanac, which Hirsch publishes and edits.
This year, the Fed meets Dec. 9, right in the middle of it. After Wednesday's hotter-than-expected inflation report , Polymarket bettors put the chances of a rate hike at about 96%.
That has been the trade's weak spot. In the nine years since 1983 when the Fed hiked during the trade, the Russell 2000's ( ^RUT ) median gain was about 1%, according to Yahoo Finance calculations. Without a hike, it was about 4%.
Stock Trader's Almanac, Federal Reserve, Yahoo Finance Three of the four worst runs since 1983 came in hike years, including 2018, when the Fed raised rates days before stocks' Christmas Eve rout. The fourth, 2024, came after the Fed cut rates but warned that fewer cuts were coming.
Nine years is a small sample, and four came back-to-back from 2015 to 2018. Leave those out, and the median in hike years is still about 1%.
The trade's recent record is thinner too. It has worked only half the time over the past dozen years.
Stock Trader's Almanac, Federal Reserve, Yahoo Finance Listen to the bond market
Drew Pettit, chief investment strategist at Roundhill Investments, is watching credit spreads — the extra yield investors demand to lend to companies instead of the US government.
"The signal that the market is telling the Fed they're making a mistake to me is the correlation between spreads and rates," Pettit said on Yahoo Finance's Market Hang on Tuesday. Small caps lagged in the latest pullback as spreads widened and rates rose, he added.

When President Donald Trump gave the order to commence large-scale military operations against Iran nearly nine months ago, his administration and those tasked with executing the assault put an unprecedented level of faith into automated systems capable of calculations far beyond humanity's capacities.
The initial results of the high-tech joint U.S.-Israeli blitz appeared promising. Within just 24 hours, more than 1,000 targets had been struck, including Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, with the help of the AI -enabled, Palantir-powered command and control and intelligence software platform known as the Maven Smart System.
By the one-month mark, that figure would reach 13,000.
Yet despite the administration's frequent declarations of victory that followed, such a conclusive outcome today eludes even the most optimistic predictive models.
Iran, now led by Khamenei's son, continues to strike at the U.S. military presence in the region as well as the few oil tankers braving the effectively blockaded Strait of Hormuz. Even Trump and Vice President JD Vance have acknowledged that the impact of the ongoing confrontation likely factored into the Republicans' midterm election defeats on November 3 as domestic support for the war continues to plunge.
Not all the blame can be put on the machine. Former officials say AI-supported systems largely performed their tasks as programmed, while the humans behind them may simply be unable to keep up.
"These targeting systems are currently being deployed primarily to maximize operational efficiency; generating as many potential high-value military targets for strikes as possible," Steven Feldstein, former U.S. deputy assistant secretary of state now serving as senior fellow and director of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace's Democracy, Conflict, and Governance Program, told Newsweek .
"The bigger question—does increasing the pace and scale of strikes lead to military victory or strategic success—aren’t being dealt with by these systems. I think one emerging problem is that leaders have conflated speed/scale with military victory and have been surprised when conflicts like the Iran war have not validated that theory."
Defining victory has been a consistent challenge for much of the U.S.' post-World War II military interventions. While a handful of operations were met with relatively swift success, such as the 1980s invasions of Grenada and Panama as well as the first Gulf War in 1991, longer and larger-scale confrontations in Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan and Iraq were often subject to shifting objectives that contributed to less decisive results.
It wasn't for a lack of firepower. In all of the conflicts, the U.S. enjoyed an overwhelming superiority and key technological advances.
In January, just over a month before the U.S. and Israel launched their joint war against Iran, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth vowed to usher in a new era of U.S. dominance through an "AI-first" strategy. Less than two weeks into the war, U.S. Central Command chief Admiral Brad Cooper touted in March that a "variety of advanced AI tools" were being leveraged so that "our leaders can cut through the noise and make smarter decisions faster than the enemy can react."
By July, however, Cooper reportedly ran into a problem reminiscent of remarks made by Air Force General Curtis LeMay during the Korean War and Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld during the war in Afghanistan. Cooper told the Pentagon, Joint Chiefs of Staff and the White House that the list of designated targets in Iran "had been mostly exhausted," according to sources cited by Axios.
Feldstein saw a fundamental flaw in the U.S. doctrine that AI may not only fail to resolve but could potentially exacerbate.
"An excessive focus on operational outputs [how many targets struck] rather than on the strategic considerations necessary to achieve broader U.S. objectives have compromised the ability to secure a meaningful victory," he said.
For all the swiftness with which AI can draw up a vast list of targets, he pointed to two lingering risks associated with the equally as important task of judging what to strike—or not—in the heat of battle.
"First, it becomes increasingly difficult to ensure meaningful human control over what targets are actually being struck," Feldstein said. "The short windows of time that military operators have to review collected intelligence and packages and ensure they’re appropriately focused on legitimate military targets is exceedingly small. The consequence is that mistakes will be made.
"Second, because machine intelligence is responsible for doing so much of the work of integrating intelligence into targeting recommendations, it is easy for design flaws to lead to outsized problems."
A tragic example of AI-linked miscalculation emerged the first day of the war. Among the hundreds of sites subject to the February 28 opening shock-and-awe bombardment made possible through the Maven Smart System was the all-girls Shajareh Tayyebeh Elementary School in the southern Iranian city of Minab.
The Pentagon has yet to release the findings of its internal probe on the attack that killed 156 civilians, including 120 children. Multiple media reports have cited U.S. officials confirming U.S. involvement in the strike, which was said to have been the result of outdated intelligence, ignored warnings and an over-reliance on AI.
The toll marks one of the deadliest U.S. attacks on civilians since the 1988 USS Vincennes shootdown of civilian Iran Air Flight 655 , killing 290 passengers during a prior period of Persian Gulf turmoil in the final year of Iran-Iraq War. Without any AI to speak of at the time, subsequent investigations—including one carried out by Newsweek —blamed human error committed by overwhelmed personnel on a warship within range of enemy aircraft.
Larry Lewis, founder of the Center for Naval Analyses' Center for Autonomy and Artificial Intelligence and former adviser to the State Department and Defense Department on civilian harm reduction, warned that the introduction of AI alone may not necessarily improve upon the military's efforts to prevent civilian casualties, which he said "often came from false assumptions that go unchallenged," in his experience.
"If AI simply copies current military tasks, it can speed up that type of error," Lewis told Newsweek . “The biggest risk isn’t from whether a human or a machine make decisions. Rather, it’s about whether anyone checks the answer afterwards. If you don’t assess the effects of operations involving AI, you can’t say whether your tools are making things better or worse. And you don’t learn from your mistakes.
“Militaries rarely ask for civilian harm prevention features in AI tools, so they don’t get them. If militaries want to reduce the risk of incidents like Minab, they need to start asking for those tools.”
Meanwhile, for Tehran, controversies surrounding the U.S. use of AI in the conflict have only fueled claims of the conflict being in violation of international law.
"Rapid technological advances are changing the nature of warfare. They increase the risks of escalation, miscalculation and civilian casualties," Iranian Ambassador to the United Nations Gholamhossein Darzi said during a Security Council meeting on Tuesday.
"Technology must serve humanity and use for exclusively peaceful purposes, not aggression, domination or violations of state sovereignty. The weaponization of AI and its use as an instrument of aggression and conflict escalation, as witnessed in the two recent wars of aggression against Iran in June 2025 and February 2026 by the United States and the Israeli regime raises serious concerns."
Newsweek has reached out to the Defense Department for comment.
Minab is not the only reported example of AI-involved miscalculation throughout the past nine months of the war.
In September, CNN reported that U.S. intelligence relying on AI-generated information had initially determined that a Chinese cargo vessel sailing in the Middle East was carrying components of a nuclear weapons program. It was only after assets had been mobilized to forcefully board the ship that analysts determined the nature of the cargo had been misidentified.
But as one of the unnamed sources was cited as saying, the incident “almost started a war."
Joseph Chapa, research fellow at the University of Massachusetts Boston's Applied Ethics Center and the Institute for Ethics and Emerging Technology, said reports surrounding the Minab strike and Chinese vessel close call demonstrated "how users' overreliance on AI-generated outputs can lead to catastrophic outcomes."
"One partial solution to this challenge is to provide a better baseline education on how statistical AI systems work to users," Chapa told Newsweek . "A joint force more aware of the limitations of statistical AI will be a joint force better prepared to identify and mitigate risks of inaccurate AI outputs."
There are limits, however, to the degree to which complex AI systems relying on vast datasets could be expected to produce accurate assessments and the pace at which human operators could reasonably decipher such vast quantities of information.
"Those limitations mean that users of these tools need to create institutional standards and requirements to validate the output of large models, especially in high-consequence applications such as combat operations," Chapa said.
"Unfortunately, this creates an inherent tension: Users expect AI to help them to go faster. But validating AI outputs requires users to go slower. This is simply a fact about modern, statistical AI systems. There is no easy button or cheat code to sidestep this tension."'

The Pentagon blacklisted Anthropic, the AI giant behind by Claude, on the eve of the Iran war over the company's alleged push for certain restrictions in the military application of its technology. Trump has publicly dismissed calls for greater guardrails on AI as he strives for global leadership, although he has more recently signaled some openness following talks with Chinese President Xi Jinping, who has called for humans to remain in charge of AI-related military decisions.
Still, the dawn of the "first AI war," as the U.S. conflict with Iran has come to be described, continues to elicit concerns that military decisions on both the tactical and strategic level may be drifting further from mankind's hands.
Recent years have already seen the rise of development of autonomous weapons systems, such as underwater drones tasked with carrying out missions in the absence of constant communication with operators thousands of miles away.
"When targets are generated faster than humans can meaningfully verify them, it’s the machine who makes life and death decisions," Brianna Rosen, a former White House National Security Council adviser now serving as research director for frontier security at the Institute for AI Policy and Strategy, told Newsweek .
“AI changes not only how we use force, but how we think about the use of force. It shapes the decision before a human enters the picture, determining which intelligence is surfaced, which targets are prioritized, and which options are considered. If humans are simply rubber-stamping those recommendations, keeping them in the loop is not the same as meaningful human control."
