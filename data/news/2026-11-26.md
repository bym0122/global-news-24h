At least 490,334 Russian soldiers have died since the start of the war in Ukraine in 2022, according to Russian government statistics analysed by the BBC.
The leaked Rosstat data reveal how the full-scale invasion of Ukraine has fundamentally changed mortality patterns in Russia, and show the high cost paid in human life in a war that Vladimir Putin had assumed would last only days but is now almost five years old.
BBC Russian has had access to more than 11 million death records from 2021 to September 2026, after a hack by Belarusian group Cyber Partisans BY and Ukraine&#x27;s Silent Crow.
Russia&#x27;s statistics agency admitted to a cyber-attack, but denied any protected information had been leaked.
Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said on Wednesday that only Russia&#x27;s defence ministry could officially report data on losses: "I suggest no other data be taken into account." The defence ministry has not released any official casualty figures for several years.
Together with independent Russian outlet Mediazona, BBC Russian has compared the Rosstat data with its own list of confirmed military deaths, pulled from open sources since the start of the invasion, and it has found matches for 90% of the records.
Military experts estimate that the BBC&#x27;s list of 290,012 deaths, gleaned from official reports, social media, new memorials and graves, may account for 45-55% of the true death toll, which would range from 527,295 to 644,471.
The records show that 65% of 18 to 24 year-olds in Russia who lost their lives in 2025 died in the war. For those aged 25-34 the number was 59%. Only among 45 to 54 year-olds does cardiovascular disease claim more Russian lives than the conflict.
The data also reveal the extent of losses in specific areas of Ukraine. Some 20,000 Russian soldiers died fighting for control of 10 Ukrainian villages whose combined pre-war population was 12,000.
During the fight for one small village called Andriivka, south of the town of Bakhmut in Donetsk region, 1,122 Russians lost their lives. Before the war 74 people lived there.
Russia does not give public figures for losses sustained since the war began in February 2022. And two years ago Rosstat stopped publishing data of deaths from external causes, which was used to gauge the extent of military losses.
For example, only 70% of deaths we have established of soldiers from the occupied regions of Donetsk and Luhansk in eastern Ukraine who fought in the Russian army were accounted for in the Rosstat files.
Overall, the Rosstat dataset confirms the trends that we have observed with our own count.

The Rosstat dataset contains no names but does include ages, places of residence, places of death and also circumstances of death.
We have filtered the records first by location, leaving only Ukraine as well as Russian border regions that saw incursions by the Ukrainian army.
Next, we identified 425,174 entries explicitly indicating a military-related cause of death, such as explosions or shrapnel during combat operations.

Fox & Friends co-hosts Brian Kilmeade and Rachel Campos-Duffy clashed during Wednesday’s broadcast over President Donald Trump ’s war with Iran , with Campos-Duffy arguing that voters had rejected the conflict and Kilmeade defending the decision to confront Tehran.
The exchange comes weeks after Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress in the November 3 midterm elections, as the war's cost and direction remain politically contentious. Their dispute highlighted a broader split on the right over Trump’s foreign policy and whether the conflict has undermined the domestic agenda that helped return him to the White House.
The disagreement sharpened when Kilmeade argued that confronting Iran had long been a U.S. priority. “Your list. Not mine!” Campos-Duffy interjected after Kilmeade said the Iran issue had been “on the top of everyone’s list for 35 years.”
Brian Kilmeade and Rachel Campos-Duffy Clash Over Iran War
The dispute began during a discussion of Trump’s planned meeting with Democratic Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and the potential for cooperation between the president and the incoming Democratic-controlled Congress. Kilmeade envisioned a scenario in which Democrats tried to use government or military funding as leverage to force Trump to dismiss Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth or withdraw from Iran.
Lawrence Jones called such demands a “non-starter,” and Kilmeade agreed. Campos-Duffy immediately challenged him, asking whether he meant the war itself was a non-starter. Kilmeade replied: “That’s just the beginning of it,” arguing that demands framed as ultimatums would put Trump “in a corner” rather than create room for negotiation.
Campos-Duffy offered a different political reading. She argued that opposition to the war was one area where populist factions on the left and right found common ground in the midterms. Trump, she said, could potentially capitalize on those overlapping constituencies to work with Democrats during the next two years.
“The president is a populist,” Campos-Duffy said. “And there is populism on the left. And there is populism on the right. And there are some places where this overlaps.”
Trump’s Midterm Strategist Outlines What Republicans Need to Change
The disagreement reflects a deeper foreign-policy divide within Trump’s political coalition, according to Jon Hoffman, a research fellow at the Cato Institute and adjunct professor at George Mason University.
“Foreign policy represents one of the deepest fissures on the political right and the Iran war brought this to the forefront,” Hoffman told Newsweek .
Hoffman distinguished between MAGA, which he described as “a brand and a cult of personality around a single individual,” and the broader America First movement that helped propel Trump to the presidency.
“The Iran war was the antithesis of America First and has fractured the Trump coalition,” Hoffman said. “It was a war of choice launched on behalf of Israel despite no imminent threat to the security, prosperity, or freedom of the American people.”
His comments mirror a central tension in the Fox exchange. Campos-Duffy argued that opposition to the war extended across the political spectrum and later told Kilmeade that the conflict had “cost us the Trump agenda.” Kilmeade rejected that assessment, arguing that Iran had presented a problem that U.S. leaders had needed to address for decades.
Hoffman said the political repercussions extend beyond the disagreement over the war itself, arguing that the conflict has drawn the administration away from other priorities.
“The America First constituency is tired of endless wars in the Middle East ,” he said. “Even from a perspective of Trump’s own interests, this war was foolish: It has pulled attention and resources away from his other priorities and now represents his legacy.”
He also pointed to Republicans ’ midterm losses as a warning about the political consequences of prolonged military involvement.
“The midterms should be a wake-up call for Republicans: Endless wars are not just bad policy; they are bad politics,” Hoffman said. “They should push for a swift end to this disastrous conflict.”
The disagreement grew more pointed when Kilmeade defended the rationale for confronting Iran, invoking decades of concern over Tehran and the 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action, the nuclear agreement reached between Iran and world powers during the Obama administration.
“Addressing the Iran situation has been on the top of everyone’s list for 35 years,” Kilmeade said, before beginning to discuss the JCPOA.
Kilmeade acknowledged the distinction but continued, describing the nuclear agreement as a “disaster” and arguing that someone eventually had to address the Iran issue. Campos-Duffy countered with the political cost she believes Trump has paid for doing so.
“Well, it cost us the Trump agenda, sadly,” she said.
“Well, it is the debate of the century right now,” he said before moving the program to another segment with Carley Shimkus.
Iran War Has Become a Political Liability for Trump
The argument unfolded against the backdrop of a conflict that began when the United States and Israel launched strikes against Iran on February 28, following months of tensions and negotiations over Tehran’s nuclear program.
Fighting has continued through periods of ceasefire and diplomatic negotiations, while attacks around the Strait of Hormuz and across the Persian Gulf have disrupted global energy and commercial shipping.
The war has also imposed high financial costs. The Defense Department told Congress in November that U.S. military spending on the conflict had reached at least $48.3 billion.
The conflict became a major issue in this month’s midterm elections, when Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress. The BBC cited polling showing 63 percent of Americans surveyed said the United States was wrong to start the war, while an NBC News exit poll found 74 percent of voters considered the conflict a crucial or major factor in their vote. The results underscored the political pressure surrounding the conflict and help explain why Campos-Duffy characterized opposition to the war as an issue that could cross ideological lines.
Trump has repeatedly defended his handling of Iran and maintained that military pressure was necessary to address the country’s nuclear and security threat. The administration has simultaneously pursued diplomatic contacts with Tehran, though a June agreement failed to produce a lasting end to hostilities.
Fox Hosts Previously Sparred Over Russia and Ukraine
Wednesday was not the first time Kilmeade and Campos-Duffy publicly split over Trump’s approach to a major international conflict. The pair had another heated discussion on Fox & Friends in October, this time over Russia, Ukraine and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky.
During the October 11 exchange, Campos-Duffy pointed to Trump’s suggestion that Ukraine might need an election and potentially a new leader. She accused Zelensky of taking actions that hurt Trump politically despite what she described as the administration’s generosity toward Ukraine’s war effort.
Kilmeade challenged that line of argument and raised the implications of penalizing countries for purchasing Russian energy. The exchange reflected the same underlying foreign-policy divide that resurfaced Wednesday: whether Trump should prioritize confronting U.S. adversaries abroad or limit American commitments that parts of his political coalition see as expensive distractions from a domestic populist program.
The Iran disagreement, however, carries an additional political dimension because Campos-Duffy explicitly linked the conflict to Trump’s ability to enact his agenda after the Republican midterm losses. Kilmeade rejected that connection outright.
