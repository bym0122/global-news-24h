British steelmakers have been pushed further behind European rivals on electricity costs after the US-Israeli war on Iran caused a surge in global gas prices.
The premium paid by the British industry over its French counterpart has risen to almost £30 a megawatt hour (MWh), its highest level in three years, according to analysis by the lobby group UK Steel.
Energy prices have long been one of the main factors businesses cite when asked what is holding back British industry. For Andy Burnham they represent perhaps the biggest obstacle to his goal of “reindustrialising” the UK .
Analysts blame the UK’s high electricity prices on its reliance on gas for power generation, which is crucial for cleaner steelmaking with electric arc furnaces .
The way the market is set up means that the wholesale gas price determines the price of electricity even from other sources that cost much less to produce, such as wind or solar power, and Iran’s closure of the strait of Hormuz in response to Donald Trump’s bombing campaign has caused global gas prices to soar .
Electricity prices in France are mainly determined by its nuclear reactors, while in Germany heavily polluting coal still plays a large role in generation.
Graph showing electricity costs in UK, Germany, France and Canada. Comparisons with European rivals are particularly important for the UK because the EU is often the main market for British manufactured products, including about 75% of exported steel, worth about £3bn.
The gap with France had fallen in the previous three years to reach £18 a MWh last year, although it remains below the £153 reached in 2022 when Russia’s invasion of Ukraine caused a crisis in the global gas market. The gap with Germany this year fell slightly to about £12.
Under Keir Starmer most of the costs added by government policy to heavy industrial businesses’ electricity costs were removed last year. The main option to bring the price down further, however, would be to set up more cheap renewables – a challenge in the short term – or to directly subsidise prices.
The director general of UK Steel, Peter Brennan, said direct subsidies were the only option to help steelmakers. The lobby group has proposed a cap on prices that it estimates would cost about £51m a year. The government would pay the difference when prices go above that level, while the industry would pay the government if prices go below.
“British steelmakers are being asked to compete with one hand tied behind their backs,” Brennan said. “Despite welcome government action, our electricity prices remain vastly higher than those of our European and global competitors, and the gap with France is getting worse.

Better AI Chip Stock: Taiwan Semiconductor vs. Micron
While companies like Nvidia and AMD may get most of the headline glory in the AI realm for their powerful processors, the reality is these companies only design some of the chips that go into data centers. The fabrication muscle in the sector lies in a handful of chip foundry businesses, with Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (NYSE: TSM) and Micron (NASDAQ: MU) being the two largest in the world by market cap.
But which one makes for the better buy? Let's dig in, as these two are very different investments.

Image source: Getty Images. Micron and Taiwan Semiconductor aren't competitors
On the surface, Micron and Taiwan Semiconductor are both chip foundry companies. Both take in client orders, then create their chips. However, that's where the similarities end.
Micron and Taiwan Semiconductor produce two entirely different types of chips; Micron designs and makes memory chips, and Taiwan Semiconductor makes logic chips designed by other companies. Both are required in accelerated computing units like GPUs, but each serves a different role.
Furthermore, the technologies behind each one are radically different. There isn't a ton that separates one memory chip from another. Take basic computer RAM, for example. Nobody cares a lot about which brand of RAM is in a laptop, only what capacity that RAM has. That's because most memory chips are practically the same, although Micron is challenging that notion. It recently agreed to design an industry-first custom memory chip for Nvidia's GPUs, which could be a different avenue to lock in better long-term contract deals.
Because one manufacturer's memory chips are much like another's, they are subject to commodity-like pricing. This year, demand for memory chips has steeply outpaced supply, causing prices to soar. The shortage is expected to get worse in 2027 and 2028, which would allow Micron to keep turning huge profits on its chips.
The logic chips that come out of Taiwan Semiconductor's foundries are completely different. Companies like Nvidia and AMD design processors, and their various devices can deliver competitive edges on a range of criteria as the chipmakers work to improve speed, energy efficiency, transistor density, and more. Choosing a reliable chip manufacturer is a big part of this design process, and with TSMC's massive production facilities, proven track record, and leading technology, it's the partner that is most often selected. However, TSMC has relatively greater production capacity than the memory chip industry, and has been expanding production capacity in recent years, so it was able to mostly handle the AI demand rush.
Micron holds an edge in the revenue growth department, as it's still benefiting from the effect of soaring memory chip prices due to the shortage. However, TSMC's growth rate isn't bad either.
TSM Revenue (Quarterly YoY Growth) data by YCharts.
Another area where Micron holds an edge is valuation. Investors today can pick up Micron shares at a much cheaper 5.3 times forward earnings compared to TSMC's 24.
Why the huge price discrepancy? It all has to do with Micron operating in what has historically been a highly cyclical industry. Every time memory demand outpaces supply, the chipmakers start to build new fabs. By the time they come online, the demand waves have often slackened, supply leaps ahead, and prices tumble.
The market isn't convinced that the current wave of intense memory chip demand driven by the AI infrastructure build-out will last, and it's discounting it as a result. But Micron and others in the memory industry have said the shortage is going to get worse before it gets better. If Micron's stock rises throughout the year due to strong demand, it could deliver incredible returns.
TSMC is also a solid investment, and because the logic chip industry is not as cyclical (it still is, but not to the same degree), the market is not as concerned about its outlook, and therefore is not valuing the stock at a discount.
I think this is a huge mismatch, and Micron makes for the better buy, at least when upside potential is considered. While TSMC is still a fantastic investment, the mismatch between Micron's stock price and its earnings potential is just too much to ignore. Investors should pounce on the deal.

Google Research's Research@ event is the company's internal showcase for work still moving from lab to product, and this year's edition gave Synaptics a stage to describe a chip that had been in development for roughly 18 to 24 months. The device belongs to Synaptics' Astra line of AI-native processors and microcontrollers, a product family the company has built specifically for local, low-power AI workloads rather than general-purpose computing.
Patel used the Mountain View appearance to lay out the chip's capabilities without disclosing full specifications, a sequencing choice that let Google tie the story to its broader research narrative before Synaptics controls the commercial unveiling six days later. The chip can process voice, vision, motion, and contextual sensing data simultaneously, and it adds GPU and multimedia processing along with hardware-rooted security, according to the companies' public statements cited in a report from SBS .

That caveat matters because power-efficiency claims in the chip industry are notoriously workload-dependent. Synaptics did not simply license Google's silicon design and ship it unchanged. The Astra chip combines the Coral NPU M2 with Synaptics' own Torq NPU and standard Arm CPU cores, giving the company a path to differentiate on top of Google's open accelerator work. That architecture lets Synaptics target a wide span of constrained devices, from battery-powered wearables to fixed industrial cameras, with one underlying platform rather than a separate chip for each category.
A less flashy but arguably more consequential part of the announcement is software. Synaptics and Google Research spent roughly 18 months building an open-source compiler on top of MLIR, the Multi-Level Intermediate Representation framework, so the chip can run standard machine learning frameworks and model formats rather than locking developers into a proprietary toolchain. Patel framed this as a break from the walled-garden software that has defined microcontroller development for years, a point echoed in R&D World's account of his remarks. Developers can already prototype against the underlying Coral NPU using Coralboard, a development platform Synaptics launched earlier this year that pairs the NPU with Astra processors ahead of the full chip's release.
Coral's first milestone was a front end built on RISC-V, the open instruction set architecture that has gained ground across the chip industry as an alternative to proprietary cores. M2 marks the second milestone and adds what Google describes as the first Coral vector processor. Google Research has also said its next design, M3, is already complete and adds native 32-bit floating-point support so the chip can run Gemma 3 270M, a compact 270-million-parameter language model, directly on-device. Neither company has given a shipping date for M3-based silicon, which means the chip debuting this month is already one generation behind Google's internal roadmap.

Patel's most direct claim from the Research@ appearance was that the chip will not stay in the lab and will also be built into Google devices, a statement widely picked up across outlets covering the preview. He also highlighted the microcontroller's support for high-density DRAM, describing memory capacity as a source of scalability for future products built on the same platform. Synaptics plans to formally unveil the chip at its Tech Day event on October 20 at 1151 McKay Drive in San Jose, which the company has billed as including a noon press and analyst briefing followed by a demonstration showcase.
What Patel did not say is just as notable. He declined to identify which Google hardware lines would adopt the chip or offer any release window, leaving a gap between the marketing claim and a verifiable product commitment. That gap is where skepticism from hardware analysts tends to concentrate, since chipmakers routinely tout future design wins that take years, or never materialize, to reach shelves.
Why Google Keeps Investing in Edge Silicon Partnerships
Google's data center strategy has long rested on designing its own Tensor Processing Units alongside the models that run on them, and the company is now pushing the same co-design philosophy to the edge. The logic, as described in reporting on the Research@ event, is that existing edge chips consume too much energy and respond too slowly for always-on, ambient AI experiences that keep personal data on the device instead of sending it to the cloud. A fragmented market of proprietary microcontroller designs compounds the problem by making it harder for developers to build once and deploy across multiple vendors' hardware.
That framing explains why Google chose a partnership model rather than building its own edge chip from scratch. Synaptics brings decades of microcontroller and sensor-fusion experience along with existing customer relationships in wearables and industrial equipment, while Google supplies the Coral accelerator design and compiler work it has already validated internally.

When Google and Synaptics first announced their collaboration on edge AI for the Internet of Things, Billy Rutledge, Director of Systems Research at Google Research, laid out why the two companies paired up in the first place. Target Devices: Wearables, Smart Glasses, and the Industrial Floor
The use cases Google and Synaptics are chasing span two very different markets. On the consumer side, smart glasses, watches, and connected cameras need to recognize speech, gestures, and context without draining a small battery in a few hours. On the industrial side, vision and voice systems on factory floors need similar always-on sensing but with tighter requirements around reliability and hardware-rooted security, since these devices often sit on networks that also touch safety-critical equipment.
Covering both markets with one chip platform is an ambitious bet. It means Synaptics has to hit aggressive power targets for battery-powered wearables while also meeting the durability and security expectations of industrial buyers, all on the same underlying silicon. If the company pulls it off, Astra could become a default reference design that OEMs reach for instead of commissioning custom edge AI silicon.
This announcement did not come out of nowhere. Google's interest in specialized AI silicon traces back to a 2017 Google research paper on its first-generation Tensor Processing Unit, which reported performance-per-watt gains of 30 to 80 times over the Intel Haswell CPUs and Nvidia K80 GPUs it was benchmarked against in production neural-network inference workloads, per R&D World's account of the comparison.
