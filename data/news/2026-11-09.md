The odds come from the futures market. After Friday's jobs report, which showed employers added 165,000 jobs in October and unemployment slipped to 4.1%, traders put the chance of a quarter-point increase at the Fed's December 8 and 9 meeting at about 78%, Reuters reported, and by Sunday CME's FedWatch tool had it at 81%. The Fed raised its benchmark rate on September 16 for the first time since July 2023, to a range of 3.75% to 4%, and held it there on October 28 by a vote of 10 to 2, with the two dissenters, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, wanting another increase that day. Inflation by the Fed's preferred measure was 3.6% in September, 3.1% without food and energy, against a 2% target.
A rate increase with stocks already falling reads as a warning to retirees that the Fed will not step in to cushion this decline the way it did in 2008 and 2020, when inflation gave it room to cut. The record since 2000 also says the market was higher a year later in 12 of the 14 cases, so the warning is about the next few months rather than the next few years.
Warsh described the mechanism at his October 28 press conference: "Interest rates get in the cracks. Interest rates affect directly or indirectly, almost everything." KPMG chief economist Diane Swonk told Yahoo Finance that higher rates taking "a little steam off of equity prices" can cool inflation among the wealthiest households. If you are retired and living off a portfolio, you are the household that policy is aimed at.
Take a 68-year-old retired engineer and his wife, 65, a retired school administrator. They have $1,800,000: $1,080,000 in an S&P 500 index fund, $540,000 in a total bond market index fund and $180,000 in a money market fund, a 60/30/10 mix. Social Security pays them $3,600 a month, $43,200 a year. They spend $108,000, so $64,800 a year comes out of the portfolio, a 3.6% withdrawal rate. Those are assumptions, so put your own numbers beside them.
Since 2000 the Fed has raised its target rate 40 times. On 14 of those days the S&P 500 closed 5% or more below its high of the previous year, which is where it sits now. The table shows how far the index was below its high that day, how much further it fell within 12 months, and where it stood a year later, using the largest S&P 500 index fund with dividends excluded.
The median outcome a year later was a gain of 14.6%. The two losses are the ones that should hold your attention, because they look most like December: the first two increases of 2022, made early in a cycle with inflation running hot. After the March 2022 increase the index fell another 18.1% within the year, and after the May increase another 16.9%. Top to bottom, the S&P 500 fell 25.4% from January 3, 2022 to October 12, 2022, and it took until January 19, 2024 to get back to its starting point, with the Fed raising the whole way. December would be the second increase of this cycle; September's came with the index 3.1% below its high, and the Fed's own projections call for one more increase this year, then a hold at 4.1% through 2027.
Run the couple's mix through 2022, price change plus the distributions each fund paid that year. The stock fund lost 18.2%, so $1,080,000 became $883,971, a drop of $196,029. The bond fund lost 12.9% as yields rose, so $540,000 became $470,080, a drop of $69,920. The money market fund earned about what three-month bills paid that year, 2.09%, and grew to $183,754. The portfolio went from $1,800,000 to $1,537,804, a loss of $262,196, or 14.6%, before a dollar was withdrawn. Call it $260,000.
The bond side is the part you may not expect. In 2008 and 2020 bonds rose while stocks fell, because the Fed was cutting. When the Fed raises into a decline, both sides fall at once, and your 60/40 mix gives up most of its cushion. On the smaller balance the same $64,800 withdrawal becomes 4.2% of the portfolio; holding to 3.6% would mean $55,361, a cut of $9,439 a year.

The fund yields 4.98%, which is $26,892 a year of income, so that hit is covered in about six months.
The money market fund is the one place a rate increase helps you: a quarter point on $180,000 is $450 a year, on top of the 4.17% short rates paid on November 5.

Gold attracts investment as a store of value and safe haven
Throughout human history, Gold has served extensively as both a store of value and a means of exchange, giving it an important role. Gold responds to geopolitical risks, interest rates and the Dollar
