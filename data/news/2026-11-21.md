The attacks could complicate U.S.-led efforts to keep oil flowing.
Reported attacks in and around the Strait of Hormuz have been higher in number in October and November than any previous months since the war between the U.S., Israel and Iran began in late February , according to data compiled by maritime organizations.
The attacks appear to be a bid to complicate U.S.-led efforts to keep oil flowing through the economically crucial Strait of Hormuz despite an Iranian-declared blockade, experts told ABC News.
The United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations Centre (UKMTO) reported 31 attacks in and around the Strait of Hormuz in October, and about 19 in November, as of Nov. 18.

If you own State Street SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust ( NYSEARCA:SPY ) or Invesco QQQ Trust ( NASDAQ:QQQ ), the selloff raises a question about the Fed. In September, it raised its target range to an upper bound of 4.00% [ 1 ] , and it still looks ready to boost again.
Peter Boockvar, chief investment officer of OnePoint BFG Wealth Partners, posted on X Friday morning about the AI tech selloff and the drop in industrial metals, oil, and fuel prices that followed. He said he is confident Fed Chair Kevin Warsh is paying attention, but that some of Warsh's colleagues are "not reading the room." He wondered about a December hike only if the selloff continues, and that condition matters for how investors read the weeks before the meeting.
Boockvar's argument depends on how much of the economy is tied to AI. Boockvar estimates that data center spending accounts for about half of U.S. GDP growth.
Boockvar also estimates that AI makes up about half of the S&P 500's market value, and he puts AI's share of S&P 500 earnings growth at about 75%. If his estimates are close, a drop in AI stocks hits the economy in two ways: companies cut data center budgets, and households feel poorer. He also said upper-income consumers depend on the stock market's wealth effect. When their portfolios contract, richer households spend less. That slows down growth and inflation without any action from the Fed, and cheaper metals and fuel push inflation lower on top of that.
The 2-year Treasury yield follows Fed expectations more closely than any other. It closed Friday at 4.63%, down from 4.88% a week earlier. That 25 basis point weekly drop included 13 basis points on Friday alone, and it matched the fall in the week ended September 6, 2024.
The 10-year yield fell to 4.92% from 5.15%. CME FedWatch still showed about 92% odds of a quarter-point December hike. A falling 2-year yield next to steady December odds means traders still expect a December hike but are pricing in fewer hikes after it.
A Citi note on Friday said markets are signaling that current rates are unsustainably high as stock valuations fall and the risk of slower AI investment grows. The bank still expects a quarter-point hike in December because the Fed wants to protect its inflation credibility. After that, it expects the Fed to hold and to start cutting later in 2027, which matches the path it described on October 14.

QQQ is now up just 0.58% for the year, and SPY is up 2.26%. The VIX closed Thursday at 28.44 [ 3 ] , close to 30, which usually signals high fear.
SPY spreads its holdings across more sectors than QQQ, so its holders keep some AI exposure without the AI trade deciding all of their returns. QQQ would likely rebound harder than SPY if the Fed stops after December, because lower yields help growth stocks the most, but if concerns about AI spending keep building, QQQ takes more damage, as its larger one-month loss shows. Short-term Treasuries also gain directly from a pause, because their prices rise as yields fall.

Liana Fix leads the Securing Ukraine’s Future Initiative at the Council on Foreign Relations. She is the author of Germany Rearmed: The Return of War and the End of Illusions .
As U.S. President Donald Trump, Russian President Vladimir Putin, and Chinese President Xi Jinping met this week for trilateral talks in Shenzhen, China, more than five thousand miles away, the so-called Coalition of the Willing was gathering in Berlin to discuss Ukraine’s future.
The symbolism was impossible to miss. While President Trump engaged with the aggressor and a key enabler of the war in Ukraine, more than thirty heads of state and government, including Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, as well as U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, were putting on a strong show of unity that underscored European leaders’ determination to secure, in the words of NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte, “a just and lasting peace for Ukraine.”
Yet European leaders pushing for a seat at the table in talks about Ukraine’s future should be careful what they wish for. They could easily walk into a trap.
As Ukraine’s strongest supporter, it makes sense for Europe to have a direct role in negotiations about the country’s future. Europeans are hoping their presence will thwart efforts to push Ukraine into agreeing to a disadvantageous deal similar to the 28-point peace plan discussed by U.S. negotiators last year. But Europe’s leaders are also mindful that negotiations are not only about Ukraine’s future—Europe’s future security is increasingly bound up in the discussions.
The United States has accepted the Russian view that negotiations on Ukraine need to involve talks about broader European security. In earlier rounds, such talks were made contingent on progress toward a ceasefire. But Washington has now explicitly pushed for Europeans, ideally after agreeing on a single representative, to make progress around a European security element of the deal.
Having a single representative for Europe does not seem viable, and European leaders have instead agreed that national security advisors from France, Germany, Italy, Poland, the United Kingdom, and EU institutions will represent European interests at the negotiating table, if peace talks open up to Europe.
One of the problems is that Russia has so far not confirmed that it is open to a direct European presence at the negotiations. On the contrary: Putin has essentially ridiculed Europeans by inviting them to Moscow to “clarify their position,” after which Russia “will decide in what format to continue the dialogue with Ukraine.”
While it is unacceptable to the Europeans to be summoned to Moscow in this way, and although they do not expect Putin to alter his position in negotiations, most European leaders have accepted the need to talk to Russia directly. But there are downsides and risks to direct talks.
Speaking after his meeting with Trump and Xi, Putin said that “discussing security issues, including security in Europe, without Europeans is pointless.” The danger for the Europeans is that both Russia and the United States, the latter by bringing Europeans in, will try to place the responsibility for solving Russia’s security “concerns” on European leaders in the hopes that it provides a shortcut to a ceasefire deal that would allow President Trump to present himself as a historic peacemaker. The U.S. side has little interest in the actual details of what this would mean for Europe, leaving European leaders pressured to deliver something that is impossible for them to agree to: a diminishment of European security.
What exactly Russia means when it talks about “European security” and the “root causes of the war” becomes clear both from past Russian positions and from a paper reportedly circulated by Russian officials last week that reiterates some of the hardline positions Russia outlined in December 2021 (and adds some new ones). These include:
Rolling back NATO’s military infrastructure to 1997 levels. This would compromise NATO protection for Poland and the Baltic states, making these countries indefensible buffer zones.
Stopping any military support for Ukraine. This would lead to Ukraine’s defeat sooner rather than later.
Stopping France and the United Kingdom from extending nuclear guarantees to Europeans. This comes at a time of ambiguity about U.S. commitments to Europe.
In principle, there is nothing wrong with talking about European security with Russia, if it leads to greater stability and possibly arms control measures in Europe. However, this is not what Russia wants. Putin is grieving the loss of Soviet and Russian influence over Central Europe, and looking for a redo, hoping that he can buy time to break Ukraine’s resistance over the winter. Despite nearly five years of unsuccessful fighting involving heavy casualties , Russia continues to put forward maximalist positions.
To avoid walking into a trap, Europeans should work closely with the U.S. side and explain to it why Russian demands on European security are not in the United States’ interests and why pressuring Europeans into accepting these demands would significantly undermine U.S. influence in Europe.
There is a path forward for constructive discussions of European security, and it starts by looking to the response to Putin’s December 2021 demands. Back then, in a last-minute effort to engage earnestly with Russia and try to prevent the invasion, U.S., European and NATO officials worked together and delivered a response to Russia’s ultimatum in January 2022. That response outlined where the United States and Europe could see areas of flexibility to discuss European security. The U.S. and European negotiating position included, for example, proposing reciprocal negotiations on arms control and military transparency.
This was good work, which should be the basis for what Europe and the United States put forward now. They should then wait for Russia’s response to gauge whether Putin is serious about negotiations. If it turns out he is not, then his maximalist aims and refusal to show any flexibility will mean it is Putin who has set himself up for eventual failure.

Europe is entering the 2026â27 winter with unusually low natural gas inventories. At the end of September 2026, gas storage in the EU was about 72 percent full, the lowest fill rate for that month since EU-wide data are available (from 2011) and about 11 percentage points below last September. Against this backdrop, this note considers under what conditions Europe could face gas shortages during the 2026â27 winter. Following the analysis in Rua (2023) of Europe's energy position ahead of the 2023â24 winter, this note projects EU gas inventories through June 2027, under a Baseline and two adverse scenarios. The projections suggest that Europe can get through an average winter without running out of gas, and through a harsh winter too, albeit with stocks lower than it has tolerated in the past. However, a notable decline in LNG imports would leave storage nearly empty by the spring.
Projecting inventories requires assumptions about inflows from abroad, domestic production, and consumption, and all three now look very different from before Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The note therefore first documents how these flows have changed since 2021, using monthly data on the EU's imports by country of origin and on gas consumption, and drawing on companion work on the structural decline in gas demand. 4
Inflows: Norway and LNG have replaced Russian pipeline gas
Russian gas has largely disappeared from Europe's supply. Imports from Russia fell from about 158 billion cubic meters (bcm) in 2021 to 40 bcm in 2025, or from 44 percent to 13 percent of the EU's imports from outside the EU (Figure 2). 5 Since the transit contract through Ukraine expired at the end of 2024, Russian pipeline gas has reached the EU only through TurkStream (Sharples, 2025), and more than half of the remaining Russian gas arrives as LNG, which the EU has banned under short-term contracts since April 2026 and will ban under the remaining long-term contracts from January 2027. 6 Norway has replaced Russia as the largest supplier of natural gas to the EU while production within the EU has fallen by 13 bcm between 2021 and 2025, mainly because the Netherlands wound down and closed the Groningen field.

LNG has filled about half of the gap left by Russian gas. LNG imports nearly doubled, from 80 bcm in 2021 to 146 bcm in 2025, when they accounted for 46 percent of imports. About three quarters of that additional LNG came from the United States. Perhaps surprisingly, despite the disruptions to global energy markets that began in February 2026, imports from outside the EU were only 1 percent lower than a year earlier during the first seven months of 2026, and LNG accounted for 45 percent of the EU's total gas imports, similar to its share for all of last year.
The reason for this resilience is that, while about one-fifth of the world's LNG passes through the Strait of Hormuz, most of it is bound for Asia and Qatar supplied only about 9 percent of the EU's LNG in 2025 and about 6 percent in the first seven months of 2026. In comparison, the United States supplied about 50 percent in 2025 and in the first seven months of 2026. 7
Its new reliance on LNG makes Europe's gas supply more exposed to global market conditions. For example, as Asian buyers lose Qatari supply, they compete with Europe for cargoes from the Atlantic basin. To explore the consequences of Europe's increased exposure to the global LNG market, the Import Scarcity scenario in the analysis below focuses on the risk to the European gas supply due to LNG import reductions.
On the demand side, EU gas consumption fell from 413 bcm in 2021 to 332 bcm in 2024, a decline of about 20 percent (Figure 3). All end users cut back: households, industry, power plants and services. Consumption edged up slightly in 2025, mainly because power plants burned more gas. In the first seven months of 2026, Europe consumed about 1 percent more than a year earlier but still 19 percent less than during the same months of 2021.

Inventories this winter: a baseline and two scenarios
These insights shape the projections of EU gas inventories from October 2026 through June 2027 (Figure 5), as they require basing expected consumption on the winters since the invasion. Stocks are rolled forward month by month, accounting for domestic production, net imports and consumption, starting from a stock of about 80 bcm at the end of September 2026. The first scenario ("Baseline") assumes that domestic production, imports from Russia and net imports from all other countries in each month equal their level in the same month a year earlier, and that consumption equals its average for that month over the four winters since the invasion (2022â23 to 2025â26). The second scenario ("Harsh Winter") keeps the Baseline's supply assumptions but sets consumption in each month to its maximum over those winters. Thus, even though the past four winters were relatively mild in historical comparison, this scenario still provides a meaningful representation of gas consumption under extreme weather conditions, such as the exceptionally cold winter of 2012â13, which had 14 percent more heating degree days than normal.
The third scenario ("Import Scarcity") assumes that Europe can only secure smaller LNG supplies from the global market: pipeline imports, exports and consumption are as in the Baseline, but LNG imports in each month are reduced by one-third from their level a year earlier. This is a pessimistic scenario as it assumes that Europe would have to proportionately share the decline in LNG supply due to a complete closure of the Strait of Hormuz (one-fifth of global LNG supply) and would also receive fewer deliveries from its established LNG providers, such as the United States and Algeria. 9

In the Baseline scenario, inventories enter the winter well below their recent average. Storage peaks at about 84 bcm in October 2026, compared with about 99 bcm on the path implied by the 2022â25 average fill rates. Inventories fall to about 32 bcm at the end of March 2027, or 30 percent of storage capacity, before refilling begins. That is below the troughs of the past two winters but above the 20 percent of capacity to which EU countries have historically allowed stocks to fall (Rua, 2023). 10
In the Harsh Winter scenario, stocks fall to about 16 bcm at the end of March 2027, or 15 percent of capacity, and stay below the 20 percent mark from February to April. In the Import Scarcity scenario, stocks fall to about 6 percent of capacity in April 2027, far below any level seen since 2011, and they remain below the 20 percent mark through June, so that refilling would start from almost zero. In all three scenarios, refilling for the 2027â28 winter would start from a lower level than this year, requiring stronger inflows than in previous years.
How much LNG could Europe lose and still get through the winter? Variants of the Import Scarcity scenario cut LNG imports to other shares of last year's level, which was about 77 bcm over October to March. With LNG at 80 percent of that level, stocks bottom out close to the Harsh Winter path. At 70 percent, they fall to about 9 bcm. At 60 percent, storage is all but empty in April 2027. In other words, a loss of about one-fifth of last year's LNG imports leaves filling levels close to the Harsh Winter path and near the critical 20 percent filling mark; beyond that, each further tenth of lost LNG imports takes roughly another 8 bcm off the trough, so that at a one-third loss storage is nearly empty and at 40 percent it is all but exhausted.
A very strong El Ni&ntilde;o is under way. The Oceanic Ni&ntilde;o Index of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) reached +2.16 for July to September 2026, the highest value for that season since records began in 1950 on the traditional index, and NOAA puts the chance that the event becomes the strongest since 1950 at 83 percent for October to December. 11 El Ni&ntilde;o affects European winters mainly through the North Atlantic. According to the literature, moderate El Ni&ntilde;o events tend to bring cold late-winter weather to northern Europe, partly because El Ni&ntilde;o makes sudden warmings of the stratosphere, which weaken the polar vortex, more likely (Ineson and Scaife, 2009). The response reverses as events become stronger, with high pressure over the eastern Atlantic and western Europe (Toniazzo and Scaife, 2006; Ineson and others, 2026).
