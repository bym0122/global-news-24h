Iran Steps Up Hormuz Tanker Attacks as Oil Prices Slide
Iran has intensified attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz in recent weeks, just as crude oil flows from the Persian Gulf recover and oil prices fall amid expectations of oversupply within months.
The number of attacks soared in October to the highest level since the Iran war began at the end of February, and has continued at nearly daily occurrences so far in November, according to data compiled by Bloomberg .
Meanwhile, crude oil volumes flowing from the Strait of Hormuz have declined in recent days, although it's unclear if this is entirely due to the more frequent attacks on tankers.
"Security risks continue to weigh on tanker operations in the Gulf," Maria Bertzeletou, Senior Market Analyst at Signal Group, wrote in an analysis this week.
"Repeated attacks continue to restrict the pool of owners willing to accept Gulf employment, while elevated war-risk insurance costs and operational delays add to voyage expenses and limit effective vessel availability."
So far this month, Iran has been reporting nearly daily incidents of tankers hitting naval mines in corridors that Iran has designated as "unauthorized" in the Strait of Hormuz.
Non-Iranian observers and organizations also report more frequent attacks on tankers in and near the Strait of Hormuz.
Five tankers were struck near Hormuz in the week between November 11 and 18, according to remote-sensing intelligence by Windward .
"This highlights Iran's continued strategy of maritime attrition, and it is unlikely to ease unless diplomatic conditions change," the maritime intelligence firm said on Wednesday.
ADNOC Logistics & Services, a unit of Abu Dhabi's national oil company ADNOC, confirmed that one of its vessels was attacked while transiting the Strait of Hormuz on the evening of November 17.
The number of attacks could be even higher, as not all incidents are being reported to the UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) and the International Maritime Organization (IMO).

Anthropic&#x27;s shares have dropped back towards their initial price after a soaring first day on the stock market, as a wider tech sell-off dragged markets down.
The sell-off comes as concerns remain over the speed and size of artificial intelligence investment, but also as some large companies including Nvidia and SpaceX faced specific issues that worried investors.
Those pressures combined to weigh on US markets through Friday, with all key US indexes closing sharply down on the day, furthering earlier falls over the week.
The S&P 500 and the Dow Jones Industrial Average were pushed into correction territory, a point where they are trading at 10% or more down from a recent high.
The S&P 500 closed down 3.7%, the Dow Jones Industrial Average was down 1.9% and the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite Index plunged 5.3%. However, all three indexes were up on where they were a year ago.
The speed at which AI investment has been growing - with more than $1 trillion spent in the last year alone by a small handful of major tech companies - has led to ongoing concerns of a financial bubble. Questions are being raised over whether those investments will pay off, and when.
Investors were also spooked after the Democrats&#x27; resounding win in the US midterms in early November, which sparked fears of a reduction in AI development.
Despite a positive first day of trading, Anthropic&#x27;s shares fell on Friday, dropping at one point by more than 12% to about $85, close to its initial public offering (IPO) price of $83. It closed down 8.5%, reversing half of the gains made on Thursday.
The artificial intelligence (AI) company&#x27;s introduction to the Nasdaq stock exchange on Thursday was a crucial test for the industry and was largely seen as a success, with trading starting at $111.50 before easing to end the day up 17% at $97.17.
Analysts said volatility was to be expected in the first days or even weeks of Anthropic&#x27;s trading, as the company was the first frontier AI firm to go public so had no direct peers.
Some have also noted the similarities with Elon Musk&#x27;s SpaceX, which at the time of its debut in June was the largest IPO on record. Shares in the rocket and AI company experienced large swings in its first few weeks of trading.

Europe&#x27;s main stock markets - which contain fewer tech stocks - also took a hit.

Earlier on Friday, tech-heavy Asian markets saw steep falls.

Warsh wants Fed officials to talk less. Instead, they’re talking more
Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh wants central bankers to talk less about where they expect interest rates to go. Instead, they&#x27;re talking more.
Bank of America says there has been more so-called Fedspeak over the past two weeks than in any similar period going back to the beginning of 2025.
The conflicting communications strategies come at a critical time for monetary policy and the economy.
In his first six months as Federal Reserve chairman, Kevin Warsh has diligently avoided signaling where he sees monetary policy and interest rates headed. His colleagues have been only too happy to fill the void.
Fed officials have been rushing to the microphones lately, voicing sometimes conflicting opinions on how the central bank should be handling an inflation bug that the economy can&#x27;t seem to shake.
Most agree that rates should be raised — and soon. Some are willing to hike at a measured pace as conditions play out, while others, such as Governor Christopher Waller and St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem , entertain the thought of more aggressive hikes in a shorter period of time.
Largely on the sidelines in the debate is Warsh, who has expressed disdain for that sort of forward guidance and argued that it handcuffs policymakers long before they actually have to make a decision. But that hasn&#x27;t stopped most of the other 18 officials who sit in on Federal Open Market Committee meetings.
In fact, according to a Bank of America analysis, there has been more so-called Fedspeak in the past two weeks than in any similar period since the start of 2025.

The increasingly vocal debate comes at a critical time for the Fed and has myriad policy implications.

The International Energy Agency said in its November report that global supply rose by 2.3 million barrels per day in October to 105.4 million barrels per day. The agency expects the global oil market to return to a surplus in the fourth quarter, with a larger surplus possible in 2027.
That forecast gave sellers a reason to press the market. It also marked a change from the earlier part of the conflict, when the immediate concern was how much supply would be lost and whether buyers could replace it.
The Energy Information Administration reported that oil shipments through major Middle East chokepoints increased 19% in the third quarter. The agency said companies have used smaller vessels and ship-to-ship transfers, while producers have made greater use of infrastructure that bypasses higher-risk areas.
The recovery does not mean the region is operating normally. It does suggest producers and shippers have found ways to move more oil despite the danger. As long as those barrels keep reaching customers, traders may be reluctant to put a large risk premium back into prices.
Earlier in the week, ADNOC L&S said a crew member was missing, and others were injured after one of its vessels was attacked in the Strait of Hormuz. Iran also said it attacked U.S. drone and missile sites in Kuwait following a U.S. strike on Qeshm Island.
On Thursday, Iranian state media reported that two oil tankers had struck naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz. Reuters said it could not independently verify the report.
Attacks can still slow the selling or produce a short-lived rally, but this week’s price action suggests traders want evidence of a lasting reduction in shipments before they pay up for crude. That could change quickly if an incident blocks traffic or damages a major export facility.
The U.S. government’s weekly inventory report gave sellers another reason to focus on supply. Commercial crude oil inventories rose by 3.4 million barrels to 417.8 million barrels in the week ended November 13, according to the EIA. Gasoline inventories increased by 5.2 million barrels, while distillate stocks rose by 1.6 million barrels.
The crude build was much larger than the increase of 58,000 barrels expected by analysts in a Reuters poll. Gasoline stocks also rose more than forecast.
One report does not establish a lasting surplus, but the increases in crude and fuel inventories supported the argument that supply is becoming less constrained. For traders already considering the IEA’s forecast, the report provided fresh evidence of barrels building in storage.
WTI has also been weaker than Brent. Earlier this week, Sparta Commodities analyst June Goh said rising U.S. Gulf Coast inventories were contributing to a wider gap between the benchmarks. A lower WTI price relative to Brent can help make U.S. crude more competitive for buyers in Asia and Northwest Europe.
Oil traders are also dealing with the prospect of higher U.S. interest rates. The concern is that tighter policy could slow economic activity and reduce fuel demand just as more crude becomes available. A stronger U.S. dollar can add pressure by making dollar-denominated oil more expensive for buyers using other currencies.
