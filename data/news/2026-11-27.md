Iran Says LNG Carrier Exploded After Hitting a Mine in Hormuz
An LNG vessel has caught fire and exploded after hitting a naval mine in the Strait of Hormuz during an attempt to transit on a route deemed "illegal" by Iran, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) Navy said in the latest communication of a tanker incident in the chokepoint.
The LNG carrier had switched off its positioning systems and attempted to use "an unauthorized U.S.-Israeli-designated route," the IRGC Navy's Public Relations Office said, as carried by Iranian media early on Thursday.
The IRGC Navy's Strait of Hormuz Traffic Control Center warned that "vessels violating Iran's regulations in the strait and, under what it described as deception by the US military, choosing unauthorized and illegal passage routes risk striking sea mines."
On Wednesday, the IRGC Navy said that two oil tankers exploded after hitting naval mines while attempting to transit the Strait of Hormuz on routes unauthorized by Iran.
Two tankers hit naval mines off the southern and eastern coasts of the Strait of Hormuz early on Wednesday, resulting in fires erupting in their engine rooms, the IRGC said in a statement carried by Iranian news agency IRNA.
These Iranian reports about tankers that Tehran says are 'violating' the Iranian terms of navigation through the chokepoint have become a nearly daily occurrence in the past few weeks.
Earlier this week, Iran said three other tankers caught fire after hitting naval mines after violating regulations and instructions from the IRGC Navy Traffic Control Center.
Iran has intensified attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz in recent weeks, just as crude oil flows from the Persian Gulf recover and oil prices fall amid expectations of oversupply within months.
Meanwhile, crude oil prices bounced back in early Asian trade on Thursday, climbing higher after fresh reports of tankers hitting mines in the Strait of Hormuz and the latest Yemeni Houthi attack on Saudi Arabia, which targeted a refinery.

Tanker Traffic at Hormuz Ticks Up Despite Intensified Attacks
The number of tankers carrying energy products through the Strait of Hormuz has increased in November despite the fact that the number of attacks and incidents with mines has hit the highest level since the beginning of the Iran war.
Between November 1 and 19, the seven-day moving average count showed more than 26 crude oil, fuel, and LNG tankers transiting the Strait each day, Bloomberg reported on Thursday, citing ship-tracking data by Vortexa. To compare, the seven-day moving average was below 25 vessels on at least two-thirds of the days in October.
The vessel numbers come with two caveats. First, the above-mentioned numbers may not be all tankers that have transited or attempted to transit the Strait of Hormuz, due to signal interference and en masse switching off of the transponders on the ships.
The other is that there has been a slight reduction in the number of very large crude carriers (VLCCs) and Suezmaxes moving through the chokepoint, but the number of smaller tankers, Aframaxes, has increased, according to Vortexa's data.
The change in the tanker fleet operating in and out of the Strait of Hormuz makes it even more difficult for analysts to estimate how much crude oil and fuel are being evacuated from the Persian Gulf and exit the Strait en route to international markets.
Smaller oil tankers moved in October the most volumes of crude oil out of the Strait of Hormuz since the war began , as Iran targets supertankers and incidents with naval mines and unknown projectiles hit a wartime high.
Moreover, a record number of the so-called long-range-2 (LR2) clean tankers that have transported fuels such as diesel and gasoline have become the so-called dirty tankers and started shipping crude.
The LR2 tankers are similar in capacity to the Aframax crude tankers, which can ship up to 800,000 barrels of crude each.
A clean-turned-dirty LR2 tanker could be employed on the complex trade shuffles near the Strait of Hormuz, where shuttle-shipments have become the prevailing mode of moving crude oil out of the Persian Gulf in recent months.

Turkmenistan Nears Major Milestone in $10 Billion TAPI Gas Pipeline
Turkmenistan expects a key section of a pipeline to begin supplying gas to Afghanistan's third-largest city in early 2027, despite years of delays and hurdles in the $10-billion Turkmenistan&ndash;Afghanistan&ndash;Pakistan&ndash;India (TAPI) project, Turkmen officials have told Reuters .
Turkmenistan is estimated to be the world's fifth-largest holder of natural gas reserves, after Russia, Iran, Qatar, and the United States. The Central Asian country holds an estimated 5.3% of global gas reserves, with 146 years of production at current rates.
At the Turkmen Energy Forum last month, Turkmenistan said that the Serhetabat&ndash;Herat section of the TAPI pipeline had reached the outskirts of Herat, in Afghanistan, with commissioning on track by the end of this year.
Downstream projects identified for Herat include a pipeline to the Herat Industrial Park, gas distribution stations, industrial zone gasification, and a compressed natural gas complex. A private investor has also proposed a gas-fired power plant near the city, Turkmenistan said in October.
However, the Serhetabat&ndash;Herat section is just around 150 kilometers (93 miles) long, or less than a tenth of the planned 1,800-km (1,118-mile) TAPI pipeline.
The TAPI project has faced political and financing hurdles in recent years. The return of the Taliban to power in Afghanistan in the early 2020s and the historical hostility and distrust between Pakistan and India have been major risks to the execution and international funding for the project, estimated to cost $10 billion.
Turkmen officials are optimistic that the imminent startup of the first leg of the pipeline between Serhetabat, a Turkmen city on the border with Afghanistan, and Afghanistan's third-biggest city, Herat, could raise confidence among international investors and creditors.
The section to Herat is expected to "provide early revenue streams and increase confidence among lenders," Muhammetmyrat Amanov, head of the Turkmen state company responsible for the pipeline project, told Reuters.

The UK will pay a "participation fee" to the EU as part of the deals agreed by Andy Burnham, according to legal documents.
The surcharge will go towards the running costs of EU agencies and systems required to deliver two of the agreements unveiled by the prime minister last week.
It will be paid in addition to a contribution towards costs that broadly mirrors how EU members contribute to the bloc&#x27;s shared budget, the documents show.
The government has sought to contrast the financial costs of the deals against their overall effect on the economy, which it estimates will receive an eventual boost by billions a year due to increased trade.
The government is yet to publish an estimate of overall additional costs, but officials said they expected them to be in the low tens of millions per year and "massively outweighed" by predicted economic gains.
British ministers had already said they were prepared to pay towards administrative costs required to implement deals to re-link the UK to the EU&#x27;s carbon trading regime, and align Britain with the EU&#x27;s food standards.
Now, details of how the payments will be calculated have been revealed in treaties to deliver the two agreements, which are meant to take effect during 2028.
Legal text for a third agreement agreed last week, on youth visas for 18 to 31 year olds, is expected to be published in the coming days.
According to the treaties, the payments will be made as a share of the running costs of three EU agencies, as well as various systems that will be used to implement the food standards and emissions deals.
The bulk of the payments, to be made in euros, will come in the form of an "operational contribution" in proportion to the size of the British economy compared to that of the EU.
This broadly mirrors the way in which EU member states split the cost of their main contributions towards the bloc&#x27;s overall budget.
But in addition, the UK will also pay a "participation fee" set at 4% of its main contribution figure.
The government has pointed to the 2021 Brexit trade agreement negotiated by the Conservatives, which also applied an additional surcharge to UK contributions towards the cost of relevant EU programmes.
However, any additional contributions are likely to be seized upon by opponents of the latest deals.
Ministers had declined to enter into specifics of the fees during negotiations on the treaties. On Sunday, Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband said the latest deals agreed by Burnham were "not about cost to the taxpayer".
It comes as the government published separate legislation granting it broad powers to incorporate new EU laws required to implement the agreements, without having to hold a full vote each time.
The food standards deal in particular will mean Britain will have to align with scores of current EU food-related laws that have been updated since Brexit, along with future rules in these areas.
The government argues the new powers are required because it would be impractical to run a full parliamentary process each time UK legislation needs to be updated to make the agreement work.
But it has prompted criticism from opposition parties, because the UK no longer has voting rights at the EU to shape European legislation.
The government argues the UK will get a say on new rules, with the treaty texts confirming British experts can be consulted when new EU laws are being drawn up, with the UK granted "observer" status in certain meetings.
But Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch said Burnham was showing "disdain for the democratic process" by asking for a "blank cheque to install and enact EU law".
"The prime minister is telling MPs to hand him extraordinary powers whilst having no idea what the EU will actually agree to," she added.
Reform UK leader Nigel Farage has argued the Burnham&#x27;s deals would take Britain back into "much of the European Union&#x27;s single market".
He added: "European economies are not growing as fast as ours. The idea that getting closer to the EU will mean growth for us is a myth."
