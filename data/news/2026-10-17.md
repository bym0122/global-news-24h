The holder of debt can buy credit default swaps to protect against any losses resulting from issuer default. Like insurance, the amount paid is tied to the value and quality of the insured asset. To be sure, yields on a debt instrument are tied to the risk of default — when the risk goes up, so does the interest rate. Other factors also matter when it comes to the yield investors demand, such as inflation expectations, liquidity premiums, and the risk-free rate — usually measured by the 10-year Treasury yield , currently nearly 5.1%. A credit default swap, however, only reflects credit risk and none of those other aforementioned sentiment factors. As a result, a CDS can provide a more transparent picture of how investors actually feel about the debt issuer itself. For example, if an investor owns $1 million in debt issued by XYZ Corporation and wants to insure it, they could purchase a CDS. TSMC , the world&#x27;s largest and most advanced contract chipmaker, manufactures Nvidia&#x27;s chips. Why is ASML the "real" bottleneck in the AI chip market?
ASML, which is based in the Netherlands, is the world&#x27;s largest producer of lithography systems. In the day-to-day of the Iran war, it’s natural to feel a touch of whiplash. Are we on the verge of agreement or at the dawn of escalation? Is Iran on the edge of collapse, or is it expanding the conflict? Will the Strait of Hormuz return to the status quo ante, or will tolling and periodic attacks on shipping be the new normal? These are important issues but there are larger, more fundamental strategic shifts underway as well. Not since 9/11 and the U.S. invasion of Iraq has the Middle East been in this degree of strategic flux.
It’s been nearly eight months since the war started, two hundred and thirty days to be precise, and, checking the tactical as well as the strategic report card, the record is decidedly mixed.
At the start of the conflict, the Trump administration sought to lay out its objectives. As Undersecretary of War for Policy Elbridge Colby put it at an event at the Council within a week of the war’s start: “The president has directed the United States Armed Forces to conduct a military campaign with a focus on degrading and destroying the Islamic Republic of Iran’s ability to project military power in the region, and potentially beyond.” Secretary of State Marco Rubio specified the objectives in even greater detail: “Number one, the destruction of their air force. Number two, the destruction of their navy. Number three, the severe diminishing of their missile launching capability. And number four, the destruction of their factories so they can’t make more missiles and more drones to threaten us in the future.”
On the basis of these goals, the war has achieved much of what it set out to do. By any measure, Iran has fewer military capabilities and a more strained economy than it had before. Iran’s navy sits at the bottom of the ocean, as the Trump administration likes to say, and its missile capabilities have been significantly degraded.
These results, of course, have not come without cost. Tragically, the lives of twenty-one U.S. service members have been lost, as well as over a thousand Iranian civilians. Additionally, the United States has spent down important munitions needed for its other key strategic commitments and priorities. By depleting Patriot interceptors and THAAD batteries, the United States faces increased challenges to maintain its force posture elsewhere around the world including, crucially, in the Indo-Pacific.
More broadly, though, if the goal of Operation Epic Fury was to prevent Iran from projecting power beyond its borders, it might well have had the opposite effect.
The most significant outcome of the war may well prove to be the power of asymmetry. Tehran knew when the war began that it lacked the direct firepower to defeat the United States, but it discovered that, with relatively few missiles, drones, and mines, it could turn its greatest asset—control over the Strait of Hormuz—into a powerful economic weapon that allows it to project power, at least for now, across the global economy.
The resulting repercussions on energy prices, inflation, and affordability in the United States led U.S. President Donald Trump to strike an agreement last week with Russian President Vladimir Putin to allow the sale of millions of barrels of Russian diesel. That sale, protested by Europeans and Ukrainians alike, is pouring more money into the coffers of the Russian war machine and has opened a U.S. rift with Europe and Ukraine, further complicating any path to ending that destructive and intractable war.
The long arm of Iran is also being felt in the country fields of England. Amid fears of a possible state-sponsored terrorist attack on the RAF Fairford base, the United States removed its long-range B–1 bombers, which played a key role in the war in Iran. Iran had previously attempted to reach out beyond its borders to assassinate dissidents as well as foreign officials involved in the 2020 attack on Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps General Qasem Soleimani, but a potential attack on a military base of a NATO ally may be one of the most striking examples of Iran’s sense of confidence despite the hits it has taken since February.

Just on Saturday, Houthi attacks on the international airport in Riyadh killed more than a dozen people and injured over three hundred more. To think that the poorly funded but scrappy Houthis are able to upset a multi-trillion-dollar, multi-decade strategy of one of the richest countries in the world is another example of the might of asymmetry. But it is the potential impact of this conflict on the United States’s regional strategy that could loom much larger.
For a long time, Iran has wanted to push the United States out of the region. Over the years, though, the United States increased its presence across thirteen bases in partner countries, including Bahrain, Kuwait, and Qatar, in order to deter aggression, promote stability, and preposition assets for conflicts as they arise.
As CFR’s Steven Cook has noted, Iran has turned the logic of those bases on its head. Partner countries initially sought U.S. bases as a source of security; now they are targets because of them. They assumed that the United States would actively defend them should Iran or its proxies attack; now that is open to question. After Iranian strikes damaged these bases, the United States withdrew certain forces, even as it deployed more mobile capabilities as part of a third carrier strike group and an additional Marine Expeditionary Unit. If and when the dust of this conflict settles, will partner countries want to rebuild those bases and have U.S. forces return to them? The long and drawn-out process of winding down the costly wars the United States has fought in the region for more than three decades will almost certainly be accelerated by this one.
And who or what would take the United States’ place? A drawdown of U.S. forces in the region would likely set off a frantic realignment of Middle Eastern politics and alliance networks. Without the U.S. security umbrella that so many of our regional partners thought they could depend on, they may turn to others, and each other, for that security. In the best case, that looks like a Middle Eastern NATO: an alliance or perhaps a lattice of alliances of like-minded neighbors committed to their collective security.
The recent Mecca Joint Defense Agreement between Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey transforming into a more operational grouping is one possibility; another is the Abraham Accords, which tied Bahrain, Israel, Morocco, and the United Arab Emirates (UAE) closer together. And how will those countries seek to guarantee their security? Potentially through the pursuit of nuclear weapons. Given that the purpose of the initial strikes against Iran was to prevent nuclear proliferation in this region, it’s ironic that we may end up seeing additional countries in the region, including Saudi Arabia and the UAE, ultimately pursuing nuclear weapons capabilities.
And what is to come for Iran in this brave new world? Tehran today is weaker than it’s ever been and stronger than it almost certainly expected to be—both at the same time. How do you negotiate with a regime that is keenly aware of that? There is reporting that the United States might be planning for an escalation of attacks and that the Iranian regime is increasingly fragile. That said, the regime has proven more resilient than many expected, as has the Iranian economy, or at least the capacity of the Iranian people to absorb economic pain. This regime could well be a long-term participant in the future of Middle Eastern geopolitics, one that we might all have to learn to live with.

Intel and AMD Drop 3% as Rotation Out of AI Chip Stocks Continues; NVIDIA Holds Steady
Investors are bailing on AI chip stocks, but not all of them equally, and the gap between winners and losers reveals something important about how Wall Street is rethinking the cost of building out artificial intelligence infrastructure.
Published October 16, 2026, 10:54am ET · 3 min read
A rotation out of AI-linked chip stocks isn't letting up, and two of the biggest names in processors are falling again. Intel ( NASDAQ:INTC | INTC Price Prediction ) stock is down 3% to $92.60 in morning trading. Meanwhile, Advanced Micro Devices ( NASDAQ:AMD ) stock is down 3% to $544.74, matching the decline in Intel shares.
Chip stocks are falling harder than the rest of large-cap tech. The iShares Semiconductor ETF ( NASDAQ:SOXX ) is down 2%, a smaller drop than Intel and AMD shares. By comparison, the Invesco QQQ Trust ( NASDAQ:QQQ ) is down 0.9%, a far softer slide than the chip fund.
NVIDIA ( NASDAQ:NVDA ) is another AI chip designer and the leader for the group. Its Blackwell Ultra systems and the newly ramping Vera Rubin platform power a Data Center business that sells to many of the same cloud providers Intel and AMD serve. NVIDIA shares are up 0.4% to $218.10, so not every chip stock is down today.
The decline extends a rotation out of AI-linked chip stocks as investors question how AI infrastructure spending gets financed . Server processors from Intel and AMD feed that same expansion, which puts both stocks near the center of the trade.
Heading into the decline, Intel has improving results. Intel's latest quarterly report posted what CEO Lip-Bu Tan called "our strongest revenue growth in more than fifteen years." That same report showed its foundry business posting a large operating loss, a reminder of how costly its manufacturing push remains.
Data center chips anchor Intel's AI business, with Xeon server processors handling cloud, enterprise and AI infrastructure workloads. Beyond servers, Intel has also demonstrated rack-scale AI systems with SambaNova and Foxconn for inference and agentic AI tasks. Its Client Computing and Physical AI Group adds processors for PCs, edge AI and robotics.
Contract manufacturing is what separates Intel from its biggest rivals. Intel designs chips and is building a contract manufacturing business that requires heavy spending, grounded by process technologies such as Intel 3 and Intel 18A. Rivals AMD and NVIDIA design chips and outsource production, which leaves the cost of building factories to their manufacturing partners.
Spending plans at Intel reflect that commitment. The company is increasing investment in equipment, clean room space and substrates, and it's expanding capacity for Xeon processors built on Intel 3. Washington backs that effort too, with Intel receiving U.S. CHIPS Act funding that supports its domestic production.
AMD built its AI franchise on an outsourced production model. Its Data Center lineup includes EPYC server processors, Instinct accelerators and Helios rack-scale systems, and that division now generates the majority of AMD revenue. Anthropic plans to deploy MI450 Series GPUs in Helios racks, while other large AI customers are scaling Instinct deployments.
Beyond the data center, Ryzen processors lifted AMD client sales, and embedded demand strengthened across several end markets. On the gaming side, AMD's business shrank over the same stretch, leaving the company more dependent on AI and server demand.
Intel's revenue recovery drives the optimistic view, with the company reporting this result: Data center and foundry sales both grew in the latest quarter, while client computing also expanded. Intel's gross margin improved sharply from a year earlier as well, giving the turnaround a stronger financial base.
Foundry spending is the main worry for Intel. Its foundry unit is still running a large operating loss.
Strong AI growth stories at Intel and AMD sit alongside real near-term pressure from the rotation. On balance, Intel brings a recovering business with heavy capital needs, while AMD offers a lighter production model tied closely to AI accelerator demand. Shareholders of these chip stocks, especially INTC, should consider keeping their position sizes modest until the foundry question at Intel settles.
