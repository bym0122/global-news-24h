The holder of debt can buy credit default swaps to protect against any losses resulting from issuer default. Like insurance, the amount paid is tied to the value and quality of the insured asset. To be sure, yields on a debt instrument are tied to the risk of default — when the risk goes up, so does the interest rate. Other factors also matter when it comes to the yield investors demand, such as inflation expectations, liquidity premiums, and the risk-free rate — usually measured by the 10-year Treasury yield , currently nearly 5.1%. A credit default swap, however, only reflects credit risk and none of those other aforementioned sentiment factors. As a result, a CDS can provide a more transparent picture of how investors actually feel about the debt issuer itself. For example, if an investor owns $1 million in debt issued by XYZ Corporation and wants to insure it, they could purchase a CDS. If the quote is 100 basis points, they would pay a premium of $10,000 (on an annual basis) to protect against default. An investor does not need to own the debt to buy a CDS — credit default swaps can also be bought and sold as ways to speculate on improvement or deterioration of a given company&#x27;s credit quality. Apply that to CoreWeave CDS over at 1,000 basis points, and the cost to insure $1 million of CoreWeave debt goes above $100,000. So, why, as stock investors, do we care about insurance rates tied to the bond market? If we think of the things in our everyday lives that require insurance, it starts to make more sense. When you look to insure a home, or car, or anything for that matter, the premium you pay is based on two key factors: (1) the value of the item to be insured, which dictates the max payout in the event of a total loss; and (2) how risky it is to insure, factoring in the odds of the policy ever being triggered and the expected payout (as any residual value can offset the amount the insurer is ultimately on the hook for). It&#x27;s that second point that we as stock investors care about. If the perceived risk of default increases, then that pushes up the quoted price for the CDS. The quoted price therefore acts as an easy-to-watch, real-time, quantifiable proxy for that risk. Obviously, the risk of default is something that should concern all stakeholders because equity value will generally be demolished long before any default actually happens. Those worries about debt risk are what&#x27;s driving the CoreWeave situation. The stock is getting obliterated even though an actual default might never happen. It&#x27;s not an official credit quality rating, like those issued by S & P Global, Moody&#x27;s, or Fitch. Going back to the housing example, say you&#x27;re considering a home on the beach but are concerned about the risk that a natural disaster can come in and damage or destroy the home. The seller wants to get as much as they can. The insurer, on the other hand, is concerned with the risks posed to the property going forward, something the seller does not care about once it&#x27;s out of their name. Bottom line So, as the AI trade plays out, be sure to watch trading in credit default swaps. Just remember that, just like insurance premiums, the movement in the CDS market can indicate whether perceived default risk is rising or falling, whether it is increasing or decreasing. Why is ASML the "real" bottleneck in the AI chip market?
ASML, which is based in the Netherlands, is the world&#x27;s largest producer of lithography systems. Whether it was youngsters proving their skills or state sabotage, Europe’s most hacked country was left with a mountain of waste after food began cooking in freezers
Shortly before midnight last Sunday, the digital thermostats controlling hundreds of supermarket cold aisles across France began to flicker and rise.
In a “bizarre and unprecedented” cyber-attack, hackers had remotely overridden the temperature settings on thousands of freezers, fridges and chiller cabinets pushing them from as low as -27C (-16.6F) to well over 50C (122F).
Over the next four to six hours, the food inside first defrosted, then slowly began to cook.
Shop staff arriving early to restock shelves reported catching a blast of hot air, like opening an oven, when they went to fill freezers. One Normandy supermarket reported a number of its freezers had been pushed to 80C (176F). In certain stores, the extreme temperature rise had sparked fires triggering sprinklers and water damage.
“It was a catastrophe,” said one worker at the large Intermarché supermarket in the south-west suburb of Issy-les-Moulineaux in Paris . “They pushed the freezers here up to 62C. Everything had to be thrown away. It was such a waste.”

If shop managers first assumed it was an isolated failure, it soon became clear up to 700 stores – including those from the Intermarché, Coopérative U, Carrefour, Leclerc and Monoprix chains along with a number of smaller independent shops – had been victims of a cyber-attack.
Dominique Schelcher, the president of Coopérative U, said 80 of its 2,000 supermarkets had been targeted. “This is a completely unprecedented, unexpected, and extremely serious attack. In my entire professional career, I’ve never seen anything like it,” the shocked executive told French radio.
The Paris public prosecutor’s office immediately opened an investigation into the latest in a series of cyber-attacks that have made France the most hacked country in Europe .
Gérôme Billois , a partner in the cybersecurity and technology company Wavestone, said the hack had the hallmarks of political sabotage.

In the day-to-day of the Iran war, it’s natural to feel a touch of whiplash. Are we on the verge of agreement or at the dawn of escalation? Is Iran on the edge of collapse, or is it expanding the conflict? Will the Strait of Hormuz return to the status quo ante, or will tolling and periodic attacks on shipping be the new normal? These are important issues but there are larger, more fundamental strategic shifts underway as well. Not since 9/11 and the U.S. invasion of Iraq has the Middle East been in this degree of strategic flux.
It’s been nearly eight months since the war started, two hundred and thirty days to be precise, and, checking the tactical as well as the strategic report card, the record is decidedly mixed.
At the start of the conflict, the Trump administration sought to lay out its objectives. As Undersecretary of War for Policy Elbridge Colby put it at an event at the Council within a week of the war’s start: “The president has directed the United States Armed Forces to conduct a military campaign with a focus on degrading and destroying the Islamic Republic of Iran’s ability to project military power in the region, and potentially beyond.” Secretary of State Marco Rubio specified the objectives in even greater detail: “Number one, the destruction of their air force. Number two, the destruction of their navy. Number three, the severe diminishing of their missile launching capability. And number four, the destruction of their factories so they can’t make more missiles and more drones to threaten us in the future.”
On the basis of these goals, the war has achieved much of what it set out to do. By any measure, Iran has fewer military capabilities and a more strained economy than it had before. Iran’s navy sits at the bottom of the ocean, as the Trump administration likes to say, and its missile capabilities have been significantly degraded.
These results, of course, have not come without cost. Tragically, the lives of twenty-one U.S. service members have been lost, as well as over a thousand Iranian civilians. Additionally, the United States has spent down important munitions needed for its other key strategic commitments and priorities. By depleting Patriot interceptors and THAAD batteries, the United States faces increased challenges to maintain its force posture elsewhere around the world including, crucially, in the Indo-Pacific.
More broadly, though, if the goal of Operation Epic Fury was to prevent Iran from projecting power beyond its borders, it might well have had the opposite effect.
The most significant outcome of the war may well prove to be the power of asymmetry. Tehran knew when the war began that it lacked the direct firepower to defeat the United States, but it discovered that, with relatively few missiles, drones, and mines, it could turn its greatest asset—control over the Strait of Hormuz—into a powerful economic weapon that allows it to project power, at least for now, across the global economy.
The resulting repercussions on energy prices, inflation, and affordability in the United States led U.S. President Donald Trump to strike an agreement last week with Russian President Vladimir Putin to allow the sale of millions of barrels of Russian diesel. That sale, protested by Europeans and Ukrainians alike, is pouring more money into the coffers of the Russian war machine and has opened a U.S. rift with Europe and Ukraine, further complicating any path to ending that destructive and intractable war.
The long arm of Iran is also being felt in the country fields of England. Here&#x27;s the catch: Stock prices do not outperform their long-term averages indefinitely. Eventually, markets experience mean reversion, a process in which unusually strong returns are followed by periods of weaker performance. While this doesn&#x27;t guarantee a crash is imminent, one particular valuation indicator is flashing a warning investors should not ignore.
The S&P 500 CAPE ratio is entering dangerous territory
The cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio (CAPE) measures the S&P 500&#x27;s price relative to its average inflation-adjusted earnings over the last 10 years. The point is to smooth out temporary earnings fluctuations, providing a more balanced perspective on whether stocks are expensive or fairly valued.
Historically, the CAPE ratio has averaged around 17.8. But as of October 2026, readings are hovering around 41. To appreciate how unusual this is, consider some history.
Before the 1929 stock market crash, the CAPE ratio approached 33. The Dow Jones Industrial Average ( ^DJI -0.34% ) subsequently lost 89% of its value between 1929 and 1932.
In more recent history, the CAPE ratio exceeded 40 in 1999 and peaked near 44 before the dot-com bubble collapse. From March 2000 through October 2002, the S&P 500 declined 49%, while the Nasdaq Composite ( ^IXIC -2.15% ) plunged 77%.
Today&#x27;s CAPE reading above 40 puts stock valuations in rare territory, suggesting the current market is historically expensive. Much like the internet revolution, generative AI promises to transform entire industries, leading investors to pay premiums for companies expected to dominate the future. But I see one important difference between today&#x27;s AI boom and the dot-com bubble.
During the late 1990s, investors poured unprecedented sums into speculative technology companies with little revenue, no profits, and untested business models. Today&#x27;s AI leaders are considerably different.
Nvidia ( NVDA -1.43% ) sells the graphics processing units (GPUs) that power much of the AI revolution. Despite its stock price appreciation over the last few years, Nvidia&#x27;s earnings have also expanded as demand for its chips continues to accelerate.

These businesses aren&#x27;t selling investors a vision of the future. They&#x27;re actually generating consistent AI-driven revenue and profits today. This is why their forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratios look reasonable relative to what the broader market&#x27;s CAPE ratio might suggest.
The crack I see is that future growth in the AI landscape hinges on the overarching assumption that big tech will continue spending at historical levels on AI infrastructure. Industrial companies stand to benefit from data center construction, electrical equipment, and automation, while energy companies can profit from rising demand for electricity and natural gas.
