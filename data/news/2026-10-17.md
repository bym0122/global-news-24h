Nvidia ( NVDA -1.43% ) and TSMC ( TSM -2.28% ) are often considered the bellwethers of the AI chip market. Nvidia , the world&#x27;s largest producer of discrete GPUs for data centers, provides the best "picks and shovels" for the AI gold rush. TSMC , the world&#x27;s largest and most advanced contract chipmaker, manufactures Nvidia&#x27;s chips. It also serves other fabless AI chipmakers, including AMD ( AMD -5.96% ) and Broadcom ( AVGO -4.53% ) .
Therefore, if Nvidia stopped developing new AI chips and TSMC stopped manufacturing them, the AI market would grind to a halt. However, neither company could produce new chips without ASML ( ASML -0.96% ) -- the real bottleneck in the AI supply chain.
Why is ASML the "real" bottleneck in the AI chip market?
ASML, which is based in the Netherlands, is the world&#x27;s largest producer of lithography systems. These systems enable chip foundries, like TSMC, to optically etch circuit patterns onto silicon wafers. It dominates the market for lower-end deep-ultraviolet (DUV) lithography systems, which are used to produce older, larger chips. It&#x27;s also the only producer of high-end extreme ultraviolet (EUV) systems, which are used to produce the world&#x27;s smallest and densest chips.
ASML began developing its first EUV systems nearly three decades ago, giving it a major first-mover advantage against its industry peers. These massive systems cost between $200 million and $400 million, require multiple planes to ship, and must be assembled on-site.

All of the world&#x27;s leading foundries -- including TSMC, Samsung, and Intel ( INTC -5.79% ) -- use ASML&#x27;s EUV systems to manufacture their newest chips. Therefore, the latest AI chips from Nvidia, AMD, and Broadcom -- as well as high-bandwidth memory (HBM) chips for AI clusters -- couldn&#x27;t exist without ASML&#x27;s EUV machines.
ASML doesn&#x27;t face any competitors in this market because it would cost too much to develop and mass-produce new EUV systems. That&#x27;s why ASML still has plenty of room to raise its prices (and expand its gross margins) without worrying about losing any customers.
In the past, ASML generated more revenue from DUV systems than from EUV systems. But over the past three years, its EUV revenue has overtaken its DUV revenue as more foundries upgraded their systems to produce smaller, denser, and more power-efficient AI chips.
In the first half of 2026, ASML generated more than half of its revenue from its EUV systems (including its older "low-NA" systems and newer "high-NA" systems). In the day-to-day of the Iran war, it’s natural to feel a touch of whiplash. Are we on the verge of agreement or at the dawn of escalation? Is Iran on the edge of collapse, or is it expanding the conflict? Will the Strait of Hormuz return to the status quo ante, or will tolling and periodic attacks on shipping be the new normal? These are important issues but there are larger, more fundamental strategic shifts underway as well. Not since 9/11 and the U.S. invasion of Iraq has the Middle East been in this degree of strategic flux.
It’s been nearly eight months since the war started, two hundred and thirty days to be precise, and, checking the tactical as well as the strategic report card, the record is decidedly mixed.
At the start of the conflict, the Trump administration sought to lay out its objectives. As Undersecretary of War for Policy Elbridge Colby put it at an event at the Council within a week of the war’s start: “The president has directed the United States Armed Forces to conduct a military campaign with a focus on degrading and destroying the Islamic Republic of Iran’s ability to project military power in the region, and potentially beyond.” Secretary of State Marco Rubio specified the objectives in even greater detail: “Number one, the destruction of their air force. Number two, the destruction of their navy. Number three, the severe diminishing of their missile launching capability. And number four, the destruction of their factories so they can’t make more missiles and more drones to threaten us in the future.”
On the basis of these goals, the war has achieved much of what it set out to do. By any measure, Iran has fewer military capabilities and a more strained economy than it had before. Iran’s navy sits at the bottom of the ocean, as the Trump administration likes to say, and its missile capabilities have been significantly degraded.
These results, of course, have not come without cost. Tragically, the lives of twenty-one U.S. service members have been lost, as well as over a thousand Iranian civilians. Additionally, the United States has spent down important munitions needed for its other key strategic commitments and priorities. By depleting Patriot interceptors and THAAD batteries, the United States faces increased challenges to maintain its force posture elsewhere around the world including, crucially, in the Indo-Pacific.
More broadly, though, if the goal of Operation Epic Fury was to prevent Iran from projecting power beyond its borders, it might well have had the opposite effect.
The most significant outcome of the war may well prove to be the power of asymmetry. Tehran knew when the war began that it lacked the direct firepower to defeat the United States, but it discovered that, with relatively few missiles, drones, and mines, it could turn its greatest asset—control over the Strait of Hormuz—into a powerful economic weapon that allows it to project power, at least for now, across the global economy.
The resulting repercussions on energy prices, inflation, and affordability in the United States led U.S. President Donald Trump to strike an agreement last week with Russian President Vladimir Putin to allow the sale of millions of barrels of Russian diesel. That sale, protested by Europeans and Ukrainians alike, is pouring more money into the coffers of the Russian war machine and has opened a U.S. rift with Europe and Ukraine, further complicating any path to ending that destructive and intractable war.

For a long time, Iran has wanted to push the United States out of the region. Over the years, though, the United States increased its presence across thirteen bases in partner countries, including Bahrain, Kuwait, and Qatar, in order to deter aggression, promote stability, and preposition assets for conflicts as they arise.
As CFR’s Steven Cook has noted, Iran has turned the logic of those bases on its head. Partner countries initially sought U.S. bases as a source of security; now they are targets because of them. They assumed that the United States would actively defend them should Iran or its proxies attack; now that is open to question. After Iranian strikes damaged these bases, the United States withdrew certain forces, even as it deployed more mobile capabilities as part of a third carrier strike group and an additional Marine Expeditionary Unit. If and when the dust of this conflict settles, will partner countries want to rebuild those bases and have U.S. forces return to them? The long and drawn-out process of winding down the costly wars the United States has fought in the region for more than three decades will almost certainly be accelerated by this one.

Without the U.S. security umbrella that so many of our regional partners thought they could depend on, they may turn to others, and each other, for that security.
