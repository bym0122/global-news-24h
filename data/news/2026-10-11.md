The high-risk, high-reward business of moving oil through the Strait of Hormuz has gotten riskier and more rewarding.
Despite steady attacks from Iran, oil tankers and other commercial vessels have continued flowing through the narrow waterway. With protection from the U.S. military and help from pipelines bypassing the strait, Persian Gulf oil flows returned to prewar levels .
But seeking to reassert its leverage over the global oil chokepoint, Iran has stepped up drone and missile strikes on ships recently, including those outside the immediate vicinity of Hormuz.
While traffic has dipped in response, ships continue to cross, with many making shuttle runs in and out of the Gulf to unload oil cargoes via ship-to-ship transfers. Then other vessels deliver the barrels to customers.
For those onboard the ships willing to tolerate the risks in the Gulf, a hefty payday awaits. Tanker captains can earn $100,000 a month for transiting the strait, plus a $50,000 bonus for each trip, sources told the Financial Times .
That “danger money” is up from regular pay of about $15,000 a month, and is necessary for shipowners to keep their fleets crewed.

Where Did the Six Words “Respect, Fairness and Reciprocity” Come From?
Phoenix Global Insight: The latest China-US summit has expanded the meaning of a “constructive relationship of strategic stability” by adding the principles of “respect, fairness and reciprocity.” When the two presidents agreed in May to build a constructive relationship of strategic stability, you described it as a shift from “tactical stability” to “strategic stability.” How do you assess this latest development? What does it mean for China-US relations going forward?
Wu Xinbo: The principles of “respect, fairness and reciprocity” come from the six Chinese characters meaning “equality, respect and mutual benefit” that were put forward during the China-US trade negotiations. China essentially conducted those negotiations in accordance with these principles. That is why the talks made progress and why the economic relationship stabilised. Now, however, the two sides need to stabilise not only their economic relationship but the entire China-US relationship. During Trump’s visit to China in May, the two countries reached an understanding on building a constructive relationship of strategic stability. The logic behind the latest formulation is the same. If equality, respect and mutual benefit are necessary to stabilise economic relations, then the same principles should apply to the broader bilateral relationship. There has been a slight adjustment in the wording. “Equality, respect and mutual benefit” has become “respect, fairness and reciprocity.” After the May meeting in Beijing, the White House actually used the terms “fairness” and “reciprocity” in its statement. These terms were consistent with the principles China had advocated during the negotiations. The English word “reciprocity” can be translated into Chinese as either “对等” or “互惠.” The nuances are slightly different, but the underlying meaning is broadly similar. So I believe the new six-character formulation draws directly on the principles China followed during the trade negotiations. After discussions between the two sides, these principles have now been extended to the overall China-US relationship in pursuit of strategic stability. We should also recognise that the United States attaches considerable importance to such concepts and formulations. During his welcoming remarks at the White House, Trump specifically mentioned that he and President Xi had established a “great friendship based on mutual respect and the fundamental interests of the two peoples.” So the American side is also talking about mutual respect and the fundamental interests of both peoples. That naturally brings in questions of fairness and reciprocity. These six words therefore reflect the latest China-US understanding on building a constructive relationship of strategic stability.
Phoenix Global Insight: You previously argued that, under the framework of constructive strategic stability, Washington has adopted a more cautious approach to Taiwan than in the past, which has helped maintain overall stability. With the addition of “respect, fairness and reciprocity,” do you think this American caution over Taiwan could develop into more binding policy commitments?
Wu Xinbo: I think the United States must move in that direction, for two reasons. The first is not really about the principles themselves, because principles alone cannot constrain the Americans. What matters more is that President Xi clearly explained China’s position on Taiwan during his visit to the United States. He made three main points. First, China’s position on safeguarding national reunification and territorial integrity is very clear. We are pursuing national reunification. Second, China hopes the United States will maintain what we regard as the correct position of opposing “Taiwan independence.” Since Trump’s visit to China, he has been relatively cautious in handling the Taiwan issue. Third, properly handling Taiwan is a solid foundation for China-US strategic cooperation and the development of bilateral relations. In other words, if the Taiwan issue is not handled properly, there can be no strategic stability between China and the United States, nor can bilateral relations and cooperation develop. That is a clear message about the consequences. The second reason is that China has cards to play. If the United States retreats from its position on Taiwan and damages China’s core interests, it will suffer substantial losses in its relationship with China. So when we talk about “respect” in the phrase “respect, fairness and reciprocity,” the first requirement is respect for China’s core interests, especially on Taiwan. Before President Xi’s visit to the United States, Ambassador Xie Feng published an article in People’s Daily discussing China’s four red lines in China-US relations. President Xi had previously raised these with Biden as well. They concern Taiwan, democracy and human rights, China’s political system and development path, and China’s right to development. I therefore believe these six words provide a clearer framework for how China and the United States should cooperate, the limits of their competition, and how their differences should be managed. They cover all these dimensions.

Phoenix Global Insight: Let us turn to specific issues concerning the Taiwan Strait. US arms sales to Taiwan have long been an important factor affecting China-US relations. Around Trump’s visit to China in May, he described a proposed arms sale to Taiwan worth approximately $14 billion as a potential bargaining chip in negotiations with China. US officials subsequently claimed that the sale would not depend on China’s actions. This once again highlights the difficulty of resolving the issue. Diplomatic protests have often failed to prevent Washington from continuing arms sales. How should China respond to prevent the United States from treating arms sales to Taiwan as bargaining chips?
Wu Xinbo: In the past, our responses have generally included diplomatic protests, sanctions against certain American companies and individuals, and military exercises around the Taiwan Strait to exert pressure. But it now appears that these measures have not been strong enough. They have not imposed costs on the United States that it genuinely feels. I think we need to increase the economic and security costs for the United States when it takes such actions. For example, if the United States sells $1 billion or $2 billion worth of weapons to Taiwan, China could cancel procurement contracts with American companies worth two or five times the value of those arms sales. That would impose a real economic cost on the United States.
Phoenix Global Insight: Beyond arms sales, the broader direction of US strategy towards Taiwan has also attracted considerable attention. In recent years, some voices in the United States have called for abandoning so-called “strategic ambiguity.” Meanwhile, Washington has continued arms sales to Taiwan, promoted joint development of unmanned systems through the National Defense Authorization Act, deepened military technology cooperation, and considered expanding Taiwan’s participation in regional military exchanges. Does this suggest that the United States is gradually abandoning strategic ambiguity? What implications could such a shift have for the Taiwan Strait?
Wu Xinbo: I don’t think we should be overly concerned about whether the United States adopts strategic clarity or strategic ambiguity. We should not allow ourselves to be led around by Washington. The key to the Taiwan issue is that China must retain the initiative and control over its own decisions. What does that mean? It means having sufficient strength and determination to handle and resolve the Taiwan issue in the way we consider most appropriate. The United States should have to adjust its policies according to China’s choices, rather than China’s Taiwan policy being determined by whether Washington chooses strategic clarity or ambiguity. Look at Biden. During his four years in office, he stated on four occasions that the United States would defend Taiwan if a conflict broke out. That sounded like strategic clarity. Trump no longer says that. He has returned to strategic ambiguity. But Biden’s strategic clarity was not necessarily all that clear, and Trump’s strategic ambiguity may not be all that ambiguous. What do I mean? Biden said the United States would defend Taiwan. But if a conflict actually broke out, would Washington really intervene? Could it win? Could it afford the costs? Trump, meanwhile, refuses to say whether the United States would intervene. But I believe he understands very well that American intervention in a cross-strait conflict would have little prospect of success and would come at an enormous cost. So I don’t think we should pay too much attention to whether Washington calls its policy clear or ambiguous. The important thing is that the Taiwan issue remains in China’s hands. We must retain the initiative.

Wu Xinbo: I think China’s irreplaceability can be found in many areas, across almost the entire supply chain, from upstream to midstream and downstream industries. At the upstream level, people immediately think of rare earths, critical minerals and active pharmaceutical ingredients. These are areas where the United States depends on Chinese supply chains. The United States is building thousands of data centres. Those facilities require all kinds of computer equipment and electrical systems, many of which have to be imported from China. I would say China’s irreplaceability goes far beyond what many of us imagine. And the United States is becoming increasingly aware of this reality, sometimes painfully so.
Phoenix Global Insight: Despite Washington’s efforts to remove China from its supply chains, bilateral trade has not moved towards complete decoupling. How do you think this situation will evolve? What changes might we see in the economic relationship?
Wu Xinbo: I think slogans such as decoupling and de-risking are much easier to proclaim than to implement. As I just explained, the United States is discovering that it depends on China across almost every part of the supply chain. So it cannot completely decouple, nor can it fully remove China from its supply chains. I therefore expect future US economic policy towards China to become increasingly pragmatic. Washington will need to redefine the boundaries of its economic relationship with China. What does that mean? There will be areas where the United States believes it must reduce dependence on China for so-called national security reasons, and where it actually has the ability to do so. There will be other areas where Washington wants to reduce dependence but cannot realistically achieve it. In those cases, it will have to compromise and find some kind of middle ground. And there will be non-sensitive sectors, unrelated to military conflict, where decoupling is unnecessary and the risks are limited. Trade and economic cooperation can continue in those areas. So I think US economic policy towards China will have to become more pragmatic and more precisely targeted. It will need to establish new boundaries for the relationship.
Phoenix Global Insight: So China-US economic relations may evolve into a state of partial decoupling?
Wu Xinbo: During the Biden administration, the US Secretary of Commerce visited China and made a remark in Shanghai. She said that only about 1% of China-US trade was problematic, while the other 99% was not. So if decoupling is necessary, perhaps only 1% truly needs to be decoupled, and only in areas where the United States can actually achieve it. The remaining 98% or 99% does not need to be decoupled. That gives us a clearer boundary and helps redefine how the two countries should manage their economic relationship. This is extremely important for the stability and predictability of China-US trade.
The Biggest Obstacle to China-US AI Cooperation Is America’s “Zero-Sum Mentality”
Phoenix Global Insight: Beyond trade, technology has become increasingly important in China-US relations. Competition over advanced semiconductors and AI continues to intensify. The repeated adjustments to US export policies for Nvidia AI chips illustrate Washington’s dilemma: it wants to restrict China’s access to advanced computing power, but it cannot completely ignore the commercial interests of American companies. As technological competition intensifies, is there still room for limited China-US cooperation on AI? Or will broader strategic rivalry make meaningful cooperation increasingly difficult?
Wu Xinbo: If we look at the situation objectively, China and the United States are already cooperating in AI, and they need to cooperate more in the future. The problem is that American policymakers largely approach the development of the Chinese and American AI industries with a zero-sum mentality. They believe that the United States must not only maintain its technological lead but also do everything possible to suppress China and slow down the development of its AI industry, both technologically and commercially. As a result, the cooperation that already exists between the two countries has not received the recognition it deserves, and the potential for further cooperation has not been fully realised. If we look at the possibilities for cooperation, AI can broadly be divided into three areas: technological development, applications and commercialisation, and governance and risk management. China and the United States are already cooperating in all three areas. Take technological development. The United States is ahead in AI technology, but China has also made important contributions. For example, more and more American AI companies are using models developed by Chinese AI companies. Many of these models are open-source and relatively inexpensive, so an increasing number of American businesses are choosing to use them. On the application side, I think we will see even more opportunities for cooperation. China has moved quickly in applying AI, particularly in robotics and advanced manufacturing. These are areas where China has strengths that the United States needs. Then there is governance. The development and application of AI will bring many risks. As two of the world’s leading countries in AI, China and the United States must discuss what risks these technologies create, how to identify them, how to manage them, and how to cooperate in addressing them. So there is actually enormous room for China-US cooperation in AI. The main problem is that American policymakers are too narrow-minded. They constantly think that maintaining America’s lead requires suppressing China’s development. As a result, they ignore both the reality of existing cooperation and the need for further cooperation. That, in my view, is the biggest obstacle.
Phoenix Global Insight: You just mentioned that China and the United States have room to cooperate in preventing AI risks. Could preventing such risks provide an entry point for China-US cooperation on AI safety?
Wu Xinbo: I think addressing AI risks is precisely where China and the United States need to cooperate. This is not a question of whether they want to cooperate. They have to cooperate. AI development is bringing many disruptive challenges, and humanity has not yet fully understood the risks involved. As more potential risks emerge, the issue will require increasing attention from the international community, especially China and the United States. AI risks also have some unique characteristics. The actors involved may not be states at all. They could be individuals, organisations or companies. That makes the risks much harder to control, because there are simply too many non-state actors. And AI is still at a very early stage of widespread application. We do not yet know how many different kinds of harm, including potentially disruptive or catastrophic consequences, could emerge in the future. There is still an enormous amount of work to be done. That is why governing AI risks will be an extremely complex and difficult task.

At the same time, US sanctions against Russia continue to affect normal economic and trade relations between China and Russia. The S&P 500 is up roughly 19% over the past six months. The Nasdaq has gained about 26% over the same period. Both indexes hit record highs this week.
Those numbers look good on paper. The mood behind them is a different story. Price gains that outrun earnings attract a certain kind of buyer. History has a record of how that ends.
Warren Buffett has a name for what he is watching. He compared the current market to a church with a casino attached, and he was not flattering the casino side.
Also read: Warren Buffett doubles down on stock market message for 2026
Buffett made the remark during a CNBC interview at Berkshire Hathaway's annual meeting. The church, in his analogy, represents long-term investors buying businesses based on what they are actually worth. The casino is the other side, where rising prices, rather than the value underneath, drive the buying.
"We've never had people in a more gambling mood than now," Buffett told CNBC.
He added that this does not mean "investing is terrible," but that "prices for an awful lot of things will look very silly." That is a warning from someone who has watched markets for more than seven decades.
The number that shows how expensive stocks really are
One figure puts the current market in context. The S&P 500 Shiller CAPE ratio has been above 40 since May 2026. The ratio measures the index's price against its average inflation-adjusted earnings over the prior 10 years. A higher number means investors are paying more per dollar of earnings.
The long-term average sits at approximately 17. The ratio hit a record 44 in late 1999, shortly before the dot-com bubble burst in March 2000. The current reading does not guarantee a crash. It does mean that stocks are priced at levels that have historically left very little room for disappointment.
Buffett has repeated this warning throughout 2026. Berkshire Hathaway has also been sitting on cash rather than putting it to work. That is its own signal.
Buffett has repeated his warning that rising prices, rather than the value underneath, have driven the buying throughout 2026.Bloomberg / Getty Images The 1990s already showed where this tends to go
The dot-com era is the closest modern comparison. Hundreds of technology companies saw their shares climb through the 1990s on enthusiasm alone. Many collapsed when the bubble burst in early 2000, and the worst performers were companies with weak balance sheets, unsustainable models, and little behind the stock price besides a rising chart.
The companies that held up were the ones with real earnings, manageable debt, and competitive advantages that were hard to copy. A rising stock price made a lot of businesses look successful. It did not keep them alive when sentiment turned.
That lesson applies now, too. The names driving today's rally may be more fundamentally sound than 1999's crop of dot-com stocks.
But the behavior Buffett is describing, buying because prices are rising rather than because the business is worth the price, is the same behavior that has always ended the same way.
You do not need to sell everything because Buffett is cautious. You do need to look harder at what you own.
Start with earnings. A company that generates consistent cash flow has something to fall back on when sentiment turns. A company that depends mostly on investor enthusiasm does not. Check whether the businesses you hold are actually profitable, not just growing revenue.
Look at debt. Companies carrying heavy debt face more pressure when credit conditions tighten or revenue slows. A clean balance sheet gives a business room to survive a downturn that a leveraged one does not have.

Check the valuation. A stock at a high multiple needs a business that can actually grow into its price. If the growth story leans heavily on what might happen, that is worth a second look. High expectations are not the problem. Paying for them before the company earns them is.
Consider your concentration. If a handful of high-flying names account for most of your gains and most of your exposure, that is a risk worth understanding before conditions change.
Buffett's concern is not that stocks are bad investments, Fortune reported . It is that a speculative mood tends to inflate prices across the board, including the prices of things that do not deserve them.
What to do when an investor this experienced raises a flag
You do not need to predict the top. Trying to time the market costs most investors more than the correction they were trying to avoid.
What you can do is review your risk. A portfolio that made sense before a six-month rally may look different now. Rebalance before a correction rather than after one. Selling at a loss to get to a sensible allocation is harder than doing it while you are still up.
Keep investing regularly. Dollar-cost averaging works because it takes the guessing out. When prices are high, you buy fewer shares. When they pull back, you buy more. You do not have to call the top.
Money you will need in the next two or three years should not be in stocks. That is true at any valuation level. It matters more right now.
Buffett's warning is not a prediction of what happens next week. It is a reminder that markets do not stay detached from fundamentals forever. The investors taking that seriously now will be better prepared than the ones who are not.
