However, one time-tested valuation tool has done a remarkable job of providing investors with apples-to-apples comparisons of broad market valuations throughout history: the S&P 500&#x27;s Shiller P/E Ratio, also known as the Cyclically Adjusted P/E Ratio ( CAPE Ratio ).

At the height of the recent crisis in the Middle East, the collapse of traffic through the Strait of Hormuz disrupted 14% of global oil and natural gas supply. Bypass pipelines, additional production and stock drawdowns covered more than half of the shortfall; lower consumption accounted for the rest. But not every country had the same room to maneuver. Those with ample reserves, alternative suppliers and the means to absorb higher costs could ride out the shock, while the rest competed for fewer, costlier cargo.
This disparity is central to “Aftershocks,” a study published on September 17 by the McKinsey Global Institute. It assesses 65 economies by their exposure to supply disruptions and their capacity to respond. The surge in freight rates pointed to the binding constraint: deliverability, not availability. A cargo of crude oil, refined products or LNG needs a suitable vessel, insurance and safe passage, and refining, storage or regasification capacity at its destination.
Greece already controls key links in this supply chain, possessing the world’s largest merchant fleet by carrying capacity, refineries and storage facilities, ports, and shipbuilding and repair yards. The EU’s Operation Aspides, whose area of operations has extended from the Red Sea to the Strait of Hormuz since 2024, is run from Larissa. Brussels’ maritime and ports strategies, adopted in March, treat such capabilities as integral to economic security, resilience and defense. What Greece still lacks is a permanent mechanism for coordination between the state and the shipping industry when supply chains break down.
Norway has relied on such a framework for decades. In 1940, its government assumed control of most of the merchant fleet outside German-controlled areas. The model was revived during the Cold War and took its present form in 2002 as Nortraship, a standing body linking government and shipowners, with an operational role in crises. When the war in Iran choked off energy flows, the security and preparedness department of the Norwegian Shipowners’ Association worked with the authorities and DNK, Norway’s war-risk insurer, to assess threats and keep companies informed. With that support, every Norwegian-linked vessel seeking to leave the Persian Gulf was out by the end of June.
A comparable Greek arrangement would provide real-time situational awareness of which Greek-owned vessels, by location, commitments and insurance cover, are available for charter in a supply emergency. It would speed their mobilization and the flow of security information, and track the status of ports, storage facilities, refineries and shipyards. Mobilization, however, depends on insurance: During the crisis, private underwriters canceled or sharply repriced war-risk cover, prompting Washington to announce a state-backed reinsurance facility. In May, an EU official stated that the bloc was considering state guarantees to help shipping companies with premiums, but no such EU initiative has yet been announced.
With Greek owners accounting for about three-fifths of the EU-controlled fleet, Athens has more at stake than any other member-state, and more standing to make the case for such an instrument in Brussels.

Egypt, Saudi Arabia Sign MoU to Expand Cooperation in Oil, Gas
Egypt and Saudi Arabia have signed a memorandum of understanding (MoU) to strengthen cooperation in oil, natural gas , and petrochemicals and expand trade in petroleum products, according to a statement by Egypt’s Ministry of Petroleum and Mineral Resources (MoPMR) on October 11.
The MoU aims to expand cooperation and integration between the two countries’ petroleum and energy sectors by leveraging their respective capabilities and expertise, including cooperation in petrochemicals, exploring investment opportunities in joint projects and expanding exports.
It covers the trade, transport, storage and distribution of crude oil, petroleum products and natural gas, as well as the development of related infrastructure and logistics services.
The MoU is also intended to improve energy market efficiency, strengthen supply chain resilience and reliability, and enhance energy security.
It provides for exchanging expertise and best practices in operating and developing fuel stations, as well as transporting and tracking petroleum products and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) cylinders.
The agreement includes exchanging technical information and expertise and organizing reciprocal visits, training programs, conferences and workshops to transfer knowledge and develop capabilities.
Karim Badawi, Egyptian Minister of Petroleum and Mineral Resources, and Abdulaziz bin Salman, Saudi Minister of Energy Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman, signed the MoU at the conclusion of the 17th ministerial meeting of the International Energy Forum (IEF) in Riyadh.
The signing ceremony was followed by a meeting between the two ministers, in presence of the Egyptian Minister of Electricity and Renewable Energy Mahmoud Esmat.
The meeting focused on expanding cooperation between Egypt and Saudi Arabia in oil, natural gas, electricity and renewable energy.
The IEF’s 17th ministerial meeting was held in Riyadh, the Saudi Arabian capital, on October 11 under the theme “Energy Security and Shared Goals in a New Era.” The meeting was inaugurated by Saudi Minister of Energy Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman and IEF Secretary-General Jassim al-Shirawi.
The agreement builds on previous energy and natural resources partnerships between Cairo and Riyadh. These include a 2022 MoU establishing broader energy cooperation in renewables and grid integration, and the ongoing $3 billion electricity interconnection project designed to link the two nations' power grids.
