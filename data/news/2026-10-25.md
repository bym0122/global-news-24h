Tehran (Special to Informed Comment; Feature) &#8211; Donald Trump has pointed to rising oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz as evidence that the energy crisis is easing. The figures offer some support for this view. In September, crude exports from the Middle East recovered to near pre-war levels on some days. Yet oil prices have remained high. On October 16, Brent crude reached $109.86 a barrel, its highest level since May. This gap between recovering exports and rising prices reveals a basic problem in Washington's approach: the volume of oil moving through the strait is only one part of the energy market.
Eastern crude exports reached an average of 14-16 million barrels per day on September. This rate suggest that exporters have found ways to move more oil despite the risks. But they do not mean that the market has returned to its previous condition.
Some of the recovery has depended on changes in shipping routes, the use of more tankers, and transfers of oil between ships outside the strait. These arrangements help maintain exports, but they also add complexity and cost. As Reuters reported on October 5 , the increase in flows has taken place alongside rising maritime risks and logistical limits.
The difference matters. A barrel of oil leaving the Persian Gulf does not reach its destination at the same cost, speed, or level of reliability as before the war. The market responds to the cost and security of delivery, not simply to the number of barrels passing through the Strait.
The cost of shipping and insurance is one of the clearest signs of this problem. On October 12, oil-producing countries in the OPEC+ group warned that shipping and insurance costs had risen to more than one-third of the value of the commodities being transported. Such costs make energy trade more expensive even when physical flows recover.
Tanker rates also remain unusually high. According to Reuters , rates for super tankers fell from their recent record levels but remained close to those highs. Insurance companies and shipping firms must consider what might happen during a voyage. A vessel may pass safely today, but the possibility of an attack tomorrow still affects insurance decisions, operating costs, and the willingness of companies to send ships into the region. These costs remain high even when more oil is moving. They also feed into the price paid by buyers, particularly when supplies are already tight and companies have few easy alternatives.
The deeper problem is uncertainty. Oil prices reflect current supply and demand, but they are also shaped by expectations about future supply and the ability of traders and consumers to manage risk.
A recent Federal Reserve analysis explains how a major supply shock changes the relationship between immediate oil prices and futures prices. When supplies become difficult to secure, buyers place greater value on having oil available now. Market liquidity can also weaken, making it harder to trade without affecting prices.
This helps explain why a rise in oil flows does not bring prices down quickly. Buyers need confidence that supplies will remain available in the coming weeks and months. If they expect another disruption such as war, they may continue to compete for secure supplies and pay more to protect themselves against future shortages.
Recent events reinforce these concerns. new attacks on ships near Hormuz and threats to Saudi energy infrastructure and more importantly news about coming war, have added to fears that supply could be disrupted again. The price increase cannot be explained by market psychology alone. Physical risks remain real. But those risks affect prices partly because they change what companies expect to happen next.
Trump's focus on oil flows treats the crisis mainly as a problem of quantity: if more oil passes through Hormuz, supply should improve and prices should fall. That logic misses the cost of restoring confidence in the energy system.

The distinction also matters for US policy. Military action may protect some shipping routes or reduce an immediate threat, but it cannot guarantee that insurers will lower premiums, or market will stop worrying about another escalation. A statement that exports " have recovered " cannot replace evidence that trade is becoming safer and more predictable. The lesson is not that oil flows do not matter. They matter greatly. The problem is assuming that increased flows are enough to solve the challenge.
Until confidence returns, higher exports will not bring lower oil prices &mdash;and, in turn, lower prices for diesel and other refined fuels. Trump can point to rising volumes of oil moving through the Strait, but the numbers alone cannot prevent prices from remaining high or even rising further. Oil prices are shaped not simply by how much oil reaches the market, but by what markets expect to happen next . And this is a challenge for which the United States has no solution, at least for now.

This is stoking inflation in a very big way. In fact, the Consumer Price Index (CPI) rose at an annualized rate of 3.7% in September, substantially higher than the Federal Reserve&#x27;s 2% target. The Fed raised the federal funds rate (overnight interest rates) by 25 basis points at its September meeting to put the brakes on the economy and cool prices down, and chairman Kevin Warsh warned there might be more hikes to come.
Rising interest rates could derail this bull market
The Fed&#x27;s last rate-hiking cycle spanned from March 2022 to August 2023, when policymakers were trying to tame the previous surge in inflation. The S&P 500 suffered a bear market decline of 20% during that period, and history could repeat.
Rising interest rates are bad for stocks for a few reasons. First, they force consumers to allocate more of their household income to debt repayments, leaving less room for discretionary spending. Second, high rates make companies think twice about borrowing money to fund their expansion plans, limiting their growth potential. These two factors hurt corporate earnings, and earnings drive stock prices over the long term.
Third, rising interest rates increase the yield on risk-free assets like cash and government Treasury bonds , giving investors an alternative to stocks. These assets are very popular during periods of uncertainty, because they give investors a guaranteed return.
The S&P 500 is trading at a Shiller cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio of 40.9 as I write this (Oct. 22), a shade below its all-time high of 44.2 from the peak of the dot-com bubble in 2000. Personally, I don&#x27;t think seasoned investors will be lining up to pay a premium for stocks in this environment, and some might even use this as a chance to cash in their gains from the last few years. As a result, the perfect storm might be brewing.

Had you invested just $500 in Berkshire stock when he took the helm and never sold, it would have been worth a whopping $24 million by the time he stepped down. The same investment in the S&P 500 would have grown to just $199,851 over the same period, so Buffett had a knack for picking winning stocks.
In his 1986 letter to Berkshire&#x27;s shareholders, Buffett said, "We simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy only when others are fearful." Simply put, he would buy stocks aggressively during downturns in the broader market, because he knew the upward trend would always prevail over the long term.
Buffett also consistently reminded investors that stocks aren&#x27;t just financial instruments that move up and down -- they represent real businesses with real fundamentals. A person wouldn&#x27;t sell their house on a whim just because its value dropped during a given year, so selling a stock for the same reason makes very little sense. As long as the underlying business is healthy, a decline in stock price should be used as an opportunity to buy more.
As a result, while stock market turbulence can be unnerving, investors might want to get ready to put some money to work.

Ukraine is in desperate need of a new satellite internet service, but a fast and reliable alternative to Elon Musk&#x27;s Starlink network simply doesn&#x27;t exist - yet.
Last week&#x27;s sudden slowdown of the service in Ukraine, which affected Kyiv&#x27;s ability to defend itself from Russian attacks, has shown how vulnerable the country can be, and sharply increased Europe&#x27;s urgency to have its own military-grade satellite network.
That vulnerability was highlighted by Musk himself on Saturday, when he accused Ukraine of violating Starlink&#x27;s terms of service by using it for "drone strikes to kill people".

Syria's Energy Comeback Could Reshape Middle East Oil and Gas
Syria is attracting international investment to revive its oil and gas production and rebuild its electricity infrastructure.
Major solar, wind, and hydropower projects could significantly expand the country's electricity generation capacity.
Disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz are increasing interest in Syria as an alternative energy transportation corridor.
Syria is undergoing countrywide reconstruction after more than a decade of conflict, which caused extensive infrastructure damage and led to millions of people being displaced. As Syrians rebuild their lives, the government has big plans for economic diversification and growth to support long-term recovery. Thanks to its favourable geographic position, this includes the development of a strong energy sector that could help eventually establish Syria as a regional hub.
Syria is now led by President Ahmed al-Sharaa, who formed a transitional government on March 29, 2025, following the end of the Assad regime. The government has introduced a wide range of policies and reforms over the past year, across all sectors, aimed at improving living standards and supporting economic growth. The national strategy focuses on institutional reorganisation, security integration, economic stabilisation, and transitional justice ahead of planned national elections in 2030.
The transitional government has taken control over Syrian energy, including the Omar oil field and Tabiyeh gas complex. This follows over a decade during which Syria’s energy sector was split, with Damascus situated far from the country’s oil-rich northeast. For years, while the government still oversaw gas output in the country’s central region, it lost control of some of the country’s largest production sites.
A 2026 agreement between the transitional government and the Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF) provides the potential to integrate SDF forces into state structures, which could further unify energy governance. Meanwhile, the lifting of United States and European Union sanctions on Syria, following the fall of the Assad regime, and the relaunch of the SWIFT transfer system have made it easier for international companies to invest in Syria.
Before the conflict, which commenced in 2011, Syria was producing an average of 380,000 bpd of crude and 900 million cubic feet of gas. However, the conflict dramatically reduced output, with oil production down 80 per cent and gas by half, according to figures from Wood Mackenzie. Nevertheless, Syria is still home to estimated reserves of 1.3 billion barrels of oil equivalent, in addition to having vast unexplored onshore areas and an entirely untapped offshore sector. Higher levels of foreign investment could support an accelerated recovery in the oil and gas sector.
In May, Syria announced that it had an offshore site for its first deep-water oil and gas exploration project to be developed by the U.S. major Chevron, Qatar’s UCC Holding, and the Syrian Petroleum Company (SPC). This supports the government’s aim to attract greater foreign investment in Syria’s energy sector. Other U.S. oil and gas firms have also expressed interest in Syrian oil and gas projects under the transitional government, opening the door to new investment opportunities.
There is significant potential for Syria’s energy industry given the current geopolitical challenges to global energy. Iran’s restrictions on energy trade through the Strait of Hormuz have spurred interest in Syria as a potential transit route for pipelines transporting Persian Gulf oil and gas to the Mediterranean. Syria’s border with Turkey offers enhanced access to major pipeline networks, which could facilitate the transport of oil and gas from the Middle East to Europe. With greater investment, Syria could serve as an “energy land bridge” for Hormuz-bypass pipelines, according to the Baker Institute .
In addition to redeveloping its oil and gas assets and potentially acting as a regional energy trade hub, the transitional government also has major plans for Syria’s renewable energy sector.
