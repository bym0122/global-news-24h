Tehran (Special to Informed Comment; Feature) &#8211; Donald Trump has pointed to rising oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz as evidence that the energy crisis is easing. The figures offer some support for this view. In September, crude exports from the Middle East recovered to near pre-war levels on some days. Yet oil prices have remained high. On October 16, Brent crude reached $109.86 a barrel, its highest level since May. This gap between recovering exports and rising prices reveals a basic problem in Washington's approach: the volume of oil moving through the strait is only one part of the energy market.
Eastern crude exports reached an average of 14-16 million barrels per day on September. This rate suggest that exporters have found ways to move more oil despite the risks. But they do not mean that the market has returned to its previous condition.
Some of the recovery has depended on changes in shipping routes, the use of more tankers, and transfers of oil between ships outside the strait. These arrangements help maintain exports, but they also add complexity and cost. As Reuters reported on October 5 , the increase in flows has taken place alongside rising maritime risks and logistical limits.
The difference matters. A barrel of oil leaving the Persian Gulf does not reach its destination at the same cost, speed, or level of reliability as before the war. The market responds to the cost and security of delivery, not simply to the number of barrels passing through the Strait.
The cost of shipping and insurance is one of the clearest signs of this problem. On October 12, oil-producing countries in the OPEC+ group warned that shipping and insurance costs had risen to more than one-third of the value of the commodities being transported. Such costs make energy trade more expensive even when physical flows recover.
Tanker rates also remain unusually high. According to Reuters , rates for super tankers fell from their recent record levels but remained close to those highs. Insurance companies and shipping firms must consider what might happen during a voyage. A vessel may pass safely today, but the possibility of an attack tomorrow still affects insurance decisions, operating costs, and the willingness of companies to send ships into the region. These costs remain high even when more oil is moving. They also feed into the price paid by buyers, particularly when supplies are already tight and companies have few easy alternatives.
The deeper problem is uncertainty. Oil prices reflect current supply and demand, but they are also shaped by expectations about future supply and the ability of traders and consumers to manage risk.
A recent Federal Reserve analysis explains how a major supply shock changes the relationship between immediate oil prices and futures prices. When supplies become difficult to secure, buyers place greater value on having oil available now. Market liquidity can also weaken, making it harder to trade without affecting prices.
This helps explain why a rise in oil flows does not bring prices down quickly. Buyers need confidence that supplies will remain available in the coming weeks and months. If they expect another disruption such as war, they may continue to compete for secure supplies and pay more to protect themselves against future shortages.
Recent events reinforce these concerns. new attacks on ships near Hormuz and threats to Saudi energy infrastructure and more importantly news about coming war, have added to fears that supply could be disrupted again. The price increase cannot be explained by market psychology alone. Physical risks remain real. But those risks affect prices partly because they change what companies expect to happen next.
Trump's focus on oil flows treats the crisis mainly as a problem of quantity: if more oil passes through Hormuz, supply should improve and prices should fall. That logic misses the cost of restoring confidence in the energy system.
File photo, Kuwaiti oil tanker. US military: PHP Thomas Tolliver. Public Domain. Via Picryl
The distinction also matters for US policy. Military action may protect some shipping routes or reduce an immediate threat, but it cannot guarantee that insurers will lower premiums, or market will stop worrying about another escalation. A statement that exports " have recovered " cannot replace evidence that trade is becoming safer and more predictable. The lesson is not that oil flows do not matter. They matter greatly. The problem is assuming that increased flows are enough to solve the challenge.
Until confidence returns, higher exports will not bring lower oil prices &mdash;and, in turn, lower prices for diesel and other refined fuels. Trump can point to rising volumes of oil moving through the Strait, but the numbers alone cannot prevent prices from remaining high or even rising further. Oil prices are shaped not simply by how much oil reaches the market, but by what markets expect to happen next . And this is a challenge for which the United States has no solution, at least for now.

Syria's Energy Comeback Could Reshape Middle East Oil and Gas
Syria is attracting international investment to revive its oil and gas production and rebuild its electricity infrastructure.
Major solar, wind, and hydropower projects could significantly expand the country's electricity generation capacity.
Disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz are increasing interest in Syria as an alternative energy transportation corridor.

As Syrians rebuild their lives, the government has big plans for economic diversification and growth to support long-term recovery. Thanks to its favourable geographic position, this includes the development of a strong energy sector that could help eventually establish Syria as a regional hub.
Syria is now led by President Ahmed al-Sharaa, who formed a transitional government on March 29, 2025, following the end of the Assad regime. The government has introduced a wide range of policies and reforms over the past year, across all sectors, aimed at improving living standards and supporting economic growth. The national strategy focuses on institutional reorganisation, security integration, economic stabilisation, and transitional justice ahead of planned national elections in 2030.

Governments generally have two main ways to finance budget deficits. The first is to create money, which can fuel inflation. The second is to borrow by issuing bonds, through which governments raise funds from investors, including companies and other governments, and pay interest according to an agreed schedule.
Money creation can generate greater inflationary pressure, while borrowing, when managed with fiscal discipline, generally has a more limited inflationary effect. However, when market expectations point to higher inflation in the future, investors demand higher yields in exchange for lending money to the government.
