An oil tanker was struck by a projectile about 2.5 miles from where he was swimming on the first day. On the second, he saw flames rising above a headland separating him from a burning liquefied petroleum gas carrier, Tara Gas, and later he passed close to the vessel while it was still on fire.
“My immediate thoughts were for the seafarers onboard,” Pugh said. “The flames were enormous. These vessels carry highly flammable gases, and an attack on one can have catastrophic consequences, not just for crew members but also on the surrounding marine environment.”
A vessel on fire in the strait of Hormuz. Photograph: Lewis Pugh Foundation/AFP/Getty Images The 56-year-old undertook his first swim in a conflict zone to urge an end to the fighting, the reopening of the strait and action to address environmental damage caused by the conflict.

He said: “War causes immense human suffering. Lives are lost, families are torn apart and homes, hospitals and schools can be destroyed in minutes. But there is another casualty that receives far too little attention: nature itself. And the damage can last long after the fighting has stopped.”
The small expedition team included a support boat and kayak that remained alongside Pugh during the swim.
Pugh said environmental damage caused by conflict often received less attention than the human cost. According to the UN, at least 175 vessels have been attacked since fighting began, and about 30 attacks have caused significant pollution.

I&#x27;m a Mortgage Lender: What the Fed&#x27;s Rate Increase Means for First-Time Homebuyers
The Fed’s rate hike doesn’t translate directly into higher mortgage rates.
The target range for the federal funds rate rose to 3.75% to 4.00% on Sept. 17, 2026, per the Federal Reserve Bank of New York . This is a quarter of a percentage point increase from what it was before. That same day, the effective federal funds rate (EFFR) increased from 3.63% to 3.88%.
It’s easy to see a rate hike like this and think it’ll cause mortgage rates to rise as well. However, this isn’t entirely true. The EFFR does have an impact on interest rates, but it’s not a one-for-one relationship.

MoneyLion asked Darren Tooley, senior loan officer at Cornerstone Financial Services , what the rate increase means for first-time buyers and whether it should factor into your decision to purchase now as opposed to waiting. Here’s what he said.
Contrary to popular belief, the Fed doesn’t control the interest rates banks and other lenders set. So, just because the effective rate has risen, that doesn’t mean mortgage rates will now rise.
“There is a big misconception that when the Federal Reserve raises or lowers rates, mortgage rates automatically increase or decrease by the same amount,” Tooley said. “Mortgage rates are based on the trading of mortgage-backed securities (MBS), which are influenced more directly by the bond market, inflation expectations and the overall outlook for the economy.”
Worth noting is that mortgage lenders don’t change their rates on a dime. That’s because home loans are a type of long-term debt. The rates you see are based on projections of future economic conditions. Just like the Federal funds rate, they can change, but not necessarily overnight.
Your credit profile as a borrower also impacts the mortgage rate you get. The better your credit (and income), the better your rate tends to be. Other factors, like having a larger down payment or paying discount points, could also lower your interest rate.
For you as a first-time homebuyer, the Fed’s rate hike could (indirectly) mean the following.
Higher monthly payments (leading to affordability concerns)
Decreased purchasing power (with higher loan costs)
Potentially more opportunities on the market for the more financially prepared buyer
“The rate hike in September sent a clear message that inflation isn’t beaten yet and since inflation is one of the biggest indicators of the economy’s health, it did impact mortgage rates,” Tooley explained. “So, while the Fed’s decisions to raise or lower rates don’t directly correlate to mortgage rates, those decisions affect the direction of the economy, which has an underlying influence on how mortgage rates respond.”
Mortgage interest rates have risen substantially since mid-September. Back then, the average rate on a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage was 6.95%. As of October 15, it’s now 7.40%, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED).
Does this mean you should hold off on buying? Not necessarily.
“I would caution first-time buyers against trying to time the housing market based solely on interest rates,” Tooley said. “There are so many factors that go into determining whether now is the right time to buy, including their overall financial stability , comfort with the monthly payment and how long they plan to stay in the home.”
Rates have fluctuated over the years. In February 2026, they fell just below 6% before once again rising. While there are no guarantees they’ll fall again, history indicates that they could. If that happens, refinancing might be an option.
“One thing I always remind buyers is that you can potentially refinance your mortgage if rates drop in the future, but you can’t go back and purchase a home at yesterday’s prices,” Tooley said. “Waiting for lower rates doesn’t necessarily mean you’ll save money , especially if home prices continue rising.”
As per FRED data, the median sales price of homes was $410,700 in Q2 2026. Despite the occasional dip, overall prices have been on the rise. If you find the home you want and you’re ready to buy, don’t change your plans simply because of the Fed’s rate hike. Consider the big picture before making any decisions like that.
