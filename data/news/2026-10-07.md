An updated report from the Congressional Research Service, or CRS, now lists 81 U.S. military aircraft as lost or damaged since the United States launched Operation Epic Fury against Iran on Feb. 28. That is nearly double the 42 aircraft the research service counted in May, as Defense News reported at the time.
The Oct. 5 revision adds jets damaged in a September Iranian strike on a base in Jordan, helicopter losses drawn from a Pentagon inspector general report and a sharply higher count of lost Reaper drones.
CRS cautions that the total is not a count of destroyed aircraft, noting, “Some damaged aircraft may have been returned to active service.”
The research service works from public information, drawing on news reports and statements from the Defense Department and U.S. Central Command. This version also draws on the inspector general report published Sept. 14. CRS said its count “may remain subject to revision due to multiple factors, which may include classification, ongoing combat activity, and attribution.”
The deadliest loss on the list remains the March 12 crash of a KC-135 Stratotanker. Two of the seven KC-135s in the report were involved in an incident over friendly airspace . One crashed in western Iraq, killing all six crew members. The other made an emergency landing at Ben Gurion Airport in Israel, Defense News reported. Central Command said the loss was not the result of hostile or friendly fire.
A Navy squadron commander was lost when one of the newly listed aircraft went down. An MH-60S Sea Hawk from the aircraft carrier George H.W. Bush made an emergency landing in the Arabian Sea on July 1. The Navy said there was no indication of hostile action.
Three of the four crew members aboard were recovered. The Navy identified the fourth as Cmdr. Gabriel Edwards, commanding officer of Helicopter Sea Combat Squadron 5, and suspended its search for him July 5 after more than 102 hours. Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao directed that Edwards be posthumously promoted to captain.
Cmdr. Gabriel Edwards vanished after the MH-60S Sea Hawk helicopter he was aboard went down. (U.S. Navy) Several of the aircraft on the list trace to the April mission to recover the two-person crew of an F-15E Strike Eagle shot down over Iran. Both crew members were rescued in separate search-and-rescue operations. That jet is one of 12 Strike Eagles on the list.
An A-10 Thunderbolt II supporting the rescue mission crashed after taking enemy fire, Joint Chiefs Chairman Air Force Gen. Dan Caine said. The pilot flew the damaged jet out of Iran and ejected over friendly territory, where he was quickly recovered.
An HH-60W Jolly Green II combat rescue helicopter in the same task force was damaged by small-arms fire, Caine said.
The second crew member was rescued nearly two days later in a separate special operations mission. Two MC-130J Commando II transports that had flown the commandos into Iran suffered a mechanical failure and could not take off, Reuters reported .
U.S. troops pulled the rescue force out in stages and destroyed the two transports and four helicopters rather than leave sensitive equipment behind. The inspector general later said U.S. forces destroyed four AH-6 Little Bird helicopters during rescue operations, likely those four. The May version listed no Little Birds, a gap Defense News noted at the time .
Iranian strikes on two air bases account for 15 of the aircraft on the list, all damaged on the ground.
An Iranian missile strike on Prince Sultan Air Base, Saudi Arabia, damaged five KC-135s on the ground in mid-March, Defense News reported , citing two U.S. officials who spoke to The Wall Street Journal. Iran hit the base again on March 27, damaging an E-3 Sentry in what the CRS report, citing a September article, described as “a direct hit.”
A damaged U.S. aircraft following an Iranian strike on the Prince Sultan Air Base in Saudi Arabia, amid the U.S.-Israeli conflict with Iran. (Social media via Reuters) The seven KC-135s on the list come from a fleet that was already stretched thin. As of March, the Air Force had 373 KC-135s and 103 KC-46As, the Government Accountability Office, or GAO, said in a June report . GAO found the fleet fell short of the Air Force’s own readiness goals every year from fiscal 2019 through 2025.
The E-3 fleet is far smaller. The Air Force operated only 16 E-3s before the war began, and six had deployed to Prince Sultan . CRS cites the E-3 as an example of aging, scarce aircraft where losses could leave the military short elsewhere in the world, and suggests Congress may want to weigh that risk.
The other base struck by Iran, Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan, came under ballistic missile attack Sept. 8-9. The second A-10 on the list lost a wing, and eight F-15Es took light damage and were returned to service, a U.S. official told Reuters .
The official said U.S. forces fired roughly 30 Patriot interceptors to defend the base. Three Army soldiers were killed there in a separate missile attack in July.
Drones account for most of the increase since May. The report now counts 45 MQ-9 Reapers lost, citing an Aug. 13 article in which three U.S. officials said the military had lost “at least 45.” That is up from as many as 30 destroyed through June 30, according to the inspector general report.
Air Force Chief of Staff Gen. Kenneth Wilsbach has called the Reaper perhaps the most valuable player of Operation Epic Fury. Washington and Beijing have developed clear—and sharply divergent—diplomatic strategies to influence how other nations will develop, deploy, and govern AI systems and establish global AI leadership in the coming decades. Washington has emphasized strategic competition and American market dominance, bundling access to advanced chips, cloud services, AI models, compute power, applications, and financing into a “full-stack” AI export package that features U.S. industry and would be available to favored nations.
Beijing has promoted a vision for widespread collaboration based in AI as a development priority, with a focus on offering AI models that can be locally owned and operated, adapted for local needs, and supported by access to training and capacity building, a new multilateral AI body, and Chinese applications. Washington’s strategic partnerships have been designed to position the United States as the undisputed winner of an AI race with China. China’s partnerships have been designed to emphasize multilateral inclusion and equitable access to the benefits of AI, particularly for middle- and low-income countries.
China has been taking steps to move its foreign policy from rhetoric to practice, even though announcements still outpace implementation. On the eve of China’s World Artificial Intelligence Conference (WAIC) in July, representatives from 29 countries signed an agreement establishing the World AI Cooperation Organization ( WAICO ) as an “independent” intergovernmental organization headquartered in Shanghai. (Additional countries are now reported to have signed on, according to Chinese media outlets.) The following day, Chinese President Xi Jinping pledged 5,000 AI training and seminar opportunities for developing countries over five years. He also announced plans for international AI application centers with leading regional organizations such as BRICS, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), the African Union, and the Arab League, as well as assistance for 30 countries to use China’s AI-powered Mazu meteorological early-warning system.
It remains unclear exactly how WAICO’s commitments would play out in practice. The July announcement described its status and aims but did not clarify a financing mechanism, offer a delivery structure for its commitments, or clarify how the organization’s independence would be protected and assured. It is clear that WAICO is designed to help China realize its stated AI strategy. For example, China’s July AI cooperation and development action plan called for accessible computing services for developing countries and support for adapting open AI models to local needs, a sentiment Xi echoed in his speech at the WAIC. (Open-weight models allow developers a model they can copy and adapt for their own purposes, without requiring them to train a model from scratch. This can make AI systems cheaper and more affordable and can be particularly useful for nations that need to build systems to operate across highly specific, local environments.)
Meanwhile, China’s existing cooperation with ASEAN may illustrate how regional partnerships would operate in practice: The ASEAN-China Artificial Intelligence Industry Innovation Center, inaugurated in Beijing on May 24, forms part of the two sides’ digital cooperation agenda. Its planned work pairs practical demonstrations of AI applications in sectors such as healthcare and agriculture with training for ASEAN officials, company employees, and students, connecting access to AI technologies with the skills needed to use them. With more than 3,000 exhibits from over 1,100 companies, the WAIC displayed the industrial breadth that could underpin Chinese AI applications.
Washington has begun implementing its own competing multilateral AI offer through Pax Silica , which seeks to secure the supply chains needed to build and power AI systems and has garnered support from approximately two dozen U.S. partners and allies. Efforts like the American AI Exports Program support U.S. consortia selling systems abroad, giving members priority access to federal financing and export-license processing. The departments of commerce and state have announced a range of new programs aimed at strengthening ties between American technology companies and foreign markets, including mechanisms to connect U.S. suppliers to foreign buyers, credential AI supply chains, and finance commerce-designated export packages.
While the U.S. approach offers components of an AI ecosystem strategy, no U.S. program creates a soft-power offering similar to China’s development offerings. The U.S. has also expressly rejected proposals for any form of independent multilateral body that would support broader AI governance or cooperation.
After the WAICO announcement, Washington indicated that it might even go a step further toward isolationism by positioning Pax Silica as an all-or-nothing proposition for its signatories. Reuters reported a draft letter from the State Department announcing that membership in Pax Silica “[could not] be held alongside membership in duplicative initiatives whose expectations conflict with our own.” While Reuters could not establish if or when such a letter would be sent, it did quote an unnamed U.S. official who said the letter was drafted to clarify that governments “​can’t have it both ways.”
China, meanwhile, has publicly defended countries’ right to choose AI partners according to their development needs, allowing Beijing to present its assistance as compatible with keeping other partnerships open. That stance aligns more closely with what other governments are seeking when it comes to AI. On Sept. 21, Finland and Norway, along with 19 other nations and the European Commission, issued a joint statement urging global collaboration to address emerging frontier AI risks. The signatories included members of Pax Silica, WAICO participants, and nonaligned states.
Nations are not only aligned in seeking more multilateral mechanisms; they are also aligned in their desire to avoid dependency on any individual nation’s technology stack. On Sept. 14, European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde warned that reliance on foreign AI raises questions about whose laws govern sensitive data and whether access to essential services could be withdrawn or used as leverage in political negotiations. Governments weighing those risks lose nothing by increasing collaboration with Beijing, and pressure from Washington to forgo alternative partnerships and deepen dependencies on the U.S. as a technology partner seems increasingly foolhardy in a geopolitical environment where partners’ and allies’ trust in the U.S. has hit all-time lows.
It is hard to see how the U.S. can “win” any AI race by forcing governments around the world to choose between the two leading powers. Governments need support that fits the systems they already use and leaves them room to adopt AI on their own terms. They will want, and need, to build systems that incorporate both American and Chinese AI technologies as they seek to harness the benefits of AI transformation. They may also look for broader support than the limited offerings that the U.S. currently provides.
WAICO gives Beijing a standing organization through which it can organize this cooperation. Its appeal to partner governments is the prospect of practical help as their needs change, with membership in an institution devoted to AI development. A government could begin with one public service application and return for support adapting it to another language or extending it to other agencies. Whether WAICO can provide that assistance, and how much influence members will exercise over its priorities, remains unclear.
In addition, governments seeking to import and scale AI systems need to know whether a system will work with their data, meet local requirements, and remain affordable to operate—requirements that China has named explicitly in its technology diplomacy strategy. AI export packages that meet those needs early in adoption will secure a critical first-mover advantage for the nation (or companies) behind them.
Training programs will shape local engineers’ familiarity with specific software or hardware tools, and procurement rules built to support early purchases will determine which technologies can be deployed, how, and by whom.
First-mover advantages won’t go to the AI leader with the most cutting-edge models. It will go to the country that can best support partner nations in connecting infrastructure, models, applications, financing, capacity building, and operational standards into a coherent offering that allows leaders to scale AI adoption while maintaining their right to mix and match systems as needed.
The world is waiting to see what, if any, agreements the U.S. and China might strike on managing AI risks. For the United States, any agreement undercuts its ability to demand that partners and allies refrain from collaboration or trade with China regarding their own AI needs. For China, a stated commitment to openness and inclusion will be challenged by a singular agreement with the United States that excludes all other nations from access to critical information sharing on AI risks and safety standards. If the U.S. and China can agree to cooperate on critical AI safety risks, world leaders will breathe a collective sigh of relief. And then, with their next breath, they will ask, “Where do we come in, and what does this mean for us?”
At the moment, Beijing is far better positioned to answer that question than Washington.

### 38. ★★★☆☆ Chemistry Nobel awarded for solving mystery of life's asymmetry

- **分类**: 🌍 地缘政治
- **来源**: BBC World
- **多源确认**: 1
- **时间**: 2026-10-07T11:51:52+00:00
- **发生了什么**: Image caption, The winners are awarded the Nobel Prize medal and a share of 12.0 million Swedish Kroner ($1.25m; £900,000)
By Victoria Gill and Esme Stallard BBC Science team
The Nobel Prize in Chemistry has been awarded to French scientist Henri B. Kagan and Japan&#x27;s Kenso Soai for providing a solution to the chemical mystery of life&#x27;s asymmetry.
The researchers discovered how nature exclusively makes the correct version of a molecule - rather than its mirror image.
For example, amino acid molecules, the building blocks of proteins, exist as two forms that are each other&#x27;s mirror image.
But living organisms on Earth only function with one of those forms. The Nobel laureates answered the question: why does life favour just one of these mirror image molecules over the other.
In organic chemistry, this property is known as "chirality", so chiral molecules have both right and left-handed forms.
You can think of them as looking like our hands - not identical, but each a mirror image of the other.
And while nature almost exclusively produces the version it requires to function, chemical reactions in a lab usually create a 50-50 mix of both left- and right-handed molecules.
When it comes to making drugs, this is particularly significant, because each version behaves differently when it interacts with our bodies.
So, one might have disease-fighting properties, while the other version does nothing or even causes harm.
The development of the Thalidomide drug is an example of this. In the 1950s it was prescribed to pregnant women suffering with morning sickness. But it is a chiral molecule - the right-handed version is an effective treatment, but the left-hand is toxic.
The original drug given was a mixture of the two, and it was later discovered that the body will convert between the two. The result was thousands of children, born to women who took the drug, developed severe disabilities.
Speaking to the Nobel Prize press conference this morning over the phone, Kenso Soai said: "This is one of the most exciting days of my life."
Image caption, The chair of the Nobel Committee for Chemistry, Heiner Linke, explains how chiral molecules have mirror forms like our hands
By solving the mystery of chirality, Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai worked out how to design chemical reactions to produce much more of the desired version of a molecule.
Heiner Linke, chair of the Nobel Committee for Chemistry, explained: "Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai have provided a solution to a chemical mystery that is over a century old: how homochirality - the selective production of the correct version of a molecule - can emerge spontaneously.
"The chemical reactions they have developed are spectacular."
Henri Kagan, who is at Université Paris-Sud, first discovered a new way of manipulating chemical reactions in 1986. This allowed him to create a greater excess of one of the mirror images than had previously been thought possible.
Kenso Soai from Tokyo University of Science took the next step in 1995. He published a scientific paper in the journal Nature , external describing the first ever chemical reaction that had the potential to be homochiral - producing only the desired version of an organic molecule.
In 2003, he finally succeeded in controlling a reaction in which only one of the two possible mirror images was formed.
"Other than life itself," the Nobel Committee said in a statement, "no one had previously achieved this feat".
At the press conference on Wednesday, Kenso Soai praised the "many, many excellent researchers" in this field.
Professor Robert Mokaya, president of the UK&#x27;s Royal Society of Chemistry said it was a "powerful example of how fundamental chemistry can underpin solutions to some of the biggest challenges facing society".
"Different isomers (or versions of molecules) can have completely different biological and physical effects, so they are crucial in finding effective medicines to treat all sorts of illnesses," he added.
Image source, Angus Davison/University of Nottingham
Image caption, Chirality is the foundation for animal features, like the way snail shells coil to the right
Prof Angus Davison, from the University of Nottingham, studies the biology of chirality and said that the Nobel-winning chemistry could help reveal how and why life favours "one-handedness".
"In ways that we don&#x27;t yet quite understand, the chirality of the basic building blocks of life ends up making bodies that are themselves also chiral," he told BBC News.
This was the foundation, he explained, of our hearts being on left hand side of our bodies and snail shells that coil to the right.
Peter Somfai, a member of the Nobel Committee for Chemistry, said the research awarded this year&#x27;s prize has become part of "our basic understanding of how chemistry functions".
He said showing how a mirror image is created is "probably the most fundamental question in life - because somehow that happened four billion years ago when life was created. And now we have, for the first time, mimicked that in the lab".

Navitas Semiconductor has completed its previously announced acquisition of Claros, expanding its power semiconductor and power delivery portfolio with integrated voltage regulator technology designed for next-generation AI data centers.
Claros develops integrated voltage regulator, or IVR, technology that addresses the final stage of power delivery to processors, or xPUs, used in AI infrastructure.
The acquisition extends Navitas' portfolio across what it describes as the complete grid-to-xPU power architecture, combining Claros' IVR capabilities with Navitas' existing gallium nitride, high-voltage silicon carbide and ultra-high-voltage silicon carbide technologies.
Claros' technology integrates power transistors, digital control, inductors and capacitors into a single IVR technology stack. The architecture is designed to support next-generation Vertical Power Delivery and Embedded Power Delivery systems.
Its Packaged Integrated Voltage Regulator, or pIVR, and Embedded Integrated Voltage Regulator, or eIVR, products are based on the same Claros technology stack and move power conversion closer to the processor.
Navitas said this approach can enable higher efficiency, faster transient response and greater power density.
Claros also developed the modular PowerArray architecture, which is designed to scale power delivery as the power requirements of next-generation AI processors increase.
The technology complements Navitas' GaN and high-voltage and ultra-high-voltage SiC products, including technologies supporting emerging 800V DC architectures for AI data centers.
Navitas is targeting power efficiency and density bottlenecks that it believes will become increasingly significant as AI data centers deploy more powerful processors and megawatt-scale server racks.
The acquisition also substantially expands the market opportunity Navitas is targeting.
Navitas expects the transaction to more than double its identified serviceable addressable market for 2030 to more than $8 billion.
The company estimates Claros will add at least $3.5 billion of opportunity from the Vertical Power Delivery and IVR markets. This is in addition to Navitas' existing approximately $3.5 billion serviceable addressable market for GaN and high-voltage and ultra-high-voltage SiC, along with approximately $1 billion associated with new JFET technology.
Navitas also expects the acquisition to deepen its engagement with hyperscalers and AI power platform providers as it expands its role across the AI infrastructure power chain.
The company plans to provide additional details about the combined technology portfolio during a webinar titled "Breaking the AI Infrastructure Power Wall: From Grid-to-xPU" on October 20, 2026.
