Hamilton&#x27;s former employer, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), confirmed her death, adding she died on 30 September.

Hamilton worked at MIT from 1959 until the mid-1970s and managed the Software Engineering Division.
It was in this division that she led a team of engineers writing and testing all onboard in-flight software for Nasa&#x27;s Apollo 11 project that guided US astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin to land safely on the Moon.

In 1969, moments before the historic Apollo 11 Moon landing, an onboard computer raised an alarm, signalling an error was occurring.
The error detected the computer was overloaded, and for a moment it looked like the complex landing might not be achieved.
But Hamilton and her team had planned for such an error by engineering "priority-driven software" to shut down any background tasks and favour the most important - the Moon landing - according to MIT.
The team&#x27;s famous software engineering was immortalised in a 1969 photograph of Hamilton standing beside the stack of printed code that towered beside her.

Hamilton, born in Indiana in 1936, first studied mathematics at the University of Michigan in 1955 before moving to Earlham College where she got a BA in mathematics in 1958.
She then moved to Boston, Massachusetts, a year later with her husband and originally took the job at MIT to support her husband during his law degree.
From the 1970s onwards, Hamilton used her expertise to found her own software businesses, including Hamilton Technologies, which is still based in Massachusetts.
"We are saddened by her death, but grateful for the opportunity to have celebrated her extraordinary achievements throughout her long life", Earlham College said in a statement on social media.
"Her pioneering spirit and remarkable legacy will continue to inspire generations of Earlhamites," the statement said.
Hamilton is survived by her daughter, a son-in-law, two grandsons and four great-grandchildren, according to MIT.

Anchorage, AK — As part of the Trump administration’s ongoing push to illegally expand oil and gas drilling into long-protected Arctic public lands in Alaska, the Department of the Interior today announced a categorical exclusion that aims to exempt oil and gas exploration projects from individual review under the National Environmental Policy Act .
Under the categorical exclusion, Interior is relying on old environmental studies from different projects in ecologically distinct areas to claim that new proposed activities would have “no significant impact on the environment.”
Categorical exclusions are typically used by federal agencies only for small projects like routine road or trail maintenance, restoration work, or minor facility upgrades — when no harm is expected to the environment. An exemption for large-scale industrial activities like exploratory drilling and seismic testing across a landscape as vast, varied, and changing as the Western Arctic is a radical departure for how categorical exclusions are typically used.
The 23-million-acre National Petroleum Reserve-Alaska in the Western Arctic is the largest tract of public land in the U.S. Its diverse habitats range from tundra and wetlands to mountain foothills, grassy uplands, riparian areas, and river deltas. Using older studies that analyzed impacts of oil exploration in a specific area of the Western Arctic to forecast impacts for future projects in ecologically different locations is extremely problematic and unsupported. It would also completely cut out the public from future permitting decisions.
“In issuing this categorical exclusion, this administration is seeking to fast track large scale oil drilling and seismic exploration without any further environmental analysis or public input,” said Earthjustice Attorney Janet Bering . “It is cutting the public out of this decision and future decisions. Nvidia and Broadcom are approaching computing from different directions
Nvidia and Broadcom are both capturing large slices of the AI spending pie, but in different ways. Nvidia&#x27;s graphics processing units (GPUs) have been the industry standard AI computing units since the AI race kicked off, but it&#x27;s not resting on its laurels. It recently started shipping its latest generation AI architecture, Vera Rubin.
This platform, built around Vera central processing units (CPUs) and Rubin GPUs, is far more advanced than the previous Blackwell platform. When tasked with inference, it reduced token cost by a factor of 10, and for training, it cuts the number of GPUs required for a given workload by 75%. Those are huge gains in power.
GPUs are great for a wide variety of computationally heavy tasks, and Nvidia clients can use its popular CUDA platform to program them for those workloads, but that flexibility comes with higher upfront costs.
Broadcom takes a different approach: It makes custom application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) that are suitable only for narrowly defined workloads. It has an impressive client list that includes the likes of hyperscalers Alphabet and Meta Platforms , as well as AI labs such as Anthropic and OpenAI.
That&#x27;s a who&#x27;s who in the AI realm , and more clients could join the list when they see what a custom AI chip can do. These computing units can outperform GPUs in both performance and cost when running a properly configured workload that suits their design. However, they would fall flat if tasked with a workload outside of their core competency.
