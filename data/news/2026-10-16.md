<summary><strong>原文 / 抓取到的正文</strong></summary>

Washington &mdash; In late December, two months before the U.S. war with Iran began, Army Chief of Staff Gen. Randy George warned that his service was critically short on missile defense munitions and that the shortage would last another five years, according to multiple U.S. officials familiar with the matter. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth ousted George in April, as the Iran war was underway.
George's risk analysis appeared in the Pentagon's Global Force Management Allocation Plan, a defense secretary-approved annual order determining where troops and weapons will be allocated across the globe, according to the U.S. officials who spoke to CBS News.
He warned that the stockpile of Terminal High Altitude Area Defense batteries and Patriot missiles would continue to be strategically low until 2030. His assessment was written for the Defense Department's plan for fiscal year 2027 and before the outbreak of the U.S.-Israel-led war with Iran, now in its seventh month.
U.S. officials who spoke to CBS News under condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to speak publicly said his assessment stemmed from the transfer of Patriots to Ukraine after Russia's invasion in 2022, the expenditure of munitions to defend Israel, and the reduced capacity of the defense industrial base.
CBS News is withholding the exact percentage figures conveyed by U.S. officials because of security concerns, and George declined to comment. U.S. officials say that while the U.S. possesses a significant stockpile of some offensive munitions, like the Joint Direct Attack Munition and Small Diameter Bombs, missile defense munitions used in Terminal High Altitude Area Defense, or THAAD, batteries, and Patriot missile systems are at low strategic-risk levels.
The THAAD system is one of the U.S. military's most advanced missile defense systems, designed to intercept and destroy incoming ballistic missiles during the final phase of their flight, either inside or just outside Earth's atmosphere. The Army has eight THAAD batteries. Each battery consists of 90 soldiers who operate six truck-mounted launchers, with each truck possessing eight launchers. A full battery controls 48 interceptors, according to a 2025 report from the nonpartisan Congressional Research Service.
The Patriot air-defense system is designed to protect military installations, troops and other critical infrastructure from incoming ballistic and cruise missiles, as well as hostile aircraft. It can engage a broader range of aerial threats at lower altitudes than a THAAD battery, but when used together, they provide even more effective overlapping layers of defense against missile attacks.
The Patriot munition at a critical low is the Patriot Advanced Capability-3, or PAC-3, a high-velocity surface-to-air interceptor designed to destroy incoming tactical ballistic missiles and cruise missiles by ramming into them. PAC-3s can also bring down enemy aircraft, according to U.S. officials who spoke to CBS News.
"Those are the two Army munitions that would be most heavily used in a conflict with China," said Mark Cancian, a retired Marine colonel and CSIS Defense and Security senior adviser. "You're not gonna use that many Javelins, for example, because the amount of ground combat is going to be relatively limited, even artillery will be limited, but air defense &mdash; missile defense &mdash; that will be huge."
An April estimate from the Center for Strategic and International Studies showed that it currently takes defense contractors four years and five months to complete delivery of new THAAD interceptor contracts. Patriot PAC-3 interceptor orders have an average delivery time of three and a half years.
CSIS' estimate said that before the Iran war, the U.S. entered 2026 with roughly 2,300 or so Patriot interceptors and expended between 1,050 and 1,430 during the conflict, potentially consuming as much as 61% of its prewar inventory. Fewer than 500 replenishment munitions were expected to be delivered in fiscal year 2026.
The stockpile for THAAD systems prior to the Iran war sat at around 355 interceptors, with the Iran war using between 190 to 290, or about 82% of its prewar inventory, according to CSIS. There were no expected deliveries for replenishment in fiscal year 2026. In the current fiscal year &mdash; 2027 &mdash; the U.S. has requested more than 800 THAADs.
Two of the nation's largest defense contractors &mdash; Lockheed Martin and RTX, through its Raytheon business &mdash; are involved in manufacturing these systems and their key components. They've recently signed agreements aimed at accelerating production.
In January, Lockheed Martin announced a plan to quadruple the production capacity of THAADs from 96 to 400 interceptors per year.
While the war with Iran has caused missile defense munitions to be depleted at a much higher rate, decades of Pentagon spending decisions, a shrinking defense industrial base and successive military operations that consume weapons faster than manufacturers can replace them have contributed to the current shortages.
One U.S. official told CBS News that even if the United States could avoid expending additional THAAD or Patriot munitions in Iran or in other conflicts, it would take until the midterm elections of the next presidential administration &mdash; 2030 &mdash; to replenish the stockpile to the already-low December 2025 levels.
Contacted by CBS News, Pentagon chief spokesman Sean Parnell said, "Claims of U.S. munitions shortages are false. When the Fake News media irresponsibly amplifies these lies, the real-world consequences is clear: our enemies are emboldened to provide &mdash; or attack &mdash; American troops."
He added that the U.S. still has "everything required to strike at the time and place of the President's choosing."
"Across multiple combatant commands," Parnell said, "we have already executed successful operations while maintaining a deep, ready arsenal to defend our people and our interests."
The U.S. military is divided into 11 unified combatant commands, each responsible for either a region of the world or specialized military mission. For operations against Iran, U.S. Central Command, led by Navy Adm. Brad Cooper, is in charge.
Each combatant command needs a certain number of troops and weapons. The Pentagon uses a framework known as Global Force Management to decide where American troops, their units and some of the military's most sought-after weapons are sent around the world.
The Global Force Management Allocation Plan, or GFMAP, is at the center of the process, laying out which forces will be provided to combatant commanders, for what missions and for how long.
The plan is typically organized around the federal fiscal year. Generally, the Pentagon enters a new fiscal year with an existing plan for many of the forces expected to deploy or remain deployed during the coming 12 to 24 months, rather than deciding from scratch each time a commander asks for troops or weapons.
But the plan is not set in stone. Wars erupt, crises develop, missions end and commanders are faced with unanticipated events to address.
For instance, the commander of U.S. European Command might want more Patriots in Poland at the same time that U.S. Central Command wants those same resources in Kuwait, while U.S. Indo-Pacific Command may also need the systems for contingencies involving China.
There's a constant shuffling of troops and assets from one region to another &mdash; in one instance in April 2025, Adm. Samuel Paparo, the commander of U.S. Indo-Pacific Command, told the Senate Armed Services Committee that the Army had transferred a Patriot battalion from his region to U.S. Central Command amid ongoing fighting with the Iranian-back Houthi rebels in Yemen.
After the collapse of the Soviet Union, American military planners abandoned the assumption that the U.S. might need to fight a prolonged war against another superpower, explained Cancian.
"After the Cold War, we planned on fighting short regional conflicts against countries like Iraq or North Korea. And for those countries we didn't need the large inventories that we had needed during the Cold War," Cancian said. "We needed much smaller inventories."
He added: "We shrank the industrial base. There was the famous 'Last Supper' which was literally a dinner that the deputy defense secretary put on for the heads of the defense industry, and he told them there's not enough money to keep you all in business, so you're going to have to consolidate or move out of the business, which they did."
The resulting mergers reduced the number of major aerospace and defense contractors from 51 to five, according to the CSIS analysis.
Also, historically, the Pentagon's procurement decisions have reduced spending on ammunition and missiles to free up money for fighter jets, tanks and warships, even while acknowledging that those weapons are essential to war fighting.
Cancian told CBS News that the consolidation of the defense industrial base shaped an industry that produces efficiently during peacetime but is not designed to surge production.
"We squeezed all the surge out because it was seen as unnecessary. So, that's where we end up when the war in Ukraine begins," said Cancian. "And after six months or so, we realized that we just don't have enough munitions and equipment for a protracted conflict."
Under the Biden administration, there was a renewed interest in building surge capabilities back into the defense industrial base but that would take time. As the war between Russia and Ukraine began in full, the U.S. transferred roughly 600 Patriot interceptor missiles to Ukraine. However, not all of those weapons came from American military stockpiles; some were manufactured through the Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative or provided by European countries.
Additionally, both Biden and Trump administrations used Patriot and THAAD missile interceptors to defend Israel against Iranian strikes. Six months before Gen. George's warning assessment, U.S. forces fired roughly 150 THAAD interceptors and almost 80 Patriot interceptors during the Twelve Day War.
Cancian noted that the five-year timeline to bring Patriot and THAAD stockpiles just to the low pre-Iran war levels is based on the assumption that the production schedule stays on track &mdash; and that additional military operations do not further deplete the inventory.

As China and India have improved their relations in recent months, many observers have attributed this improvement to the end of U.S. strategic altruism toward India. Indeed, in the past year, China has watched India lose its balance and simmer in a sense of strategic disorientation due to America's change of heart. Delegation after delegation of Indian strategists visited China, trying to mend ties in search of balance in the Chinese view. Many Chinese experts see India as returning to middle-power alignment for " economic authority and strategic freedom ," which translates into vigorous outreach to Europe, Japan, Canada, and all the middle powers. It's almost as if India is returning to its middle-power roots. The Chinese reassessment of India's power status and its strategic malleability &mdash; the extent to which its choices can be shaped &mdash; and predictability has significant implications for understanding future dynamics between the two powers. The renewed improvement in ties raises questions about its sincerity and limits. While China recalibrates its relations with India, it is also building fences and chokeholds, first and foremost through supply chain dependence. It is unclear what future policy the next U.S. administration will pursue toward either China or India. Both powers therefore keep their options open.
These assessments reflect ongoing research on China's role in South Asia. China demonstrates a strong tendency toward binary judgments on India's alignment choice, while the reality is far more nuanced, as U.S. policy should be.
In the Chinese policy discourse on India, the first and foremost question has always been India's power status: Is India a great power, or is it simply a middle power with great-power potential? While India's gross domestic product has consistently been a fraction of China's, its claim to great-power status has had major sway over China's calculus. Vishwa Guru &mdash; "India the world's guru" &mdash; is a famous slogan created under the government of Prime Minister Narendra Modi that, in the Chinese view, best represents India's strategic aspiration: India not only aspires to be a world-class great power but also sees itself as superior and therefore will teach all the other great powers. India's bold statement made Beijing question whether it underestimated fundamental aspects of Indian capability and potential that warranted India's great-power status.
More importantly, during the first Trump administration and the Biden administration, China stopped questioning India's great-power status. India played a pivotal role in America's Indo-Pacific strategy during these eight years, to the extent that Washington "hyped India's power status" &#65288;&#22823;&#32902;&#21561;&#25447;&#21360;&#24230;&#65289; and "exaggerated India's development potential."&#65288;&#22840;&#22823;&#20854;&#21457;&#23637;&#28508;&#21147;&#65289;
America's willingness to elevate India to a global great power and its seemingly endless provision of assistance to lift India up changed China's original view that only real comprehensive national power mattered. India's gross domestic product has been only about 20 percent of China's and has been unlikely to catch up any time soon to become China's peer. However, that could entirely change if American support continues. Beijing knows this too well because China had been the beneficiary of similar American support and elevation from the 1970s to the 2000s. The difference for China is that back then the United States supported China to counter the Soviet Union, and during the first Trump and Biden administrations, the United States lent similar support to India to counter China. The lesson for China is that great-power status does not actually have to be expressed in real, current terms rather than future expectations and potentials, and U.S. support could very realistically and rapidly elevate India to a new height and single-handedly make India a new great power.
Seeing India as an aspirational and rising power, China believes it followed a policy of accommodation with India throughout this period and beyond. General Secretary Xi Jinping continued outreach and friendly messaging to Modi, including in 2018 (right after the Doklam standoff in 2017); in 2024 at the Kazan summit of the expanded economic bloc originally formed by Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa; and in 2025 when hosting Modi at the Shanghai Cooperation Organization summit. China did not pursue reciprocal, retaliatory economic policies toward India after 2020. Even on the issue of visas, Chinese officials cited the 280,000 visas China issued to Indian nationals in 2025 to highlight the 2,000 visas India issued to Chinese nationals during the same period to demonstrate the vastly different treatments. For China, these are all reflections of its unwillingness to completely sour ties with India.
But now, with India losing America's favor, India's great-power status has become much less persuasive. Chinese strategists are returning to the view that perhaps, after all, India is just a middle power. It has great potential, but elements in the Indian strategic culture pose irremovable obstacles and consistently sabotage its ascension.
India's Strategic Unmalleability and Predictability
As Chinese strategists observe the fluctuation of ties between the United States and India in the past nine years, it is unsettling and reassuring at the same time. The vast scope and depth of what the United States and India could jointly achieve materially and in practice is unsettling for China, exacerbating China's anxiety about India's future alignment or alliance with the United States and how it could undermine China's roles and aspirations. However, the limitations to what the United States could persuade India to do, even with great rewards and strategic benefits promised, are reassuring for Beijing.
For example, despite the favors and support the United States is willing to provide India, India has refused to abandon its strategic autonomy. At the height of the Russo-Ukrainian War and American demand for India to cooperate, India refused to abandon economic and energy ties with Russia. In private conversations, India's foreign policy community firmly distinguished between "like-minded" and "same-minded" countries to protect its strategic autonomy, and Indian diplomats vehemently emphasized issue-based alignments rather than permanent, rigid alliances.
For China, this is cognitively incomprehensible, as it essentially equates to "India wants to have its cake and eat it, too." If the United States does not have full confidence in Indian loyalty and support, why would the United States spend its valuable resources to do India the favor and let India run with its own agenda? If the answer is a shared U.S.-Indian aspiration to curtail China's rise and expansion, India's expressed reluctance to support the United States and confront China militarily on China's western flank in a future Taiwan contingency, a position that was reiterated amply in track two dialogues, has certainly made the case of "strategic altruism" less appealing for the United States.
On the drastically different interpretations of U.S. support and Indian reactions, all of the China and India specialists I spoke with refer to the Indian concept of " D&#257;n&#257; " (almsgiving) to explain what they see as India's "ungratefulness." What the United States identified as strategic altruism, India sees as strategic egoism because, in Indian culture, the concept of almsgiving views the recipient as doing a favor to the giver by providing them an opportunity to clear negative karma and practice detachment. Following this logic, Indian acceptance of U.S. assistance is India doing the United States a favor, and the United States should be in debt to India consequently. The correctness of this Chinese argument is debatable, but Chinese specialists' unanimous claim of this cultural reading of a religious tradition as the origin of Indian strategic behavior is certainly noteworthy.
Essentially, China perceives India's strategic choice as unmalleable, which significantly, or even critically, undercuts India's potential. Despite the amount of support the United States was willing to supply, India has adhered to its strategic autonomy and refuses to alienate Russia or support a U.S. military campaign against China. Similarly, despite the amount of accommodation China sees itself as willing to provide India, including in economic cooperation, India also adheres to its longstanding position and refuses to forge closer ties. That means India's strategic choice is predictable and cannot be shaped by the enticement of rewards or the threat of punishments. That substantially reduces the strategic value of India as a U.S. partner in strategic competition with China, and consequently, China's anxiety about India's alignment with the United States. Meanwhile, because of the expected limitation to India's cooperation with the U.S. on the latter's competition with China, it also considerably decreases India's strategic appeal for China to pursue.
Once the strategic questions of India's power status and its alignment choices are answered, China's remaining choices concern tactical approaches. With diplomatic engagement and maneuvering ongoing, China is most confident in the supply chain dependence it has built up vis-&agrave;-vis India. This is primarily prevalent in trade rather than investment, as India's stringent domestic investment environment and the restrictions on Chinese investment have made investment an unlikely candidate to boost bilateral relations.
Trade tells a very different story. It is in fact profitable for China, especially considering the overwhelming trade surplus China runs vis-&agrave;-vis India. In fiscal year 2025 to 2026, the total bilateral trade volume reached $151.1 billion, but India ran a $112.1 billion trade deficit. That means 87 percent of bilateral trade consisted of Chinese exports to India. The trade imbalance is therefore a prominent sore spot for India.
What China has identified, however, is India's middle position in the global supply chain. The argument is that India primarily imports from China materials, parts, and equipment that Indian companies use to make finished products for export to developed-country markets. The deficit India runs vis-&agrave;-vis China translates into a trade surplus it runs with other trading partners, such as the United States. India's economic dependence on China, therefore, is already established despite New Delhi's efforts to reduce supply chain risks associated with China. Decreasing India's dependence on China will create significant economic pain for Indian industries and companies. The same supply shock the United States experienced in 2025 in the tariff war with China will happen to India if New Delhi attempts to shut Chinese products out. This economic dependence alleviates China's anxiety over India's strategic autonomy and hostility, as it creates more leverage for China
The other distinct aspect of China's economic relations with India is the missing element of "empowerment," which is prevalent in China's ties with the rest of the South Asian subcontinent. "Empowerment" has been a pillar of China's Belt and Road Initiative engagement in developing countries, meaning China not only trades and invests in the recipient countries, but also utilizes trade, new productive forces, infrastructure development, and industrial cooperation to empower local economies from within. Examples include agricultural technology transfers in Nepal, green technology and the digital economy in Pakistan, and campaigns such as "Smart Bangladesh." However, "empowerment" is not an element in China's economic engagement with India, which raises the question of whether China deliberately avoids economic engagement that could elevate India's technological productivity, industrial upgrading, or high-tech development. One could say India rejects it from China anyway, but the lack of Chinese intention is obvious.
Predicting the future of Chinese-Indian relations is both easy and difficult. It is easy because bilateral relations have a ceiling, considering the many stressors from Pakistan to border disputes to regional competition to complicated geopolitical factors. It is hard because they could have no floor, given the potential for deterioration and the history of war, conflicts, and skirmishes between the two. For the time being, bilateral relations are stabilizing and improving, but their sustainability is subject to at least two unpredictable factors.
The first factor is Indian domestic politics , especially the relationship between the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) and the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) and the future successor to Modi. Despite the RSS being the parent and ideological guide of the ruling BJP, Chinese observers have noted growing tensions between Modi and the RSS due to electoral friction and the centralization of power and control by Modi. The recent trip by RSS chief Mohan Bhagwat to the United States is seen as a sign of the organization's growing desire to raise its profile. No matter who the next prime minister will be, that person is unlikely to command the vast popularity and persuasiveness Modi has enjoyed. As such, India's future internal cohesiveness and foreign policy appeal remain a question for China.
The bigger uncertainty in Chinese-Indian relations goes back to the United States. Washington's current India policy has taken a dramatic turn, but future shifts in the post-Trump era are also a high-probability event. There is no guarantee that the United States will not resume its competitive strategy vis-&agrave;-vis China and once again see India as a needed partner in the process. China believes that India is also waiting for the United States to change its approach again &mdash; therefore, nothing current is set in stone. The result is that neither China nor India will be willing to lock in their future strategic posture. Keeping options open remains the optimal approach, but that also means the current rewarming of ties may have an expiration date sooner than people realize.
These assessments have considerable implications for U.S. policy. If Chinese anxiety about the American-Indian relationship has genuinely diminished, then the deterrent value Washington derived from that relationship has diminished with it, which is a cost of the current policy that nobody is counting. If the Chinese read of Indian unmalleability is correct, then a post-Trump return to competition with China, and hence a return to cooperation with India, would face the same limits that frustrated the previous approach, and American planners should develop realistic expectations.
However, a more careful reading of the record also offers a more nuanced position on China's largely binary interpretation of Indian "malleability versus unmalleability." India by and large still holds significant skepticism and concern regarding China's intentions, capabilities, and influence. Despite U.S. dissatisfaction with India's lack of reciprocity, it would be erroneous to assume India's position has not moved at all, such as on defense procurement and the issue of Taiwan. The subtlety does not lie in whether things change, but to what degree. The takeaway is that the United States and India will have to resort to much more careful calibration and negotiation over the terms of their agreement, rather than throwing possible future cooperation entirely out of the window. In March 2026, Deputy Secretary of State Christopher Landau issued a blunt warning during the Raisina Dialogue that the United States would not repeat its "China mistake" with India. The delicacy, however, lies in the cost-benefit analysis, as most policy options are about the measurement of relative losses and gains, rather than an absolute, binary, black-or-white answer.

Shanghai’s property revival raises hopes that China’s housing crisis is easing, but falling prices elsewhere point to a long and uneven recovery
When sales began at the new Greentown Shanghai Yuehaitang development in early August, the response exceeded expectations. Despite average prices CNY2,000 (USD300) per square metre above those of local competitors, all 228 units sold within days, with the project more than four times oversubscribed. Properties fetched an average of CNY62,198 per square metre, a local record.
The project’s distinctive above-ground, damp-resistant design and accompanying marketing campaign certainly helped, as did its prime location just 200 metres from a newly opened station on an extension of Metro Line 2. From Greentown Shanghai Yuehaitang, downtown Shanghai is a direct 35-minute subway ride away.
That a project so far from the centre of China’s most populous city sold out so quickly, and attracted such strong buyer interest, is one of several recent signs that a slow, uneven revival in the residential market may be underway. Shanghai is one of the few major Chinese cities showing signs of sustained price growth and buyer confidence as the country struggles to emerge from its worst housing crisis in a generation. Sponsored
But is the market finally bottoming out and beginning a sustained recovery, or is this just another false dawn?
Prices of new homes in Shanghai rose 3% year-on-year in July, more than in any other Chinese city, while prices for larger units of over 144 square metres climbed 5.5%, according to the latest data from China’s National Bureau of Statistics (NBS). Shanghai was also one of only five major cities to record month-on-month price growth for second-hand homes in July, with prices rising 0.3%.
Official data also showed that 147,000 second-hand homes changed hands in Shanghai during the first half of 2026, up 13% year-on-year.
“The price plus volume recovery together is different from previous false dawns,” says James Woo, head of valuations for China at Colliers. “The bottoming out is real but city-specific, not national.”
Beyond Shanghai, it remains difficult to argue that markets in most major cities have reached the bottom. Shenzhen has been the next-best performer, Woo says, with new-home prices climbing 7% in the first half of this year, second-hand home prices up 6.4% over successive months, and land premiums exceeding 150% in core districts.
Guangzhou is also “quietly stabilising”, posting the strongest month-on-month gains in the country’s second-hand market, at 0.4%.
Among Tier 2 cities, Hangzhou remains by far the strongest market, with new-home prices rising 2.6% year-on-year in July, the best performance after Shanghai, according to NBS data. Of the other cities surveyed, only Shenyang and Hefei, both up 0.3%, recorded annual price growth.
Nationally, therefore, just four of the 70 largest cities recorded rising new-home prices in the year to the end of July; the other 66 reported declines.
Yet after six years of market pain, signs that the market may finally be turning the corner have been greeted with both hope and caution.
In late May, a Reuters housing market poll found that respondents expected Chinese home prices to rise by an average of 0.3% in 2027, marking the poll’s first positive forecast for the following year.
Other analysts have since issued cautiously positive forecasts. “We expect home prices to drop at a more moderate pace in 2026 and bottom out by 2027-2028,” says UOB macroeconomic analyst Ho Woei Chen.
The Chinese government and state media have continued to strike a more positive tone, promoting central government measures and apparently seeking to restore confidence in the battered market.
In late April, the Politburo used stronger language than before in a policy statement, speaking of “striving to stabilise the real estate market”. It reiterated similar wording after its latest meeting in late July.
Earlier in July, Huang Yu, executive deputy president of the China Index Academy, spoke of “continued bottoming out and intensified differentiation”, acknowledging the increasingly varied pace of recovery expected across China for the rest of this year and beyond.
NBS Deputy Director Mao Shengyong told journalists in mid-July: “Data from the first half of the year shows that policies are gradually taking effect, and the real estate market has seen positive changes.”
Mao cited four consecutive months of price rises in Tier 1 cities before July and a “narrowing of the overall decline in residential property prices” in Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities.
Throughout much of 2026, China’s news media have promoted the idea that the market is bottoming out. State television network CCTV repeatedly referred to a xiaoyangchun, or “mini-boom”, following Chinese New Year in February.
Whether China’s residential market is turning the corner remains to be seen, particularly in smaller cities, Woo says.
“For 2026 as a whole, I’d expect Tier-1 city new home prices to approach year-on-year flattish [price trends] by year-end, with Shanghai clearly positive,” he says.
“The national picture will remain negative but with declining magnitude. The real question for 2027 is whether Tier-1 momentum can sustain without fresh policy stimulus, and whether Tier-2 cities like Hangzhou, Chengdu, and Suzhou can follow the Tier-1 lead.”
