Washington &mdash; In late December, two months before the U.S. war with Iran began, Army Chief of Staff Gen. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth ousted George in April, as the Iran war was underway.
George's risk analysis appeared in the Pentagon's Global Force Management Allocation Plan, a defense secretary-approved annual order determining where troops and weapons will be allocated across the globe, according to the U.S. officials who spoke to CBS News.
He warned that the stockpile of Terminal High Altitude Area Defense batteries and Patriot missiles would continue to be strategically low until 2030. His assessment was written for the Defense Department's plan for fiscal year 2027 and before the outbreak of the U.S.-Israel-led war with Iran, now in its seventh month.

The THAAD system is one of the U.S. military's most advanced missile defense systems, designed to intercept and destroy incoming ballistic missiles during the final phase of their flight, either inside or just outside Earth's atmosphere. The Army has eight THAAD batteries. Each battery consists of 90 soldiers who operate six truck-mounted launchers, with each truck possessing eight launchers. A full battery controls 48 interceptors, according to a 2025 report from the nonpartisan Congressional Research Service.
The Patriot air-defense system is designed to protect military installations, troops and other critical infrastructure from incoming ballistic and cruise missiles, as well as hostile aircraft. It can engage a broader range of aerial threats at lower altitudes than a THAAD battery, but when used together, they provide even more effective overlapping layers of defense against missile attacks.
The Patriot munition at a critical low is the Patriot Advanced Capability-3, or PAC-3, a high-velocity surface-to-air interceptor designed to destroy incoming tactical ballistic missiles and cruise missiles by ramming into them. PAC-3s can also bring down enemy aircraft, according to U.S. officials who spoke to CBS News.
"Those are the two Army munitions that would be most heavily used in a conflict with China," said Mark Cancian, a retired Marine colonel and CSIS Defense and Security senior adviser. He added that the U.S. still has "everything required to strike at the time and place of the President's choosing."
"Across multiple combatant commands," Parnell said, "we have already executed successful operations while maintaining a deep, ready arsenal to defend our people and our interests."
The U.S. military is divided into 11 unified combatant commands, each responsible for either a region of the world or specialized military mission. For operations against Iran, U.S. Central Command, led by Navy Adm. Brad Cooper, is in charge.
Each combatant command needs a certain number of troops and weapons. The Pentagon uses a framework known as Global Force Management to decide where American troops, their units and some of the military's most sought-after weapons are sent around the world.
The Global Force Management Allocation Plan, or GFMAP, is at the center of the process, laying out which forces will be provided to combatant commanders, for what missions and for how long.
The plan is typically organized around the federal fiscal year. Generally, the Pentagon enters a new fiscal year with an existing plan for many of the forces expected to deploy or remain deployed during the coming 12 to 24 months, rather than deciding from scratch each time a commander asks for troops or weapons.
But the plan is not set in stone. Wars erupt, crises develop, missions end and commanders are faced with unanticipated events to address.
For instance, the commander of U.S. European Command might want more Patriots in Poland at the same time that U.S. Central Command wants those same resources in Kuwait, while U.S. Indo-Pacific Command may also need the systems for contingencies involving China.
There's a constant shuffling of troops and assets from one region to another &mdash; in one instance in April 2025, Adm. Samuel Paparo, the commander of U.S. Indo-Pacific Command, told the Senate Armed Services Committee that the Army had transferred a Patriot battalion from his region to U.S. Central Command amid ongoing fighting with the Iranian-back Houthi rebels in Yemen.
After the collapse of the Soviet Union, American military planners abandoned the assumption that the U.S. might need to fight a prolonged war against another superpower, explained Cancian.
"After the Cold War, we planned on fighting short regional conflicts against countries like Iraq or North Korea. And for those countries we didn't need the large inventories that we had needed during the Cold War," Cancian said. "We needed much smaller inventories."
He added: "We shrank the industrial base. There was the famous 'Last Supper' which was literally a dinner that the deputy defense secretary put on for the heads of the defense industry, and he told them there's not enough money to keep you all in business, so you're going to have to consolidate or move out of the business, which they did."
The resulting mergers reduced the number of major aerospace and defense contractors from 51 to five, according to the CSIS analysis.
Also, historically, the Pentagon's procurement decisions have reduced spending on ammunition and missiles to free up money for fighter jets, tanks and warships, even while acknowledging that those weapons are essential to war fighting.
Cancian told CBS News that the consolidation of the defense industrial base shaped an industry that produces efficiently during peacetime but is not designed to surge production.
"We squeezed all the surge out because it was seen as unnecessary. So, that's where we end up when the war in Ukraine begins," said Cancian. "And after six months or so, we realized that we just don't have enough munitions and equipment for a protracted conflict."
Under the Biden administration, there was a renewed interest in building surge capabilities back into the defense industrial base but that would take time. As the war between Russia and Ukraine began in full, the U.S. transferred roughly 600 Patriot interceptor missiles to Ukraine. However, not all of those weapons came from American military stockpiles; some were manufactured through the Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative or provided by European countries.
Additionally, both Biden and Trump administrations used Patriot and THAAD missile interceptors to defend Israel against Iranian strikes. Six months before Gen. George's warning assessment, U.S. forces fired roughly 150 THAAD interceptors and almost 80 Patriot interceptors during the Twelve Day War.
Cancian noted that the five-year timeline to bring Patriot and THAAD stockpiles just to the low pre-Iran war levels is based on the assumption that the production schedule stays on track &mdash; and that additional military operations do not further deplete the inventory.

Over the past 14 months, liquefied natural gas companies have proposed new expansion projects at seven gas export terminals along the Gulf Coast, encouraged in part by wars in the Middle East and Ukraine that have boosted the market for U.S. LNG shipments.
Three of the expanding terminals are in Louisiana, including southeast of New Orleans at the Plaquemines LNG terminal; and in Cameron, in southwest Louisiana, at the CP2 and Commonwealth LNG terminals, according to public records available in the Oil & Gas Watch database. Four expanding terminals are in Texas, near Port Arthur, Brownsville and Corpus Christi.
If built, these new expansions would increase by about 14%, or 75 million metric tons per year, the capacity of U.S. LNG export terminals that are operating, under construction, or previously proposed.  The expansions and associated pipelines could also damage hundreds of acres of fragile coastal wetlands and release millions of tons of greenhouse gases, according to permit applications.
“These gas export facilities are a radical transformation of the Louisiana coast,” said Anne Rolfes, director of the Louisiana Bucket Brigade. “This coastal area, including our wetlands, protects south Louisiana from storms, a crucial buffer as hurricanes get more fierce. Now that protection is paved over, transformed into concrete so that an out-of-state corporation can make money.”
Developers of the LNG projects argue that the wars in Iran and Ukraine are contributing to increasing market demand overseas for U.S. LNG exports, and the new expansions of Gulf Coast export terminals would help meet that rising need.
“Recent disruptions to LNG exports through the Strait of Hormuz are increasing demand for LNG cargoes from outside the strait,” Venture Global, which is building the CP2 LNG expansion project near Cameron,  said in its May 26 application to the Federal Energy Regulatory CommissionCommittee.
Overall, nine LNG export terminals have been built across the U.S. over the past 10 years. The industry’s rise was driven by the proliferation of new drilling techniques, including hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling, which dramatically expanded production of natural gas from shale rock formations.
The expansions at seven LNG terminals, proposed since August 2025, would not be completed for several years. The largest expansions are proposed at the Port Arthur LNG Terminal in Texas, the CP2 LNG Terminal in Cameron and the Plaquemines LNG Terminal in Port Sulphur.
This new wave of expansions are on top of projects to build 24 new or expanded LNG terminals that were previously proposed, before August 2025, along the Gulf Coast, Alaska, and in other states, according to public records in the Oil & Gas Watch database .
The boom started in Louisiana, which has been an epicenter of the LNG industry. The first export of LNG from the U.S. came in 2016 from Cheniere Energy’s Sabine Pass Liquefaction Terminal located in Cameron Parish.
Since then, U.S. LNG exports have grown into a $44 billion annual industry that, within the next five years, has the potential to become the second largest net export from the U.S., behind only civilian aircrafts and parts, according to an estimate by S&P Global Energy .
As wars overseas continue to destabilize global energy markets, Wall Street investors are reportedly eager to fund U.S. LNG export opportunities that are perceived as more reliable and stable than LNG coming out of Russia or the Middle East.
One of the expansions is of the Venture Global CP2 LNG Terminal under construction in Cameron Parish. The terminal, as originally proposed, would have had a processing capacity of 28 million metric tons of liquified natural gas per year.
But then in June 2026, Venture Global received permission to increase this terminal’s capacity by 7 million metric tons per year. On top of that, Venture Global also submitted an application in May seeking approval to increase the terminal’s export capacity by an additional 11.7 million metric tons per year.
The company’s Chief Executive Officer, Mike Sabel, said that Venture Global is positioning itself to become the largest exporter of LNG in the U.S. “We will build on the strong construction progress already underway and deliver reliable American LNG to customers around the world,” Sabel said in a press release.
But Breon Robinson, the Southwest Louisiana organizer for Healthy Gulf, a nonprofit organization, said the scale and the pace of the construction projects are threatening radical changes along the Gulf Coast.
“What’s especially concerning is the trajectory we’re seeing,” Robinson said. Regulators should “stop looking at these projects as isolated developments and start asking what the cumulative impact of continually expanding LNG infrastructure means for our communities, our environment, and Louisiana’s long-term future.”
The potential emissions of the expansions are significant. The CP2 expansion project alone could release more than 4.8 million tons of greenhouse gases per year, according to permit applications and public records. That would be the equivalent of the carbon dioxide from roughly one million cars and trucks driven for a year.
“Southwest Louisiana is already living with the health impacts, the traffic and the loss of fishing grounds from the terminals we have, and families are being asked to absorb even more,” said James Hiatt, executive director of For a Better Bayou. “Regulators keep approving these expansions one piece at a time while the people of the Gulf Coast live with the whole thing.
Venture Global has also proposed a similar expansion its Plaquemines LNG Terminal, on the west bank of the Mississippi River about 25 miles southeast of New Orleans.
As planned originally, the facility would have been capable of producing 24 million tons of liquefied natural gas per year. But Venture Global has since applied for various expansions that would almost triple this capacity to 66 million metric tons of LNG per year.
These expansions are not limited to Louisiana. Similar proposals are also being announced for LNG terminals in Texas. One example is the Port Arthur LNG Export Terminal in East Texas, which is already under construction and could process up to 27 million metric tons of LNG per year. But in September of 2026, Sempra announced plans to build an expansion of the terminal that would double the facility’s processing capacity, bringing its annual total of 54 million metric tons of LNG, according to permit documents .
Impacts from these projects extend beyond the projects themselves to associated infrastructure, including pipelines.
The Cloud Connector , a proposed 165-mile pipeline that would transport natural gas to the Plaquemines LNG Terminal, is expected to damage up to 1,428 acres of wetlands, according to public records. Despite these impacts, the public was only given 11 days to notice to submit public comments — instead of the normal 30-day public comment period — because of expedited “energy emergency” procedures the Trump administration enacted.
Wetlands are a shrinking resource in Louisiana. The Bayou State has lost close to 2,000 square miles of land since the 1930s, according to the Louisiana Coastal Protection and Restoration Authority.
