At least 490,334 Russian soldiers have died since the start of the war in Ukraine in 2022, according to Russian government statistics analysed by the BBC.
The leaked Rosstat data reveal how the full-scale invasion of Ukraine has fundamentally changed mortality patterns in Russia, and show the high cost paid in human life in a war that Vladimir Putin had assumed would last only days but is now almost five years old.
BBC Russian has had access to more than 11 million death records from 2021 to September 2026, after a hack by Belarusian group Cyber Partisans BY and Ukraine&#x27;s Silent Crow.
Russia&#x27;s statistics agency admitted to a cyber-attack, but denied any protected information had been leaked.
Together with independent Russian outlet Mediazona, BBC Russian has compared the Rosstat data with its own list of confirmed military deaths, pulled from open sources since the start of the invasion, and it has found matches for 90% of the records.
Military experts estimate that the BBC&#x27;s list of 290,012 deaths, gleaned from official reports, social media, new memorials and graves, may account for 45-55% of the true death toll, which would range from 527,295 to 644,471.
The records show that 65% of 18 to 24 year-olds in Russia who lost their lives in 2025 died in the war. For those aged 25-34 the number was 59%. Only among 45 to 54 year-olds does cardiovascular disease claim more Russian lives than the conflict.
The data also reveal the extent of losses in specific areas of Ukraine. Some 20,000 Russian soldiers died fighting for control of 10 Ukrainian villages whose combined pre-war population was 12,000.
During the fight for one small village called Andriivka, south of the town of Bakhmut in Donetsk region, 1,122 Russians lost their lives. Before the war 74 people lived there.
Russia does not give public figures for losses sustained since the war began in February 2022. And two years ago Rosstat stopped publishing data of deaths from external causes, which was used to gauge the extent of military losses.
For example, only 70% of deaths we have established of soldiers from the occupied regions of Donetsk and Luhansk in eastern Ukraine who fought in the Russian army were accounted for in the Rosstat files.
Overall, the Rosstat dataset confirms the trends that we have observed with our own count.
Ukrainian losses were last put at about 50,000 by President Volodymyr Zelensky in July 2026, but another list based on open sources by the anonymous UA Losses website puts the figure at 115,952.
The Rosstat dataset contains no names but does include ages, places of residence, places of death and also circumstances of death.
We have filtered the records first by location, leaving only Ukraine as well as Russian border regions that saw incursions by the Ukrainian army.
Next, we identified 425,174 entries explicitly indicating a military-related cause of death, such as explosions or shrapnel during combat operations.
As this count could include civilians, we filtered out women as well as men outside the 18-65 age group. And if someone lived and died in the four Ukrainian regions occupied by Russia, we excluded them as a possible civilian â unless he had a record of military service attached to his entry.
Another 14,093 entries were identified by looking at employment, and 51,067 were recorded in the database as deceased on the basis of a court ruling. This often happens when a soldier goes missing in a combat zone for more than six months and their body has not been recovered.
Again, we filtered out civilians by only counting deaths in the war zones in Ukraine and Russia.
According to BBC Russian&#x27;s analysis of the leaked files, in the first year of the war the average age of those killed was 34. By 2025, as prisoners and contract soldiers entered the ranks, it had risen to 40 (38 according to our own list).
Some of Russia&#x27;s poorest regions in Eastern Siberia account for a disproportionately large share of the losses. Men from Tyva, Buryatia and Zabaykalsky Krai are 20-25 times more likely to die in the war than residents of the biggest cities of Moscow and St Petersburg.
In most regions, the distribution of deaths based on Rosstat data differs from that in the BBC&#x27;s list by just a few tenths of a percentage point.
At least 5,017 foreigners are among those who died fighting for Russia, according to our analysis of the Rosstat files, including 1,031 Uzbeks and 720 Tajiks. We have left out Ukrainians from this count to avoid including civilians.
This is a conservative estimate as dual nationals are listed as Russians in the database, and there is no reference to North Koreans who died repelling the Ukrainian incursion into Russia in 2024.
The BBC identified 2,304 North Korean soldiers who died there by analysing satellite imagery and official photographs of a newly built memorial in Pyongyang.
The leaked Russian statistics may not be complete but taken in conjunction with BBC Russian&#x27;s own work they are significant.
In the context of a war that has lasted four years and 10 months, these are high numbers for Russia, when compared with the Soviet war in Afghanistan that ended in 1989.
In that 10-year war, an estimated 15,000 Red Army troops died, and an estimated one million civilians were killed.

Iran has intensified attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz in recent weeks, just as crude oil flows from the Persian Gulf recover and oil prices fall amid expectations of oversupply within months.
The number of attacks soared in October to the highest level since the Iran war began at the end of February, and has continued at nearly daily occurrences so far in November, according to data compiled by Bloomberg .

Goldman Sachs Sees U.S. Natural Gas Demand Surging on Data Center Boom
Data center proliferation will drive demand for natural gas in the United States over the next two years, Goldman Sachs has forecast, estimating that some 52 GW of new data center capacity could be added to the grid in 2028 alone.

"We expect growth acceleration in data centers to be driven not only by a higher volume of facilities, but by increasingly larger physical footprints and higher power density," they said.
This will drive stronger growth in demand for electricity and, by extension, for natural gas, which has become the preferred method for data center operators to source reliable, baseload electricity since nuclear takes too long to build.
That data centers are massive energy demand sources is no news. Indeed, data centers are the primary reason for upbeat demand growth projections in the natural gas industry. Data center operators like natural gas. And natural gas producers like data centers.
No wonder, then, that U.S. natural gas production is on track to close a second consecutive year with record-high natural gas output as producers respond to demand signs both at home and overseas.
Soaring demand for powering data centers combined with rising liquefied natural gas exports amid expanding liquefaction capacity, as well as surging global demand for spot LNG cargoes in the absence of Qatar's gas due to the Iran war, is pushing American natural gas production higher this year.

This agglomeration of events, issues, passions, and outrages is an analytical nightmare from hell. Each invocation of Trump corruption or Chinese model release unavoidably triggers sympathetic neurons on the other end of society’s addled collective brain. Specific empirical questions such as whether Alice Huang was working directly for China’s Ministry of State Security will be nearly impossible to discuss without some people involuntarily but stubbornly thinking of Trump’s evident sympathy for a powerful man facing consequences over bad behavior with women, or about data center politics in a rural county.
In dubbing this a “hyper-spectacle,” I am mangling an idea advanced by the French theorist Guy Debord in The Society of the Spectacle , first published in 1967. “The whole life of those societies in which modern conditions of production prevail presents itself as an immense accumulation of spectacles . All that once was directly lived has become mere representation,” Debord begins (emphasis original). “Images detached from every aspect of life merge into a common stream, and the former unity of life is lost forever.” He calls it “a world view transformed into an objective force.” (If you’ve read the book, forgive me and don’t worry: We will not be reviewing 1960s discourse on socialism or the nature of time.)
Issues get conflated all the time. Yet the way in which the diverse questions underlying this heap of themes has been anointed as one by voices ranging from social media gadflies to button-down national security commentators reminds me of Debord’s insight: The image serves to cut us off from the real. You can see an involuntary glee in the way some people intone about the gravity of a situation that, just this once after years of grinding corruption and AI speculation, also comes with a titillating sprinkle of a potential spy-sex scandal.
The hyper-spectacle alienates each of these serious matters of political life and techno-social systems from real events and uncertainties. It stops even careful thinkers from pausing to ask whether billions of dollars of smuggled semiconductors are, as some chip control advocates believe, a dire national security threat to the US—or, rather, not-so-simply a series of very large violations of export controls, possibly with parts of the Trump administration looking the other way. Both options are a big deal, but when everything is tied together, it’s hard to keep track of the stakes.
The average news article or commentary cannot be expected to receive the attention necessary to sort out this mess. But I hope some of us—not least the members of Congress who are now unavoidably on their way into the hyper-spectacle’s vortex—will be able to disaggregate this jumble and navigate the important and real consequences. Here are just a few of the challenges:

Maybe! It is also possible that something radical will happen, at another time, with another form of material input. Or that the present trajectory of AI development may plateau, bringing more modest transformation (and, therefore, more modest implications for national power and geopolitics and markets).
